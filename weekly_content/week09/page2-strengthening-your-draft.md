# Strengthening your draft

Professional teams produce deliverables in parts, then assemble them. The assembly stage is where a document either becomes a coherent whole or reveals itself as a collection of sections. This module covers three things that happen at this stage: integrating work written by different people, testing whether your recommendations are actionable, and managing scope decisions that tend to surface under the pressure of a deadline.

---

## Making sections talk to each other

The video above walks through what multi-author integration looks like in practice. When your team does the integration pass, look for these specific problems:

- **Inconsistent terminology.** The same concept referred to by different names across sections. Pick one and use it throughout.
- **Undefined assumptions.** One section depends on context that was only established in a previous section the reader may have skimmed. Add the context or add a cross-reference.
- **Logic breaks between sections.** The argument in section A points toward conclusion B, but section B starts without connecting to what came before. Bridge it explicitly.
- **Register shifts.** One section reads as formal academic prose, the next as conversational. Match the register across the document.
- **Transition gaps.** Sections that start cold, without any signal that the document has shifted focus. A short orienting sentence at the opening of each major section goes a long way.

## The actionability test

A strong deliverable does more than report what you found. It gives the employer something to act on. This is the standard that matters most for the Actionability dimension of the Final Deliverables rubric, which assesses whether your recommendations are specific enough to implement, realistic given the employer's constraints, and prioritized so the most important work is visible.

The gap between "here's what we found" and "here's what you should do about it" is where many teams lose points. The findings may be solid; the recommendations that follow from them may be too vague to act on.

A useful test for each recommendation: could your employer pick this up on Monday and know what to do? "Improve community engagement" fails the test. "Develop a monthly update email for the 200 program participants on your current mailing list, starting with a single pilot in Q3" passes it. The second version is specific, time-bounded, and scoped to what the employer can actually do.

**Using AI to test actionability**

This is a new application of AI as a production partner, building on the structural review skills from the previous module. Once you have a draft of your recommendations section, try this prompt: "Read these recommendations and tell me which ones are too vague for a client to act on. For each one, identify what's missing — is it specificity, feasibility, or prioritization?"

Review what AI flags. It will often surface real gaps. It will also sometimes flag recommendations as vague when they're deliberately high-level because the implementation is the employer's call. Use your judgment: the test isn't whether AI approves of the recommendation, it's whether your employer can act on it.

## Scope decisions: what to keep, what to defer

Around this point in a project, teams often discover they've identified more interesting territory than they can cover in the time remaining. That pressure tends to produce one of two responses: teams either rush to include everything (producing thin coverage of a lot of things) or they quietly drop threads without explicitly deciding to (and then forget what they chose not to pursue).

The professional approach is to make scope decisions explicitly. There are three options:

- **Include it.** It belongs in the current deliverable because it's central to what the employer asked for.
- **Defer it.** It's valuable, but it's outside the scope of this engagement. Include it in a "future recommendations" section at the end of the deliverable so the employer knows the team saw it and made a choice.
- **Decline it.** It's a direction that came up but doesn't fit the project's purpose. Name it clearly in a team conversation so everyone knows the decision was made.

When scope pressure comes from the employer (a comment in Check-In #1, an email, a suggestion to add something), the same framework applies. You can acknowledge the suggestion, explain that you've thought about it, and explain which category it falls into. "That's a direction we looked at and decided to flag in future recommendations rather than include in the current scope" is a professional response, not a refusal.

## The internal review gate

Before your team submits the draft and before Check-In #2, run one internal review: each team member reads the full draft end-to-end, not just their own sections. This is a professional quality habit, not a formality.

The goal is to find the problems the employer would find, before the employer finds them.

What to look for:
- Places where sections don't connect (the seams the video describes)
- Recommendations that don't follow clearly from the analysis
- Arguments that are present but underdeveloped
- Sections that are strong vs. sections that need more work before the final version

After the review, the team should triage: which problems get fixed before submission, and which are known gaps to address after Check-In #2 feedback. Not everything needs to be fixed now. But every team member should understand the current state of the full document, not just their own piece of it.
