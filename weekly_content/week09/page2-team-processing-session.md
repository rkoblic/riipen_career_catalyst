# The Team Processing Session

Two kinds of feedback arrive at your team this week, and both can be hard to work through honestly. The peer evaluation tells each of you how your teammates have experienced working with you across the project so far. The employer's mid-project survey tells your team how your working partner has experienced the project from outside. Some of what comes back will be validating. Some will be surprising, or feel off.

These two feedback sources are processed differently. Peer feedback is yours to work through individually, before the team meets, with the goal of arriving at the session with one or two commitments you've made to yourself. The employer's feedback is what the team works through together in the session itself, deciding as a unit what to change. Both pieces come out as a single written agreement at the end.

---

## Working through your peer feedback (before the session)

Peer feedback is processed individually rather than as a team, and that's deliberate. Real-time team conversation about personal peer evaluations, especially with grades involved, can put a strong team into defense mode and a weaker one into finger-pointing. Coming to the session with your own reflection done is the more professional version of the same conversation. You're not avoiding the feedback. You're owning it before bringing what you'll do about it to the people it affects.

Read your peer evaluation results in private and give yourself time to react. Notice what you agree with and what surprises you. Notice any gap between how you rated yourself and how your peers rated you. You don't need to resolve those gaps before the session, but having thought about them means you'll engage more honestly when you're in it.

If your first reaction is strong, AI can be a useful partner for working through it. The goal is to push back on your first interpretation and get at what else might be true, not to draft a response or have AI validate how you're reading it. A prompt like *"Here's feedback I received, and here's how I'm reading it. What am I not considering?"* tends to work. Remove identifying details before pasting, and treat what comes back as questions to sit with, not answers.

By the end of your individual work, land on one or two specific commitments you'll bring to the session. Concrete behavioral changes, not vague intentions. *"I'm going to be more communicative"* is too vague to act on. *"I'll send a quick async update by 5pm on the days I'm not in our standing meeting, even if it's a sentence"* is specific enough that you and your teammates will know whether it's happening. The Moving from conversation to agreement section below has more on what makes a commitment specific.

The commitments don't have to address every piece of feedback you received. Pick the one or two changes that would matter most to how you show up on this team for the rest of the project.

---

## Reading the employer's survey (before the session)

Read the employer's survey responses before the session as well. The employer's feedback is team-level rather than individual, so there's less personal load to process beforehand. Arriving with everyone having already read it means the team can respond thoughtfully in the session rather than reacting to the survey live.

---

## Opening the session: sharing your commitments

The session opens with each person briefly sharing the one or two commitments they made during their individual work. The point is to name what you've decided to do, not to relitigate the feedback that led you there. You're owning a behavior change in front of the teammates it will affect.

A few examples of what that might sound like:

- *"I committed to sending a brief async update on the days I'm not in our standing meeting, so the team isn't waiting on me to know where things stand."*
- *"I committed to asking 'what would change your mind?' before I push back on a teammate's analysis. I noticed I argue first and listen second."*
- *"I committed to reading the full document end to end before our review meetings, not just the section I worked on. I want to be useful in those reviews, not just present."*

You decide how much context to give. Naming the commitment in your own words works; you don't need to explain the feedback that produced it. What the team needs is each person's commitment, not a recap of the peer evaluation.

If a teammate is struggling to articulate their commitment in the moment, give them space rather than pressing. They've done the work alone; the share-out should feel like a declaration, not a defense. If someone hasn't landed on a commitment yet, naming that openly is better than inventing one under pressure. The session can leave room for them to bring it back to the team later.

## Processing the employer's feedback together

The employer's mid-project survey is the team's collective work in the session. Peer feedback is about how your team works from the inside; the employer's feedback is about how your team works from the outside: whether communication has been clear, whether the team has been prepared, whether responses have come on time.

A few things to keep in mind while working through it as a team.

**Start with the open-ended response.** That response is the most actionable part of the survey. It's the employer naming, in their own words, what they want more of, less of, or different for the rest of the project. Read it together, and make sure the team agrees on what the employer is asking for before debating what to do about it. If the wording is vague, spend a minute on interpretation; if it's specific, less interpretation is needed.

**Use the act-on, note, or set-aside framework.** Not every item in the employer's response is automatically an act-on. A request to change how the team communicates is usually act-on. A passing mention of something outside the project's scope is more often a note or a set-aside. The same framework your team has been using to triage feedback during development applies here.

**Look for overlap with patterns from your individual peer work.** During your individual peer-feedback work, some of you may have noticed themes that also show up in the employer's response. If multiple team members are seeing the same pattern from both directions, that's worth naming aloud — it's a stronger signal than either source alone. If the employer is flagging something that didn't show up in any of your peer work, the team may be presenting differently externally than internally.

**If the survey response was mostly positive, spend time on it anyway.** Positive feedback is data about what's working that the team should know to keep doing. It also deserves acknowledgment, which is covered later on this page.

**Remember what the survey is and isn't.** The employer rates three dimensions that are visible from outside (communication, preparedness, responsiveness). Internal dynamics and the technical quality of the deliverable are assessed elsewhere. If the employer's feedback doesn't touch on something your team knows is a real issue, that doesn't mean the issue isn't real. It means the employer didn't have visibility into it.

## When feedback feels off

Some feedback will be specific and recognizable. Some will be vague in a way that makes it hard to know what to do with. Some will describe a pattern you didn't intend. The questions below apply both to peer feedback you're working through alone and to employer feedback the team is processing together.

**Separate impact from intent.** Feedback often reflects the impact of something you did, not what you meant by it. If your peer feedback flagged you as seeming disengaged, you may have had your camera off for a practical reason. That reason is real. The experience of disengagement the feedback describes is also real. Both can be true, and addressing the impact doesn't require abandoning the intent.

**When feedback styles differ.** People from different backgrounds sometimes experience the same behavior very differently, and evaluation rubrics don't always account for that. If a rating feels culturally unfamiliar, or if the feedback language doesn't match how you think about a given behavior, it's worth naming that — to yourself if it's peer feedback, or in the team conversation if it's employer feedback. Useful agreements can still be made even without complete agreement on the interpretation.

**Ask for specifics, not validation.** When the team is working through employer feedback together, "Can we point to a specific instance where this showed up?" is a more useful question than "Do you all agree with this?" The first surfaces usable information. The second pulls the team toward consensus where there isn't any.

**When the feedback is vague.** If a piece of feedback is so generic you can't trace it to a specific behavior, the Clarify step from the Feedback Protocol (introduced on the Receiving Feedback Well page in the Production module) applies. For peer feedback, the team norm is that you don't bring it back to the team for clarification — sit with what's actionable in the comment, leave what isn't, and move on. For employer feedback, vague items go on a list of clarifying questions to bring to the next check-in rather than getting guessed at.

## When something needs a different venue

Most of what comes up can be worked through — peer feedback in your individual prep, employer feedback in the team session, both feeding into the agreement at the end. A few situations call for stepping outside that flow.

**Feedback that feels discriminatory or identity-based.** If the peer feedback you received targets you based on who you are rather than how you worked, or if it reads as prejudiced, that's not material to process alone or bring to the team. Bring it to your Experience Manager before the session. The same applies to anything in the employer's feedback.

**Someone unable to engage with the conversation.** If a teammate is in distress in a way that the share-out or the employer discussion can't hold, pause and let your Experience Manager know. Distress that the team can't absorb in the session isn't theirs to handle alone. The session can be rescheduled. Working through feedback when someone isn't in a state to engage with it does more harm than the delay.

**Employer feedback that raises a concern about the working relationship itself.** If the employer's survey response points to something structural about how the project is being run (scope that keeps shifting outside the brief, requests that don't line up with the project's boundaries, behavior that feels unprofessional toward the team), that's a conversation to have with your Experience Manager in the loop, not something for the team to decide how to respond to alone.

The purpose of the session is to produce useful agreements the team can live by. Stepping outside it when the situation calls for that is part of how the session stays useful.

## Moving from conversation to agreement

The session should end with a written agreement, not a conversation. Written agreements are more durable than intentions formed in the moment, and they give the team a reference point when the same issues come up again.

A useful agreement is specific, whether it's an individual commitment from the share-out or a team-level decision from the employer-feedback discussion. Specific means it names the behavior, the person responsible (or the team as a unit, where it's a team change), and how the team will know if it's working.

What makes an agreement specific enough to be useful:

**Too vague to act on:** "We'll communicate more consistently."

**Specific enough:** "Marcus will send a brief message in the team channel by end of day Thursday each week summarizing what he's finished, even if it's only a sentence."

The agreement doesn't have to cover every piece of feedback. Focus on the changes that would have the most effect on how the team is working. Two or three owned commitments are more useful than a long list nobody follows.

## The team processing agreement

End the session with a short written agreement, a paragraph or a simple table, that captures both halves of the work:

- **Individual commitments from the share-out.** Each person's commitment, in their words, with how the team will know it's happening.
- **Team commitments from the employer-feedback discussion.** What the team agreed to change, who owns each change, and how the team will know it's happening.

Save this to your team's shared workspace. It isn't submitted as a formal checkpoint. It's a working document your team keeps as a reference for the rest of the project, and it's also the material you'll draw from in Reflection #3, where you'll have a chance to write about what you personally committed to and what you think will be hard about following through.

Keep the agreement honest. It becomes a reference point later in the course when teams complete a second round of peer evaluation. If the same patterns resurface after an explicit agreement not to repeat them, there's a record, even if the record lives in your team's working files and the individual reflections rather than a single formal submission.

## Acknowledging the feedback with your employer

Your employer should hear, briefly, that your team received the mid-project survey feedback and is acting on it. This is a professional norm more than a course requirement. When someone takes the time to give structured feedback, silence on the receiving end tends to read as either dismissal or disengagement, and neither is what you want from the working relationship going into the rest of the project.

The acknowledgment belongs in the next check-in with your employer, not in a separate written message. It works best when it's short and forward-looking. Two parts:

1. A sentence naming that your team received the feedback.
2. One or two concrete things, tied to what the employer raised, that your team is doing differently because of it.

For example: *"We appreciated your notes in the mid-project survey. Based on what you raised about turnaround time on questions, we've agreed as a team to acknowledge any message from you the same day, even if the full response needs to come later."*

If the survey response was mostly positive, acknowledge it briefly without inflating it: *"Your note about how we've been structuring meetings was useful. It confirmed we were on the right track there, and we'll keep going that way."*

Keep the acknowledgment short. Debates about the feedback, apologies, or long explanations don't belong in it. If there are parts of the employer's feedback your team doesn't agree with, or context the employer may not have visibility into, the check-in conversation itself is a better place to surface those than the acknowledgment.
