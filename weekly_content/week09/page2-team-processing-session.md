# The Team Processing Session

Two kinds of feedback arrive at your team this week, and both can be hard to work through honestly. The peer evaluation tells each of you how your teammates have experienced working with you across the project so far. The employer's mid-project survey tells your team how your working partner has experienced the project from outside. Some of what comes back will be validating. Some will be surprising, or feel off. How your team handles the conversation about all of it has a real effect on how the rest of the project goes.

The Team Processing Session is a structured team conversation where you work through both feedback sources in a single sitting: your anonymized peer evaluation qualitative comments, your aggregated BARS averages (the behaviorally anchored scales from the peer evaluation) by dimension, and the employer's mid-project survey results. The goal is to come out of it with specific, named changes your team has actually committed to, not vague reassurances that things will improve.

---

## Before the session

Read your own peer evaluation results before the team gets together. You want to arrive having already had your first reaction privately, so that reaction doesn't land on your teammates.

Notice what you agree with and what surprises you. Notice any gap between how you rated yourself and how your peers rated you. You don't need to resolve those gaps before the session, but having thought about them means you'll engage with the conversation more honestly when you're in it.

If your first reaction to the feedback is strong, AI can be a useful partner for working through it before the session. The goal is to push back on your first interpretation and get at what else might be true, not to draft a response to the feedback or have AI validate how you're reading it. A prompt like *"Here's feedback I received, and here's how I'm reading it. What am I not considering?"* tends to work. Remove identifying details before pasting, and treat what comes back as questions to sit with, not answers.

Read the employer's survey responses before the session as well. The employer's feedback is team-level rather than individual, so there's less personal load to process beforehand. But arriving having already read it means the team can respond to it thoughtfully in the session rather than reacting to it live.

---

## Opening the conversation

There's no single right way to start. One approach that tends to work: each team member shares one observation from their results that felt significant, whether validating or surprising. This isn't a defense or an appeal, it's just naming what stood out. It gets everyone into the conversation without anyone feeling like they're being targeted or evaluated first.

A few examples of what that might sound like:

- *"The feedback on how I contribute during our analysis discussions was more positive than I expected. I'd been uncertain about whether I was adding enough there, so it's useful to hear."*
- *"A couple of comments mentioned me coming across as checked out during async updates. That surprised me, since I thought I was being responsive. I want to understand what's giving that impression."*
- *"There's a gap between how I rated myself on communication and how my teammates rated me. I rated myself higher, and I want to hold that in mind during the conversation."*

You decide how much of your own feedback to share, and in what words. Naming what stood out in your own language works; reading specific ratings or comments verbatim isn't required. What the team needs from the session is enough signal from each person to land on useful agreements, not a full reading of every piece of feedback.

If someone on your team received feedback that's difficult, they'll likely need a moment before they can engage with it productively. That's normal. The instinct to defend or minimize usually passes quickly if the team isn't pushing. Letting it pass is part of what makes the conversation worth having.

---

## When the feedback feels off

Some feedback will be specific and recognizable. Some will be vague in a way that makes it hard to know what to do with. Some will describe a pattern you didn't intend.

A few ways to engage with feedback that doesn't immediately make sense:

**Ask for specifics, not validation.** "Can anyone point to a specific instance where this showed up?" is a more useful question than "Do you all agree with this?" The first surfaces usable information. The second asks teammates to take sides.

**Separate impact from intent.** Feedback often reflects the impact of something you did, not what you meant by it. If a teammate received feedback about seeming disengaged, they may have had their camera off for a practical reason. That reason is real. The experience of disengagement the feedback describes is also real. Both can be true, and addressing the impact doesn't require abandoning the intent.

**When feedback styles differ.** People from different backgrounds sometimes experience the same behavior very differently, and evaluation rubrics don't always account for that. If a rating feels culturally unfamiliar, or if the feedback language doesn't match how you think about a given behavior, it's worth naming that in the conversation. The team can still make a useful agreement even without complete agreement on the interpretation.

**When the feedback is vague.** If the feedback you received is so generic that you can't trace it to a specific behavior, the Clarify step from the Feedback Protocol (introduced on the Receiving Feedback Well page in the Production module) applies here too. Ask the team what they noticed. That conversation may surface more than the written feedback did.

## Processing the employer's feedback

The employer's mid-project survey is a different kind of feedback from the peer evaluation and deserves its own time in the session. Peer feedback is about how your team works from the inside. The employer's feedback is about how your team works from the outside: whether communication has been clear, whether the team has been prepared, whether responses have come on time.

A few things to keep in mind while working through it as a team.

**Start with the open-ended response.** That response is the most actionable part of the survey. It's the employer naming, in their own words, what they want more of, less of, or different for the rest of the project. Read it together, and make sure the team agrees on what the employer is actually asking for before jumping to what to do about it. If the wording is vague, spend a minute on interpretation; if it's specific, less interpretation is needed.

**Use the act-on, note, or set-aside framework.** Not every item in the employer's response is automatically an act-on. A request to change how the team communicates is usually act-on. A passing mention of something outside the project's scope is more often a note or a set-aside. The same framework your team has been using to triage feedback during production applies here.

**Look for overlap and divergence with the peer feedback.** If the employer is flagging something that also showed up in the peer evaluation, that's a pattern the team is now seeing from two directions and should treat seriously. If the employer is flagging something that doesn't appear anywhere in peer comments, the team may be presenting differently externally than internally. Both cases are worth a few minutes of team conversation.

**If the survey response was mostly positive, spend time on it anyway.** Positive feedback is data about what's working that the team should know to keep doing. It also deserves acknowledgment, which is covered later on this page.

**Remember what the survey is and isn't.** The employer rates three dimensions that are visible from outside (communication, preparedness, responsiveness). Internal dynamics and the technical quality of the deliverable are assessed elsewhere. If the employer's feedback doesn't touch on something your team knows is a real issue, that doesn't mean the issue isn't real. It means the employer didn't have visibility into it.

## When something needs a different venue

Most of what comes up in peer and employer feedback can be worked through in the team session, and that's the default. A few situations call for stepping outside the session instead.

**Feedback that feels discriminatory or identity-based.** If feedback targets a teammate (or you) based on who they are rather than how they worked, or if it reads as prejudiced, that's not a team-processing conversation. Bring it to your instructor.

**Someone unable to engage with the conversation.** If a teammate is in distress in a way that the session can't hold, pause the conversation. The session can be rescheduled. Working through feedback when someone isn't in a state to engage with it does more harm than the delay.

**Employer feedback that raises a concern about the working relationship itself.** If the employer's survey response points to something structural about how the project is being run (scope that keeps shifting outside the brief, requests that don't line up with the project's boundaries, behavior that feels unprofessional toward the team), that's a conversation to have with your instructor in the loop, not something for the team to decide how to respond to alone.

The purpose of the session is to produce useful agreements the team can actually live by. Stepping outside it when the situation calls for that is part of how the session stays useful.

---

## Moving from conversation to agreement

The session should end with a written agreement, not a conversation. Written agreements are more durable than intentions formed in the moment, and they give the team a reference point when the same issues come up again.

A useful agreement is specific: it names the behavior, the person responsible, and how the team will know if it's working.

What makes an agreement specific enough to be useful:

**Too vague to act on:** "We'll communicate more consistently."

**Specific enough:** "Marcus will send a brief message in the team channel by end of day Thursday each week summarizing what he's finished, even if it's only a sentence."

The agreement doesn't have to cover every piece of feedback. Focus on the changes that would have the most effect on how the team is working. Two or three genuinely owned commitments are more useful than a long list nobody follows.

---

## The team processing agreement

End the session with a short written agreement, a paragraph or a simple table, that names:

- What specific behavioral changes the team agreed to make
- Who owns each change
- How the team will know if the change is happening

Save this to your team's shared workspace. It isn't submitted as a formal checkpoint. It's a working document your team keeps as a reference for the rest of the project, and it's also the material you'll draw from in Reflection #3, where you'll have a chance to write about what you personally committed to and what you think will actually be hard about following through.

Keep the agreement honest. It becomes a reference point later in the course when teams complete a second round of peer evaluation. If the same patterns resurface after an explicit agreement not to repeat them, there's a record, even if the record lives in your team's working files and the individual reflections rather than a single formal submission.

## Acknowledging the feedback with your employer

Your employer should hear, briefly, that your team received the mid-project survey feedback and is acting on it. This is a professional norm more than a course requirement. When someone takes the time to give structured feedback, silence on the receiving end tends to read as either dismissal or disengagement, and neither is what you want from the working relationship going into the rest of the project.

The acknowledgment belongs in the next check-in with your employer, not in a separate written message. It works best when it's short, specific, and forward-looking. Two parts:

1. A sentence naming that your team received the feedback.
2. One or two concrete things, tied to what the employer raised, that your team is doing differently because of it.

For example: *"We appreciated your notes in the mid-project survey. Based on what you raised about turnaround time on questions, we've agreed as a team to acknowledge any message from you the same day, even if the full response needs to come later."*

If the survey response was mostly positive, acknowledge it briefly without inflating it: *"Your note about how we've been structuring meetings was useful. It confirmed we were on the right track there, and we'll keep going that way."*

Keep the acknowledgment short. Debates about the feedback, apologies, or long explanations don't belong in it. If there are parts of the employer's feedback your team doesn't agree with, or context the employer may not have visibility into, the check-in conversation itself is a better place to surface those than the acknowledgment.
