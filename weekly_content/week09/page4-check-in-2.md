# The check-in with your employer

This check-in follows the same working session format as the last one: your team drives the agenda with specific questions, and the employer acts as a resource. What's changed is the question your team is now in a position to ask.

At your last check-in, the most useful questions were about direction: is the approach right, are we focused on the right problem, does the framing match what the employer needs? You had partial work and needed to confirm you were building the right thing.

Now you have a draft. The question has shifted from "are we on track?" to "is this good enough?" Your team can ask directly about quality, depth, completeness, and whether the work meets the bar the employer needs it to meet. That's a different conversation, and the prep has to shift with it.

The meeting runs about 30 minutes and may end earlier if your team covers what it needs to.

---

## Preparing

### Develop questions about quality

The questions that earn their time in this check-in are specific to the judgment calls in your draft. The employer won't have read the draft before the meeting, so your questions need to describe the choice your team made, then ask whether it's right:

- "Our competitive analysis covers [three peer organizations] and focuses on [specific dimensions]. Is that the right breadth for the kind of benchmarking your team needs?"
- "Our recommendation on [specific topic] is based on [specific assumption]. Does that assumption match your read of the situation?"
- "We've scoped [specific thread] out of this deliverable and flagged it in future recommendations. Does that framing make sense given your priorities?"

The shape is consistent: state the call in one sentence, then ask whether it was the right one. A question the employer can answer in the moment is worth more than a question that would require them to read the draft first.

Walk through the draft with your team before the prep session. Every section where your team made a judgment call about depth, scope, or approach is a candidate question.

### What's different about this prep compared to previous check-ins

At your last check-in, your team was preparing questions about decisions you hadn't fully made yet. Now you've made them. The questions should reflect that: you're not asking for general direction, you're asking whether the specific choices you made were the right ones.

Your team also has more context about how to use working sessions productively. Questions informed by the internal review gate findings, the peer evaluation process, and your team's own assessment of the draft's strengths and weaknesses will be sharper than the questions your team was able to bring before..

### Anticipate what the employer will ask

Employers in these meetings often ask questions about reasoning, not just findings. Why did your team approach the problem this way? Why didn't you pursue a particular direction? What's the evidence base for this recommendation?

You can use AI to anticipate this. The same approach your team used before previous check-ins applies here, focused on quality and reasoning questions rather than progress ones. Review what AI generates and use it to identify any places where your team's reasoning is thin. If AI surfaces a question your team can't answer, decide how to handle it before the meeting.

### The brief status opening

Open the meeting with two to three minutes of context: what's in the draft, where it stands relative to your original project plan, and any significant changes from what the employer saw at the last check-in. This opening is also the right moment to briefly acknowledge the employer's mid-project survey feedback and name one or two concrete things your team is doing differently because of it. The Team Processing Session page has the detail on what that acknowledgment looks like. Keep the whole opening short. The employer is there for the conversation, not a progress report.

---

## During the meeting

A reasonable shape for the 30 minutes:

- **Status context (about 3 minutes).** Brief factual summary. What's in the draft, what's changed since Check-In #1.
- **Your questions (about 18–20 minutes).** The team leads each question. Capture answers as specifically as possible. Follow up when an answer is directional but not specific: "You mentioned the recommendations could be stronger. Is there a particular section where you'd want to see more depth?"
- **Employer observations (about 5 minutes).** After your questions, the employer may have observations that didn't come up in response to yours. This is often where the most direct quality feedback surfaces.
- **Preview of what's next (about 2 minutes).** Let the employer know what the near-final version will include and roughly when they'll see it. This keeps them oriented and gives you a natural close.

If anything the employer says is ambiguous, clarify in the moment: "Just to confirm: are you saying you'd like more primary source data in this section, or that the methodology itself needs explaining?" An answer that goes unclarified tends to get interpreted differently by different team members in the debrief.

---

## After the meeting

Debrief immediately. The mechanics are the same ones your team has used after previous employer meetings: pull up your notes, capture the substance of what you heard, and translate it into concrete next steps before the conversation fades.

What's different here: the debrief feeds into two things. First, your revision plan for the final version of the deliverable. Second, your Change Log, the running record your team started in the previous module of feedback received, decisions made, and how those decisions show up in the deliverable. Log each piece of substantive feedback the employer gave, what you heard them saying, and what you plan to do about it. The Change Log builds throughout the remainder of the project and will be submitted with the final deliverable as a record of how employer input shaped the work.

If you used an AI note-taking tool during the meeting, cross-check the transcript against your manual notes before the debrief closes. The cautions from earlier in the course apply: the transcript is a backup, not a source of record for decisions.
