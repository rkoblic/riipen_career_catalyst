## Patterns to carry forward

**Scrub em dashes on every pass, and treat them as banned in video scripts.** Week 9 had em dashes scattered across three page files and both video scripts. Most were doing work commas, periods, or colons could do. In the video scripts they're particularly bad because they don't translate to narrated audio. Worth running a quick grep for `—` before declaring a week done.

**Ambiguous "the previous module" is a tell.** Twice on Page 2 the text said "the previous module" when it meant Week 8's structural review, not the section directly above. When the referent could be either the current page or an earlier week, name the skill ("the structural review skills you've been using during production") rather than pointing at a file.

**Drop specific percentages in favor of the assessment component's name.** Continuing the Week 8 pattern, the overview page's "contributes approximately 5% of your final grade" became "contributes to the Reflection Portfolio component of your final grade." Specific percentages age badly and compound errors across pages.

**Replace numbered "Check-In #N" labels with relational evergreen phrasing.** Extending the Week 8 pattern: numbered meeting labels ("Check-In #2," "Check-In #1") read as internal-spec references and break if the sequence changes. Replaced every learner-facing "Check-In #2" on Week 9 with contextually chosen evergreen wording: "your next check-in with the employer," "this check-in," "the check-in," "when the employer reviews it," "the employer review." Within the same sentence as "Check-In #1," I converted both to ordinal form ("our first check-in," "the second") so the dialogue example reads naturally. Worth running the same pass over "Check-In #1" references throughout Week 9 and upstream weeks where they appear.

**Weigh employer burden when deciding what to ask them to review.** The Week 9 check-in was originally designed with the employer pre-reading the assembled draft. Reviewing that choice against the rest of the touchpoint arc — kickoff, Direction Check (document review), Check-In #1, Check-In #2, Near-Final (document review), Final Presentation — the Week 9 draft review was redundant with the Week 11 near-final document review, and doubled the document-reading ask on the employer. Dropped the employer review; the Draft Deliverable stays as an internal assembly checkpoint, and the check-in becomes purely question-driven. Generalizable rule: when adding an employer touchpoint, ask whether the same feedback surface is already covered elsewhere in the arc before adding to the employer's reading load.

**Don't conflate a collective artifact with individual accountability.** The Team Processing Session originally produced a team agreement that got appended to the Draft Deliverable submission. That tried to do two jobs: a team-owned working record of agreed behavior changes, and the accountability thread for the Week 15 peer evaluation. Moved the second job into Reflection #3, where each student writes about what they personally committed to and what they expect to find hard about following through. The team still produces the written agreement (the session needs a concrete output), but it's saved to the team's shared workspace as a working document rather than submitted as an appendix. More honest accountability (each person owns their commitment in their own words); lighter submission bureaucracy; the Week 15 reference point now lives in four individual reflections rather than one team document.

**Anchor the overview video on the one thing that makes the week distinct.** The original video ("what assembling a draft actually tests") was technically well-constructed but wasn't about anything particular to Week 9 — "you can ask sharper questions now that you've done the work" applies to most check-ins in the back half of the course. Rewrote the video around the Team Processing Session: the professional insight that the instinct when surprising feedback arrives is to explain yourself, and the observation that most workplaces don't run this kind of conversation until something has already gone wrong. That's actually distinctive about Week 9, and it's the moment where the learner's choices set the tone for the rest of the project. Generalizable rule: if an overview video could be dropped into another week with minor edits, it's not doing enough work.

**Let the page structure follow the actual flow of the work.** Originally Week 9 had seven pages: overview, strengthening-your-draft (skills), peer-evaluation-conversation, draft-deliverable (submission logistics), check-in, reflection, what's next. The skills page and the submission page were teaching the same arc (assemble → review → submit) split across two files, which meant the learner had to flip back and forth between them. Collapsed skills-plus-submission into a single Draft Deliverable page: opener frames the assembly stage, then the three skills (integration, actionability, scope), then the internal review gate, then the checkpoint logistics (what it is, what to include, completion criteria, AI guidance, submission). Also moved the Team Processing Session to Page 2 because it's the first thing that happens chronologically in the week and the most distinctive thing the learner does. New order: Overview → Team Processing Session → Draft Deliverable → Check-In → Reflection #3 → What's next.

**Name the referent on second use of a course-specific term.** Page 3 said "your aggregated BARS averages by dimension" without reminding the learner that BARS is the behaviorally anchored scales introduced the previous week. Same fix on Page 5 for the Change Log: a one-line pointer back to where the term was introduced keeps the scan readable for someone dipping into a single page.

**When a question is a learner-to-employer example, check the pronoun antecedents.** Page 5's example question read "Is the depth… sufficient for what your team needs to act on these findings?" — but the team asking the question wouldn't then also act on the employer's behalf. Every "your team" in a scripted question should get a quick audit against "who is speaking, who is listening, who actually does the action."

**Close video scripts on the strongest concrete line, not a meta summary.** Both Week 9 videos had summary closers ("That's what this week is designed to test…", "That's what the integration pass is for…"). Dropping the summary and letting the last concrete line land makes the video feel like a person explaining something, not a voice wrapping up. The overview video guide says this explicitly; it's worth treating as a default rule, not an optional check.

**"The place where Y lives" is a named AI-ism.** Page 6 had "The pattern is where professional self-knowledge lives." The content-production guide flags this construction by name. Worth grepping for `where.*lives` and similar metaphorical constructions on each pass.
