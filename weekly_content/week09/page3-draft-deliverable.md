# The Draft Deliverable

Professional teams produce deliverables in parts, then assemble them. The assembly stage is where a document either becomes a coherent whole or reveals itself as a collection of sections. The point of the work this week isn't to finish the document; it's to assemble the pieces, see the full thing honestly, and identify what has to change before the near-final version.

Three skills help you get there: integrating work written by different people, testing whether your recommendations are actionable, and managing scope decisions that tend to surface under the pressure of a deadline. The value of the work is seeing what's there, not finishing it all at once.

---

## Making sections talk to each other

When your team does the integration pass, look for these problems:

- **Inconsistent terminology.** The same concept referred to by different names across sections (for example, the same audience referred to as "followers," "users," and "community members" in three sections of one social media strategy). Pick one and use it throughout.
- **Undefined assumptions.** One section depends on context that was only established in a previous section the reader may have skimmed. Add the context or add a cross-reference.
- **Logic breaks between sections.** The argument in section A points toward conclusion B, but section B starts without connecting to what came before. Bridge it explicitly.
- **Register shifts.** One section reads as formal academic prose, the next as conversational. Match the register across the document.
- **Transition gaps.** Sections that start cold, without any signal that the document has shifted focus. A short orienting sentence at the opening of each major section goes a long way.

## The actionability test

A strong deliverable does more than report what you found. It gives the employer something to act on. This is the standard that matters most for the Actionability dimension of the Final Deliverables rubric, which assesses whether your recommendations are specific enough to implement, realistic given the employer's constraints, and prioritized.

The gap between "here's what we found" and "here's what you should do about it" is where many teams' recommendations get flagged as not yet actionable. The findings may be solid; the recommendations that follow from them may be too vague to act on.

A useful test for each recommendation: could your employer pick this up on Monday and know what to do? "Streamline the intake workflow" fails the test. "Reduce the intake form from 12 fields to 6 by removing the three fields the operations manager flagged as unused, before the August system rollout" passes it. The second version is specific, time-bounded, and within what the employer can do.

**Using AI to test actionability**

This is a new application of AI as a development partner, building on the structural review skills you've been using during development. Once you have a draft of your recommendations section, try this prompt: "Read these recommendations and tell me which ones are too vague for a client to act on. For each one, identify what's missing: is it specificity, feasibility, or prioritization?"

Review what AI flags. It will often surface real gaps. It will also sometimes flag recommendations as vague when they're deliberately high-level because the implementation is the employer's call. Use your judgment: the test is whether your employer can act on it.

## Scope decisions: what to keep, what to defer

Around this point in a project, teams often discover they've identified more interesting territory than they can cover in the time remaining. That pressure tends to produce one of two responses: teams either rush to include everything (producing thin coverage of a lot of things) or they quietly drop threads without explicitly deciding to (and then forget what they chose not to pursue).

The professional approach is to make scope decisions explicitly. There are three options:

- **Include it.** It belongs in the current deliverable because it's central to what the employer asked for.
- **Defer it.** It's valuable, but it's outside the scope of this engagement. Include it in a "future recommendations" section at the end of the deliverable so the employer knows the team saw it and made a choice.
- **Decline it.** It's a direction that came up but doesn't fit the project's purpose. Name it clearly in a team conversation so everyone knows the decision was made.

When scope pressure comes from the employer (a comment in a check-in, an email, a suggestion to add something), the same framework applies. You can acknowledge the suggestion and explain which category it falls into. "That's a direction we looked at and decided to flag in future recommendations rather than include in the current scope" is a professional response.

## The internal review gate

Before your team submits the draft and before the next check-in, run one internal review. Each team member reads the full draft end-to-end, not just their own sections. This is a professional quality habit.

What to look for:
- Places where sections don't connect (the seams the video describes)
- Recommendations that don't follow clearly from the analysis
- Arguments that are present but underdeveloped
- Sections that are strong vs. sections that need more work before the final version

After the review, the team should triage: which problems get fixed before submission, and which are known gaps to address as the draft evolves toward the near-final version. Not everything needs to be fixed now. But every team member should understand the current state of the full document, not just their own piece of it.

The structural review prompts you've been using during development apply here too. Running the full assembled draft through a structural review ("identify weak arguments, unclear transitions, or places where evidence doesn't support the claim being made") can surface issues that are hard to catch when reading section by section. Apply it to the assembled document after your team has done the human read-through, not as a substitute for it.

## What this checkpoint is (and isn't)

The Draft Deliverable is a completion-based team checkpoint. It is not graded for quality. What's assessed is whether your team has made substantive progress and produced genuine work product.

The employer doesn't pre-read this submission. It's an internal checkpoint submitted to the platform for completion verification. The next check-in is a question-driven working session, not a draft review, and the employer will do a full document review at the near-final stage later in the course. The assembly this week is for your team's own quality control: to see the document as a whole and identify what has to change before the near-final version.

It is not a polished final document. Rough edges, incomplete sections, and areas you know need more work are all normal at this stage. What makes a draft different from a placeholder is that it reflects real work, not a list of topics your team intends to address.

---

## What counts as complete

A complete submission:

- Contains all major sections of the deliverable in draft form, even if some are incomplete
- Has been through your team's internal review gate
- Includes an AI disclosure statement

"Major sections" means the primary components defined in your project plan, not supplementary materials, appendices, or optional content. For each section:

- The analysis or content your team has developed, in prose or structured format appropriate to your deliverable type
- The recommendations or outputs that follow from that analysis, even if they're still rough
- Source citations or references where relevant

Sections that aren't fully developed should still be present. A section in rough form is more useful to the internal review than a gap with a note that it's coming. The standard is genuine work product, not outlines, bullet-point placeholders, or intent statements. If your team is genuinely behind on a section, submit what exists and name the gap in the document rather than leaving it unmarked.

## Using AI

The development-oriented AI skills introduced over the previous weeks apply here. Appropriate uses include structural review of the assembled draft, stress-testing recommendations for actionability, checking for consistency across sections, and organizing your team's notes into a revision plan.

AI cannot produce the substance of the deliverable. The research, analysis, and recommendations must reflect your team's genuine intellectual work. The test is the same as it has been throughout the course: could you defend every claim in the document without referring back to what an AI produced? The check-in with your employer is where they'll ask about the work you've built. Anything you can't speak to doesn't belong in the draft.

**AI disclosure:** Required on this submission. If AI was used at any point in drafting or organizing the deliverable, include a brief description of how. If AI was not used, include a one-sentence statement to that effect.
