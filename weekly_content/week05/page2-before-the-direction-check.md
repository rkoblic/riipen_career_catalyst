# Before the Direction Check

Direction Checks go best when teams come in with a plan for the time. This page covers how to structure the conversation, handle harder topics like scope or feasibility gaps, and confirm logistics with the employer.

---

## How to run this meeting

[VIDEO: How to walk through a document with a stakeholder]

The video above walks through how to structure a 30-minute document-based meeting with a stakeholder. Here's a recap of the key moves:

- **Don't walk through the document from start to finish.** You'll run out of time before you get to the parts where you need input. Highlight 2-3 key areas instead.
- **Choose what to highlight based on where the employer's perspective matters most.** Your strongest finding and why it matters, the recommendation you're most confident about, and the area where you're least certain.
- **Skip what the employer can read on their own.** Don't restate the organizational context. Don't read research findings like a list. Don't walk through the timeline item by item.
- **Transition to feedback with specific questions, not vague ones.** "We chose to focus on this segment because of our research. Does that match what you're seeing internally?" gets a real answer. "Any questions?" gets silence.
- **Assign roles before the meeting.** Someone opens and frames, someone presents key points, someone takes notes (exact language, not summaries), someone tracks time.

## Managing expectations and difficult conversations

At some point during a project, you may encounter a gap between what the employer expects and what's feasible. Maybe the scope has grown beyond what your team can deliver in the time remaining. Maybe the data they assumed you'd have access to doesn't exist. Maybe their vision for the final deliverable doesn't match the skills or resources available to your team.

This is normal. It happens in every professional engagement, not just course projects. Teams that handle it well name the gap early and clearly.

Saying "we can't do that" is professional when it comes with context. "Based on what we've found so far, the original scope would require access to data we don't have. Here's what we can do instead, and here's why we think it still addresses your core question." That's the team doing its job, not failing at it.

The version to avoid is silence: agreeing to something you can't deliver because the conversation feels uncomfortable. The employer would rather hear a realistic adjustment now than a disappointing deliverable later.

## Preparing for the Direction Check

You have 30 minutes. Here's how to use them well.

**Decide what to highlight (before the meeting):**

Go through your Context Analysis & Project Plan and identify the 2-3 areas where the employer's input matters most. Good candidates:

- A finding that surprised you or that might surprise the employer
- The recommendation you're most confident about and the evidence behind it
- An assumption you're making that could change your whole approach if it's wrong
- A tradeoff you made (e.g., narrowing scope) where you want the employer to confirm the choice

**Prepare specific questions:**

- Questions that couldn't be answered from the project brief alone
- Questions about priorities: "If we had to choose between X and Y, which matters more to your team?"
- Questions about audience: who will actually use or read the deliverable, and what do they care about?

**A rough time structure:**

- Opening and framing (~2 min): "You've read our document. We'd like to highlight three areas and get your feedback."
- Key highlights (~8-10 min): Your 2-3 most important points, with reasoning
- Employer feedback and discussion (~15-18 min): This is the most valuable part. Protect this time.
- Confirming next steps (~2 min): What changes, what stays, and what you'll follow up on

**Assign roles:**

- Who opens and frames the conversation
- Who presents which highlights
- Who takes notes (capturing exact language, not summaries)
- Who tracks time — 30 minutes goes fast, and you don't want to eat into feedback time

If your team uses an AI note-taking tool, set it up before the meeting starts. It's a useful backup, but it's not a replacement for a human notetaker. AI transcripts miss nuance, misattribute speakers, and sometimes get key phrases wrong. You'll want both the transcript and someone on your team actively capturing what matters.

**One last thing — confirm logistics with the employer.** Before the meeting, send a brief message through the Riipen platform with the time, meeting link, who's attending from your team, and a short agenda. Something like "We'd like to highlight a few key areas from our Context Analysis and Project Plan and get your feedback on our proposed approach" is enough.

## When the meeting doesn't go as planned

Two things happen often enough to plan for. The employer arrives without having read your document, or the conversation shifts off your prepared agenda. Neither is an emergency.

### When the employer hasn't read the document

This is more common than you'd expect. Busy partners skim, and sometimes the document is still sitting unopened in their inbox when the call starts. The skill is making the meeting useful anyway.

**Confirm early, and give them an out.** Don't spend five minutes presenting to someone who's reading the document for the first time over your shoulder. Open with something like: "Just to make sure we use the time well, did you get a chance to look at the document, or would it be more useful if we walked you through the key pieces?" That phrasing keeps the employer off the defensive and lets you adjust before you've burned your highlights.

**Swap your agenda for a short verbal walkthrough.** Don't try to summarize the whole document. In about 5-7 minutes, cover:

- The problem you understood you were solving, so they can correct you if you've drifted
- The direction you're proposing
- The biggest assumption or tradeoff you've made

That's enough to give the employer something to react to without recapping every section.

**Shift your questions from confirmatory to exploratory.** Confirmatory questions ("does our analysis hold up?") need someone who has read the work. Exploratory ones don't. Try:

- "Does this match what you had in mind when you scoped the project?"
- "Is there context we should know about that isn't in the brief?"
- "What would make the final deliverable feel useful to you when you see it?"

**Send a short written recap within 24 hours.** A few bullets is enough: the direction you walked through, what they confirmed, what's still open. This is your real chance to get them aligned in writing. It's lower-effort than re-reading the full document, and it gives them an easy place to flag anything later.

**A note on tone.** Treat this as the norm rather than a failure. A meeting where the employer hasn't prepped can still be one of the most useful conversations of the project. Sometimes more so, because it forces your team to articulate the direction out loud.

### When the conversation shifts off your agenda

Sometimes the employer raises a concern that pulls the meeting in a different direction than you planned. Follow their lead. What they want to talk about is usually what matters most to them, even if it isn't what you prepared for. Capture what they say carefully, and use the last few minutes to confirm where that leaves your priorities for the next phase.
