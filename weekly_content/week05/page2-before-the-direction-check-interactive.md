# When the direction shifts: responding without defending

## Purpose

This exercise practices the skill of responding constructively when an employer redirects a project mid-stream. Defending your work is the natural impulse when feedback feels unexpected, but it shuts down the conversation just when you most need to understand what the employer is asking for. The interactive gives you a low-stakes practice rep before your real Direction Check, so the moves from this page (clarifying questions, capturing exact language, separating reaction from response) are already familiar when you need them.

## Interaction model

**Format: Video stimulus + decision points.**

Students watch a short Synthesia video of the employer (David Park, returning from the Week 3 kickoff) delivering pushback during the Direction Check meeting. After the video, a brief text card explains what the team's situation was going into the meeting (the "what just happened" framing). Then students answer questions about what David said, what to say next, what to capture, and how to handle the redirect afterward.

Each question is multiple-choice with one best answer. After answering, students receive immediate feedback explaining why the correct answer is correct and why tempting alternatives are wrong. The video is not available during the questions — students answer from their own notes, mirroring a real meeting.

**Why video and not text:** This exercise is about hearing the actual critique versus a generic version of it, and capturing exact language. Text lets students re-read and visually find the right phrase; video forces them to listen for tone, qualifiers, and the specific ask in real time. That's closer to the professional skill being practiced.

## Context setup

You're 15 minutes into your Direction Check with Bridgeway Community Health. You may remember David Park, your contact from the kickoff — Director of Community Programs. Over the past two weeks, your team has researched the literature on health behavior change, and you came into this meeting confident in your direction.

You've just walked David through your most important finding: a well-cited model from the public health literature explaining why educational interventions often fail to produce behavior change. You proposed that your recommendations would build on this framework.

You stopped talking. David takes a beat before responding.

## Stimulus

### David's response (video)

The student watches a ~1:30 Synthesia video of David delivering the pushback. Full script is in `page2-before-the-direction-check-synthesia-script.md` (alongside this file). Key listening targets:

- **The acknowledgement:** "I appreciate the rigor here" — used in Q1 wrong-answer A to test whether students hear the methodology critique that isn't there.
- **The actual critique:** Research grounded in "general adult populations," whereas Bridgeway's participants don't fit those samples. The educators "have built up a lot of knowledge about what actually shifts behavior here." This is the Q1 correct-answer target.
- **The softener:** "I don't want you to walk away thinking we're starting from scratch." The Week 5 questions test whether students hear this and don't overreact (Q1 wrong-D, Q4).
- **The specific ask:** "I'd like to see how you're going to weigh what our educators have told you against what the literature says." This is the verbatim quote Q3 asks students to capture.
- **The pointed follow-up:** "If you haven't talked to them yet, I'd like to know what your plan is there." Sets up Q2 and Q5.

### What just happened (text card after the video)

Shown as a separate text card between the video and the first question:

> Your team had not planned to consult the community health educators. That wasn't in the original scope, and primary research was excluded at the kickoff. Your team has been working primarily from secondary research.

This is meta-narration (the teacher pulling the curtain back, not David speaking), so it stays as a text card rather than being added to the video.

## Questions

### Question 1

**Question text:** What is David actually pushing back on?

**Type:** multiple-choice

**Options:**
- A) The quality of the team's research methodology.
- B) The relevance of the team's source material to Bridgeway's specific participant communities.
- C) The team's ability to deliver the project on time.
- D) The decision to build recommendations at all, rather than leaving that to the educators.

**Correct answer:** B

**Feedback if correct:** Right. David was specific: the literature your team is using is grounded in general adult populations, and Bridgeway's participants don't fit those samples. He's not questioning whether you're doing good research — he's questioning whether it's the right research for this project. Hearing the actual critique, not a more generic version of it, is the first step in responding well.

**Feedback if incorrect:**
- A) David said "I appreciate the rigor here" — he's not critiquing your methodology. Defaulting to "they think our research is bad" when the actual critique is more specific would lead your team to defend the wrong thing.
- C) David didn't mention timeline. Hearing time pressure where none was raised is a common reaction when feedback feels destabilizing, but it puts you in defensive mode about something the employer isn't asking about.
- D) David explicitly said the opposite — "I don't want you to walk away thinking we're starting from scratch." Interpreting a redirect as a rejection of the entire project would lead to an overreaction that's harder to walk back than a small course correction would be.

### Question 2

**Question text:** Before responding, what's the most useful thing to say next?

**Type:** multiple-choice

**Options:**
- A) "Our framework is well-established in the public health literature, so we'd want to defend keeping it as the foundation."
- B) "We can pivot to interviews with the educators if you think that's a better direction."
- C) "Can you tell us more about what the educators have told you? We want to make sure we're weighting that the right way."
- D) "We hadn't planned to interview the educators because primary research was out of scope, but we can rework it."

**Correct answer:** C

**Feedback if correct:** Good. This is the listening-without-defending move: ask a clarifying question before you commit to anything. You don't yet know what specifically the educators have observed, what access David has to that knowledge, or what "weighting" he has in mind. Committing to a pivot, defending your framework, or volunteering to rework the project all skip past the part where you understand the redirect first. Curiosity buys your team time and information.

**Feedback if incorrect:**
- A) Leading with a defense signals that you're more invested in your existing work than in hearing the feedback. Even if your reasoning is sound, this response shuts down the conversation. You can name your reasoning later, after you've understood the critique fully.
- B) Pivoting on the spot, before you've understood what's being asked, is the other defensive move — overcorrecting to make the discomfort go away. You don't yet know what the right adjustment is. Asking comes first.
- D) This one is closer to the right move, because it names a real constraint (primary research scope), but it leads with the constraint rather than with curiosity about what David is actually asking. It also implicitly commits to reworking the project before you've fully heard him out.

### Question 3

**Question text:** Your team's notetaker should capture what David said. Which of the following is the most useful version to write down?

**Type:** multiple-choice

**Options:**
- A) "David disagreed with our research approach."
- B) "David wants us to interview the community health educators."
- C) "David: 'I'd like to see how you're going to weigh what our educators have told you against what the literature says.'"
- D) "David thinks our research isn't relevant to immigrant and refugee communities."

**Correct answer:** C

**Feedback if correct:** Exactly. Capture exact language, especially the specific question David is asking. "How you're going to weigh what our educators have told you against what the literature says" is the actual ask — not "do interviews," not "your research is wrong." When your team debriefs, you'll need David's words, not your interpretation of them. The interpretation can drift; the quote can't.

**Feedback if incorrect:**
- A) "Disagreed with our research approach" is your reaction to what David said, not what he actually said. Notes that capture impressions instead of language lose the specific information your team will need to act on the feedback later.
- B) David didn't ask for interviews. He asked how you'd weight the educators' knowledge — which might mean interviews, or might mean reviewing internal documents, or might mean a conversation with David himself. Recording your interpretation as the ask narrows the options before you've thought it through.
- D) This is a summary of part of what David said, but it loses the specific question. Notes that paraphrase fluently are easy to write and hard to use, because they leave the team without the original framing to refer back to.

### Question 4

**Question text:** After the meeting, your team is debriefing. One teammate says, "We have to scrap the whole literature framework and start over with interviews." What's the most professional response?

**Type:** multiple-choice

**Options:**
- A) Agree. The employer made it clear the literature approach isn't working.
- B) Disagree. The literature is established and David said not to scrap it.
- C) Reread the notes from the meeting before deciding what changes. David said he didn't want the team to walk away thinking they were starting from scratch, and the specific ask was about weighing.
- D) Wait until the next regular check-in to ask David what he meant.

**Correct answer:** C

**Feedback if correct:** Right. The biggest risk after a redirect is overcorrecting based on the emotional weight of the meeting rather than the actual content. Going back to the notes — especially the exact quote — anchors the conversation in what was said. Decisions like "scrap the framework" or "keep the framework" are both premature without revisiting the ask, which was about weighting, not scrapping.

**Feedback if incorrect:**
- A) David explicitly said the opposite — "I don't want you to walk away thinking we're starting from scratch." Overcorrecting based on the team's reaction to feedback, rather than what was said, is one of the most common ways a redirect gets mishandled. The post-meeting debrief is where this can be caught.
- B) Disagreeing is just as premature as agreeing. David also named what worries him — recommendations built mostly from outside literature that don't reflect what the educators know works on the ground. That's a real concern the team needs to address. Going straight to "we don't have to change anything" misreads the feedback.
- D) Waiting two weeks to clarify lets the team's interpretation harden. If you genuinely need clarification, follow up sooner — a short written message within 24 hours is the standard. But before you reach out, check whether your notes already answer the question.

### Question 5

**Question text:** What's the most useful thing for your team's Direction Check Summary to capture about this exchange?

**Type:** multiple-choice

**Options:**
- A) The employer disagreed with our research approach and we'll need to revise our project plan.
- B) The employer asked us to demonstrate how we're weighting the community health educators' knowledge against the published literature. We'll follow up to understand what access we have to that knowledge.
- C) The employer wants us to conduct interviews with the community health educators before continuing with the research.
- D) The employer raised concerns but we're confident our framework is still appropriate. No major changes needed.

**Correct answer:** B

**Feedback if correct:** Good. The Summary captures the specific ask (weighting), the open question (what access do we have), and the next step (follow-up). It doesn't overstate (a pivot to interviews isn't yet confirmed), doesn't understate (this is a real redirect that affects the project), and doesn't paraphrase the feedback into something more comfortable. The Summary is the record your team will execute against — soft language now means soft direction later.

**Feedback if incorrect:**
- A) This captures the emotional shape of the feedback ("disagreed") but loses the specific ask. A Summary that says "we'll revise our project plan" without naming what's changing leaves the team without a shared record of what was actually decided.
- C) David didn't ask for interviews specifically — he asked about weighting. Documenting interviews as the agreed direction would commit the team to something that wasn't agreed, and if the employer sees the Summary later, the gap between their feedback and your record will be visible.
- D) Minimizing the feedback to preserve the team's existing plan is a common but serious mistake. David raised a substantive concern about whose knowledge counts as evidence. A Summary that says "no major changes needed" understates that, and the next phase of work will be built on a shaky foundation.

## Debrief

That scenario covered the full arc of a Direction Check redirect: hearing what was actually said, choosing a constructive first response, capturing the right language, processing the feedback as a team, and writing it up honestly.

The pattern to watch for in your own Direction Check is the gap between what the employer said and how it lands emotionally. Redirects feel destabilizing in the moment, and the team's instinct is often to either defend (no, our approach is right) or overcorrect (you're right, we'll scrap everything). The professional move is in between: ask, capture, debrief, decide — in that order.

You'll practice this for real in the meeting itself, then again when you write the Direction Check Summary on the next pages.
