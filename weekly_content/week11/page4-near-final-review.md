# Reviewing your near-final package

This meeting is the second document-sharing checkpoint in the course. Your team submitted the near-final package through the platform, and the meeting is where you walk the employer through it, surface the questions you want their read on, and get the last major directional input before the final submission.

If the project has been going well, this meeting should feel light. You've been checking in with the employer for weeks. The big decisions have been on the table before. There shouldn't be surprises in the package that aren't already in the conversation.

---

## What this meeting is for

The meeting has three parts, all driven by the team.

**Walking through what's in the package.** The employer may or may not have read closely. Some will have skimmed. Some won't have opened the package before the meeting. A short, structured walkthrough works in all cases: here's what we submitted, here's how it's laid out, here's what changed since the last check-in. Five minutes or so. It gives the employer a reason to engage and frames the rest of the conversation.

**Getting targeted input on open questions.** Your team has been making decisions across weeks of production. Some the team is confident about; some the team made on incomplete information and wants a sanity check on. The middle of the meeting is for putting those in front of the employer as specific questions. Not "what do you think?" but "we scoped the competitive analysis shallow in favor of deeper stakeholder mapping. Does the competitive section give you enough to work with, or is that a gap?"

**Hearing anything the employer raises on their own.** Whatever the employer has read or reacted to shows up here. Some meetings this is detailed; others it's general. Either way, it's input the team factors into the final push.

Emphasis shifts meeting to meeting depending on how much the employer engaged with the package. The team drives regardless.

This meeting is also the last structured moment of support before the final push. The package is completion-based for a reason: the goal isn't to grade where the team is, it's to give the employer a chance to tell you where the work needs more, and your instructor a chance to see the same submission through the platform and follow up with the team on anything that needs it. If a section is behind, a piece of scope still needs a nudge, or the team is uncertain about a call, this is the window where that can be addressed.

## If the employer hasn't read the package

This is the most common scenario outside of highly structured agency settings. Plan for it.

[AUDIO: A team walkthrough in practice]

The audio above is a team meeting with an employer who has skimmed but not read in depth. Listen for the opening agenda-setting move, how the Change Log summary connects prior feedback to what's in the package, and how the team handles the employer's mid-walkthrough observation without getting thrown.

The move modeled in the audio is the same one a consultant would make: structured walkthrough first, specific questions second. Don't assume the employer read the document and ask questions that only land if they did. Don't try to re-explain the entire package either. Walk the high points, stop at the sections where you have specific questions, and ask those questions directly while the content is fresh.

A useful sequence:

- Two minutes of orientation: here's the shape of the package, here's what's in each section
- The team's most pressing questions, one at a time, with the relevant section on screen or in view
- Open time at the end for any reaction the employer formed during the walkthrough

A skimmed or unopened package doesn't usually mean the employer is disengaged. It often means they're busy and the document sat in their queue. Once the team puts specific questions in front of them, they usually engage well. The structured walkthrough creates the engagement that wasn't there when the document was closed.

---

## If the employer has read closely

This is the more structured version. The employer brings their own list of things that landed, things that didn't, and things they want the team to reconsider. The walkthrough compresses because the employer already knows the shape. The middle of the meeting becomes two-directional: the employer's observations and the team's targeted questions in one conversation.

The Change Log summary is particularly valuable here. An employer who read closely and has comments will connect your team's explanation of what you did with prior feedback to what they're seeing now. "We heard your concern about segment three at the last check-in and added implementation detail in section five" is the kind of sentence that closes loops and signals that the team has been building in response to input.

Either way, the team drives. The employer's depth of reading changes the balance of the conversation, not the team's job in it.

## Preparing

### Run the internal review gate first

Before the employer reads the package, your team should read it. The internal review gate you've run before applies here: is the package clean, navigable, and self-explanatory? Does it look like something your team would be comfortable putting your names on? The Quality and Craft module covers what to look for and the self-review checklist to work through.

Do this before submitting the Near-Final Package, not after. The employer reads what you submit.

### Prepare the walkthrough and Change Log summary

Decide who on the team will walk the employer through the package. Pick two or three highlights for the opening: the key findings, the structure of the package, what's changed since the last check-in. Five minutes of this sets up the rest of the meeting regardless of how much the employer read ahead of time.

The Change Log summary is part of that opening. The structure is: here's the feedback we received at recent check-ins, here's what we did with it, here's where it shows up. You don't need to walk through every entry. A few representative ones are enough to signal that the team took prior feedback seriously.

Be ready to say explicitly where you didn't act on something and why. That's part of the conversation too. Employers tend to respond well to teams that can say "we heard this, we considered it, and here's what we decided" rather than teams that silently made choices the employer has to guess at.

### Develop targeted questions

The questions to bring to the near-final review are different from the questions you brought to working sessions. At those meetings, you were asking for input on decisions in progress. Here, you've largely made those decisions. The questions at this stage are about completeness and quality judgment.

Useful questions to develop:

- Where the team made a judgment call with limited information, and you want the employer's view on whether the call was reasonable. For example: "We prioritized the competitive analysis over the stakeholder mapping because we ran short on time. Does the competitive section give you enough to work with, or is the stakeholder piece a gap?"
- Anywhere the package makes a recommendation that assumes something about the employer's context, and you want to confirm the assumption holds
- Sections where the team is uncertain whether the depth of analysis is sufficient for the employer's purposes. For example: "We're least confident in the implementation section. Does the level of detail there feel actionable for your team, or does it need more specificity?"
- Any gap in the deliverable that the team identified in the internal review gate but didn't have time to fully address before submission

If your team has a question that amounts to "should we do X or Y?" (a decision your team hasn't made yet), make the call first, then bring it to the employer as a check: "we decided to do X; does anything about your context make that the wrong call?" That's still a real question, and it's a much more productive one. The employer responds to specific proposals more usefully than to open choices.

The same logic applies to open-ended questions like "what do you think of the recommendations?" Reframe them before the meeting: "we're least confident in section 3. Does the depth of analysis there meet what you actually need?" Specific questions get specific answers.

### Use AI to anticipate the employer's questions

Before the meeting, try the same approach your team has used at prior check-ins: ask AI to generate the questions an employer is likely to raise after reading a near-final version of your deliverable. Paste the relevant sections and ask what a skeptical reviewer would want to push back on. What you get back is a list to examine, not a script to memorize. Anywhere AI generates a question your team isn't confident answering, that's worth preparing for.

## During the meeting

**Suggested time breakdown for approximately 30 minutes:**

- Walkthrough and Change Log summary (about 5-7 minutes): Open with the shape of the package and a quick summary of what the team did with recent feedback.
- Targeted questions (about 15-18 minutes): Work through the questions your team prepared. The employer's responses are the primary output of the meeting.
- Employer observations (about 5-8 minutes): Leave time for the employer to raise anything the package prompted that your questions didn't cover. If they read closely, this window grows. If they skimmed, it may be shorter.

**Capturing notes during the meeting**

Designate one team member to take notes on everything the employer says, including observations that don't seem to connect to your questions directly. Feedback that sounds general in the moment often becomes specific when you're reading your notes later.

When the employer says something ambiguous, ask a follow-up before the meeting ends. "When you say the recommendations feel high-level, is that about the level of specificity, or about the feasibility information we've included?" is a better use of meeting time than spending the debrief guessing what they meant.

---

## After the meeting

Debrief as a team immediately after the meeting ends, while the conversation is still fresh. Review the notes and agree on what you heard. The interpretation of feedback tends to diverge more than teams expect when they compare notes later.

Update the Change Log before the team disperses. Add each piece of feedback from the meeting with a note about the decision your team made in response. This entry will be the most recent one in the Change Log by the time you submit the final deliverable.

Translate the feedback from the meeting into a concrete revision priority list before ending the debrief. What changes before the final submission, who owns each one, and what's the target for completion? The window between this meeting and the final submission is shorter than it looks. Teams that leave the debrief with a shared list of named priorities use that window well. Teams that leave with a general sense of what needs to improve tend to find that things are still in progress at the end.
