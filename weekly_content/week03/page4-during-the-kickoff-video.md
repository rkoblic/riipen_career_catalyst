# Video Script: What actually happens in a kickoff meeting

If you've never been in a professional kickoff meeting before, here's what to expect, and what the people on the other side of the call are expecting from you.

You own this meeting. You made that clear when you sent the agenda. So you open it: welcome everyone, thank them for being there, and start introductions. Ask the employer to go first. Something like "we'd love to start by hearing from your team." That lets you hear how they frame the project before you speak.

When it's your turn, keep it short. Name, program, one sentence about what you're looking forward to. Fifteen seconds, max. If four people each take two minutes, you've burned eight minutes on introductions alone, and that's eight minutes you don't have for the actual conversation.

After introductions, ask the employer to walk through the project. Something like "we've read the brief and are excited to work on this with you, we'd love to hear more about it in your own words." They'll describe their organization, the problem they want you to work on, what success looks like, the timeline, and who you'll be interacting with on their side.

Your job during this part is to listen. You're listening for what they emphasize, what they repeat, what they seem most invested in. You're also listening for the gaps: what they don't mention, where the brief and the conversation don't quite match, what seems unclear or assumed.

Here's the part most teams get wrong. They treat their question list like a script. They wait for the employer to stop talking, then go down the list one by one. The problem is the employer may have already answered half your questions during their overview. If you ask something they just spent three minutes explaining, it signals you weren't listening.

Instead, track your questions as the employer talks. Cross off the ones they've answered. Adjust the ones that need rephrasing based on what you've heard. When it's your turn to ask, lead with what's still open. Follow-up questions are often more valuable than prepared ones because they show you were processing the conversation in real time.

One more thing. Pay attention to tone and emphasis, not just content. When an employer says "this is really important to us" or circles back to a topic multiple times, that's a signal. The things they emphasize are the things they'll evaluate you on, whether the rubric says so or not. When they hedge or seem uncertain about something, that's a signal too. It might be an unknown on their side, and knowing that early helps you plan.

The teams that do well in kickoffs aren't the ones who ask perfect questions. They're the ones who listen well enough to understand what the employer actually needs, even when that's not exactly what's written in the brief.
