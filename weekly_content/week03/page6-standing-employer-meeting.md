# Establishing the standing meeting

The kickoff is a one-time event. Most of the work you do with the employer happens between meetings — but you still need a regular touchpoint to keep the project on track. Setting up a recurring meeting now, while the kickoff is fresh, is one of the highest-leverage moves your team can make this week.

This page covers how to lock in that meeting at the kickoff, what to do afterward, and the norms that keep these meetings productive.

---

## Why your team needs a standing meeting

A recurring meeting is how you de-risk a project. Without one, communication tends to drift: questions pile up, small misalignments compound, and by the time anyone notices something is off, the team has spent weeks heading the wrong direction. With one, you have a built-in checkpoint to surface decisions, flag blockers, and keep the employer engaged in the work.

The cadence we recommend is **30 minutes, every other week** — short enough that it doesn't burden a busy professional, frequent enough that nothing important sits stale for long. The kickoff (60 minutes) is the exception. From here, your meetings get shorter and more focused.

---

## Lock it in at the kickoff

The best time to schedule a recurring meeting is at the end of the kickoff, while everyone is already on the call. Don't leave it to a follow-up email if you can avoid it — recurring meetings tend to slip when scheduling becomes its own back-and-forth.

In the last few minutes of the kickoff, raise it directly:

> "Before we wrap, we'd like to set up a recurring 30-minute check-in every other week so we can keep you posted and pull you in on decisions as they come up. Would [day/time] work as a standing slot?"

Have a default proposal ready: a specific day and time that works for everyone on your team. Offering "what works for you?" without a starting point usually leads to a longer conversation. If your first proposal doesn't work, offer two alternatives.

If the meeting runs long and you don't get to it, raise it in your follow-up message. Don't let the week end without a confirmed time.

---

## Send the calendar hold

Within 24 hours of the kickoff, send the recurring calendar invite. This is the team's responsibility, not the employer's.

A good calendar invite includes:

- **Title:** Specific enough that the employer can identify it at a glance — for example, "[Project name] / Riipen team biweekly check-in."
- **Recurrence:** Every other week, on the agreed-upon day and time, with a clear end date (the last week of the course).
- **Video link:** Use the same platform as the kickoff unless the employer has indicated a preference for something else.
- **Description:** One or two sentences on what these meetings are for — brief status updates, blockers, decisions needed. This sets expectations for the employer every time the invite shows up on their calendar.

Send the invite to all team members and to the employer contact. If the employer has identified more than one stakeholder, ask in your follow-up message who else should be on the recurring invite.

---

## What these meetings are for

These standing meetings are structurally different from the kickoff. They are not listening sessions, and the employer is not the main speaker.

A 30-minute biweekly meeting is your team's working session with the employer. The agenda is short:

- **Brief status update.** Five minutes max. What was completed since last time, what's underway, what's on deck.
- **Decisions or input needed.** Specific questions where the employer's judgment unblocks your team.
- **Blockers.** Anything where the employer's involvement — an introduction, access to data, a stakeholder pull-in — would help.
- **Open discussion.** Anything the employer wants to raise.

These meetings are also distinct from the **internal check-ins** your team has with each other. Internal check-ins are about coordination among yourselves; the standing employer meeting is about keeping the client engaged and informed. Both matter, and they don't replace each other.

---

## Professional meeting norms

A few baseline norms make these meetings work for everyone. None of these are unusual in a professional setting; what's unusual is teams that don't follow them.

- **Send the agenda at least 24 hours ahead.** Even a three-line bulleted list is enough. The employer should know what's on the table before they show up.
- **Start and end on time.** Beginning four minutes late and running seven minutes long communicates that you don't respect the employer's calendar. If you're the host, open the meeting room a couple of minutes early.
- **Designate a notetaker.** One person is responsible for capturing decisions and action items. Rotate the role across meetings so it doesn't always fall on the same person.
- **Capture action items in real time.** Every action item needs an owner and a due date. "We'll look into that" is not an action item. "Maya will share the user research notes by Friday" is.
- **Send a brief follow-up summary.** Within 24 hours, send the employer a short message summarizing decisions and action items from the meeting. This is the same pattern you used after the kickoff, applied at a smaller scale every time.

## Between meetings: async updates

On the off-week — the week with no scheduled meeting — send the employer a short written update through the Riipen platform. This keeps them in the loop without adding to their meeting load.

A good async update is brief: three or four bullets covering what your team completed, what's underway, and any open questions or things you'd appreciate input on. You don't need a response. The point is visibility. An employer who sees consistent async updates feels confident the project is moving even if they've barely thought about it that week.

## When meetings get skipped or rescheduled

Professional schedules slip. Your employer contact will sometimes need to reschedule, and occasionally a meeting will be cancelled outright. This is normal and not cause for alarm.

When it happens, here's how to handle it:

- **If the employer reschedules,** offer two or three alternate times in the same week or the week after. Don't let the gap stretch to a month.
- **If a meeting can't happen at all,** convert it into an async update. Send the brief written summary you'd have used in the off-week, plus any decisions or questions that needed the meeting. This keeps the project moving and signals that your team can adapt.
- **Don't let two cycles go silent.** If you've gone four weeks without either a meeting or an async exchange, something has gone off-track. Treat that as a signal, not an inconvenience.

---

## When to escalate to your instructor

Your instructor is a support resource, not a referee. Most communication friction with an employer is normal and resolves itself through the patterns above. But there are a few situations where you should bring your instructor in early rather than wait:

- **Two consecutive missed meetings with no async substitute.** Not a couple of reschedules — that happens. This is the employer going dark for a full month.
- **A sustained pattern of unanswered messages,** where more than a week passes with no response to a clearly worded question that the project depends on.
- **A change in the employer's situation** that materially affects the project — new contact, organizational shift, or a scope change your team can't reasonably handle on its own.

Reaching out to your instructor is not a complaint. It's the same pattern you'd use in a job: when something is blocking your team and the path forward isn't obvious, you raise it with someone who has more context. Faster is better than later.
