# Continuing your research

The research you do this week has a specific purpose: it feeds directly into the Context Analysis & Project Plan, your first graded team deliverable. That assignment asks you to analyze the landscape your project exists in and propose a clear direction based on evidence. The stronger your research base coming out of this week, the less backfilling you'll need to do when the deliverable is due.

## Targeted landscape research

[VIDEO: How to focus research that could go on forever]

Earlier in the course, your research on the employer's organization was broad and orienting. You were building general familiarity without knowing the specific project scope. Now that the kickoff has confirmed what you're actually working on, your research can be targeted.

This is the second stage of a three-part progression: your initial reconnaissance gave you a foundation, this stage focuses that foundation on the confirmed project scope, and the next stage is analytical synthesis where you draw conclusions for the Context Analysis. Right now, you're gathering and organizing, not yet analyzing.

The video walks through how to turn what you heard in the kickoff into focused research questions. As a quick reference, your research this week should cover three areas:

- **The broader landscape.** What's happening in the space your project lives in. Depending on the project, that might be a market or industry, a policy area, a community, or a field of practice. Enough context to make recommendations that aren't naive about the environment your employer operates in.
- **Similar initiatives.** Case studies, program evaluations, published reports, or examples of organizations that have tackled a similar problem or opportunity. What worked, what didn't, what you can learn from their experience.
- **The people your work affects.** If the employer mentioned specific audiences, users, clients, or communities, build a working understanding of who they are, what they need, what barriers they face, and what others have gotten wrong serving them.

Organize your findings by theme rather than by source as you go. Grouping by topic (trends, similar initiatives, audience characteristics, constraints) makes it far easier to synthesize later than a chronological list of everything you read.

If you can't tie a research question back to a sentence the employer said in the kickoff, you're probably researching the topic, not the project. Focused questions take something specific the employer said and turn it into something you can search, read, and decide from.

AI is useful for getting oriented in an unfamiliar field, especially if you're researching a sector you've never encountered. Ask it to explain how the space works, what key terminology means, or what major trends are. For specific facts (organizations, data, recent developments), go to primary sources — AI's training data has a cutoff, and in a research context where recency matters, you need current information.
