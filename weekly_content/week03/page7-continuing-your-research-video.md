# Video Script: How to focus research that could go on forever

Most teams start landscape research the same way and get the same result: a hundred bookmarks, three days gone, no clearer sense of what to recommend. The problem isn't effort. It's that the research question was too broad to answer.

Focused research starts with a question only your employer's project could answer. Three examples.

A nonprofit working on health outcomes in immigrant communities. The unfocused version of that research is something like "research public health." You'd read for three days and get nowhere useful. The focused version comes from what the employer told you in the kickoff. Maybe it's "what approaches have worked for health behavior change in culturally diverse populations, and what have similar organizations tried in this space." Same topic, completely different research session. One ends in a Wikipedia rabbit hole. The other ends with two or three case studies you can cite.

Take a different project. An employer wants you to redesign their volunteer onboarding. The lazy version of the research is "best practices for nonprofits." The focused version is "what onboarding looks like at organizations that retain volunteers past six months, and what causes drop-off in the first month." Now you know what to look for.

One more. An employer wants a strategy for reaching first-generation college students. Searching "research first-gen students" gives you a textbook overview. Searching "what messaging and channels have moved this audience for organizations doing similar work, and what assumptions has the field gotten wrong" gives you something you can actually use. That last clause is the one that turns research into recommendations.

Each focused question takes something specific the employer said and turns it into something you can search, read, and decide from. If you can't tie a research question back to a sentence the employer said in the kickoff, you're probably researching the topic, not the project.

Two practical notes before you start. AI is a fast way to get oriented in an unfamiliar field, but it gives you plausible overviews, not citable sources. Use it to learn what to search for, then go to primary sources for specific facts. And organize what you find by theme as you go, not by source. When you sit down to write, you want categories, not a stack of bookmarks.

Stop when you can describe the landscape, point to relevant examples, and talk about the people your work affects with specificity. That's enough.
