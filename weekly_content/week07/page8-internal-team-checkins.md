# Internal team check-ins

Most professional teams have a rhythm of checking in with each other, separate from any meetings with clients or stakeholders. Some teams do it daily in a 10-minute standup. Others do it once a week in a longer working session. Some do it asynchronously in a shared channel. The format varies, but the purpose is consistent: keep the team aligned on what's getting done, surface blockers early, and make sure no one is working in the dark on something a teammate could help with.

Your team has been doing some version of this informally throughout the project. Starting this week, the course adds a structured prompt to make the practice deliberate and to keep going as the project moves into its production-heavy weeks.

---

## Why teams check in with each other

When several people are working on different parts of the same deliverable, the work tends to drift in predictable ways. Someone makes a decision the rest of the team would have weighed in on. Two people unknowingly produce overlapping work. A blocker that one teammate could have unblocked sits for days because nobody mentioned it. Regular internal check-ins exist to catch all of this before it costs the team time.

There's a second reason that matters more as production picks up: a project plan written at the start of a project gets stale fast. Reality diverges from the plan within days. Teams that revisit the plan against actual progress at a regular cadence tend to keep moving. Teams that treat the plan as a one-time artifact usually discover the gap between plan and reality late, when there's less time to recover.

---

## Common check-in formats

There's no single right format. A few that show up often in real teams:

- **Daily standup.** A short sync, usually 10 to 15 minutes, where each person says what they did yesterday, what they're doing today, and what's blocking them. The "standing" comes from the original idea that the meeting should be uncomfortable enough to keep short.
- **Weekly working check-in.** A longer sync once a week, usually 30 to 45 minutes. Covers progress, blockers, decisions to make, and what each person owns for the coming week. More substantive than a standup.
- **Async written check-in.** A structured update each team member posts to a shared channel or document, often weekly. Useful when team members are in different time zones or when schedules don't line up. Works only if everyone actually reads each other's updates.

Most teams blend formats: brief async updates between meetings, plus a longer working sync once or twice a week. What matters is consistency, and that the check-in is short enough that people actually do it.

---

## What makes a check-in actually useful

A check-in that produces useful information has a few habits behind it.

**Specificity over summary.** "We made progress on the report" is the kind of update that makes meetings feel like a waste of time. "I drafted the competitive landscape section and got stuck on whether to include two regional competitors" is the kind that lets a teammate respond. The harder a check-in is to skim past, the more useful it is.

**Honesty about what's stuck.** The whole point of surfacing blockers is to get them unstuck. A team where nobody ever reports being blocked is either extraordinary or not telling the truth, and the second is much more common. Naming the blocker is what makes the check-in worth doing.

**Action-oriented closing.** Every check-in should end with someone clearer on what they own next. If the meeting ends and nobody is sure who's doing what by when, the check-in didn't do its job.

**Equal airtime.** Watch for the pattern where one or two people dominate every check-in and the rest go quiet. Quiet teammates often have the information you need most. A round-robin format helps. So does asking direct questions to people who haven't spoken.

## The Weekly Progress Check-In

Starting this week and continuing through the rest of the course, try using a structured weekly prompt when checking in as a team. Use it once a week, capture the answers somewhere your whole team can see them, and treat it as a living record of where the project actually stands.

- **What got done this week?** Be specific. Name the sections drafted, the decisions made, the work completed.
- **What moved since last week?** What progressed, even if it isn't completed? This catches the work that's underway but invisible.
- **What's blocked?** What can't move forward until something else happens? Who or what is the blocker, and what would it take to unblock it?
- **Who owns what this week?** Name specific tasks and owners for the coming week.

The four prompts work as a sync conversation, an async written update, or a quick post in a team channel. Pick the format that fits your team's working style and stick with it. Consistency matters more than which format you choose.

---

## Why this is worth doing well

The Weekly Progress Check-In is not graded. The reason to do it carefully isn't compliance, it's that the check-in is the cheapest way to keep your team's project plan a living document rather than something you wrote earlier and haven't looked at since. Teams that check in consistently catch problems while they're still small and stay clearer on what each person is responsible for.

It also builds something tangible: a written record of what each team member contributed across the project. That record matters for the peer evaluations coming up. Teams that have been checking in consistently have specific, dated evidence of each member's contribution to draw on. Teams that haven't are working from general impressions, which are harder to write up and easier to disagree about.
