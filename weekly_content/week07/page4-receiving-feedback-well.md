# Receiving feedback well

This week you'll be on both sides of peer review. The previous page covered how to give it; this one covers how to take it.

Two flavors of feedback come at you this week. Your teammates will likely leave most of their review comments asynchronously, in doc comments and Slack messages. Your employer's feedback at Development Check-In #1 will come live in conversation. The habits that help you receive each are slightly different, and the sections below cover both.

---

## Why this is harder than it sounds

Receiving feedback on something you've spent real time building is uncomfortable. The instinct when someone evaluates your work is to defend or deflect, to manage how you're being perceived rather than to hear what's being said. That instinct is understandable, but it gets in the way.

A defensive response closes off the information you need. When a teammate says "this section's main point isn't landing for me," or an employer says "this framing doesn't quite match what we were looking for," they're handing you a course correction. If your response is to justify the choice you made, you may walk away having heard the feedback without taking it in. The feedback then quietly costs you twice: once because you didn't use it, and once because the person who gave it now knows you're hard to give feedback to.

The protocol below won't make the discomfort go away. It creates a structure that keeps you listening when the instinct is to argue.

---

## Receiving feedback in conversation: Listen → Clarify → Acknowledge → Decide later

When feedback comes in real time, like in a meeting or an exchange after a working session, you don't get to step away and process before responding. The protocol below is for that case. It's a structure for staying in listening mode when the instinct is to argue.

**Listen.** Hear the feedback before you respond to it. Don't start formulating your reply while the other person is still talking. If you catch yourself thinking "but we chose that because...," pause and listen to the end. Most feedback gets more specific or more nuanced as the person keeps explaining, and the version you would have argued with after the first sentence often isn't the version they actually meant.

**Clarify.** Before reacting, make sure you understand what's being said. "Can you say more about what you mean by framing?" or "Is the issue with the scope, the approach, or how we've communicated it?" Questions like these show you're taking the feedback seriously and they give you information to work with. Asking a clarifying question is not the same as pushing back on the feedback. You're trying to understand it more precisely.

**Acknowledge.** Name what you heard. "So the concern is that our recommendation doesn't address the operational constraints you mentioned earlier" tells the other person you understood them and gives them the chance to confirm or correct. Acknowledging is not the same as agreeing. You're confirming that the message landed, not committing to act on it.

**Decide later.** Don't commit to a response or a course of action in the moment. You don't have to agree with every piece of feedback, and you don't have to decide what to do with it on the spot. Your job during the conversation is to hear and understand. The decision about what to do with the feedback happens after, with your team or on your own, once you have time to weigh it against everything else you know about the work.

## Receiving feedback asynchronously

A lot of the feedback you'll get on your work won't arrive in person. It will show up as a comment on a document or a message in Slack. Async feedback has one big advantage over face-to-face feedback: there's a built-in gap between when you read it and when you respond. That gap is the most useful thing about it. Use it.

It also has one big disadvantage: written feedback strips out tone and facial expression. You only have the words, and your brain will fill in the rest. The same comment can read as helpful or dismissive depending on what mood you're in when you read it, and the version you imagine is rarely the version the person meant.

A few habits help:

**Don't read tone into text that isn't there.** A short comment isn't a curt one. "This doesn't work for me" written in a doc comment is almost never as cold as it sounds in your head. The person typing it was likely in a hurry, not annoyed. Assume the most generous version of what was written, the way you'd want someone to read your own quick comments.

**Question your reaction before you act on it.** If a comment makes you bristle, that's information, but it's information about you, not about the comment. Notice the reaction, then ask whether the same point would have landed differently in a meeting. Often the answer is yes, and the feedback itself is fine. It was the medium that stung. Separating those two things keeps you from arguing with a teammate who didn't say what you reacted to.

**Use the gap.** You don't owe an immediate reply to a comment or a Slack message. Read it, then sit with it for a few hours or overnight before you respond. Almost everything you would have written in the first ten minutes will read as overcooked the next morning. The clarify and acknowledge moves from the protocol above still apply. They just happen in writing, on your timeline.

**Use AI to help you process, carefully.** If a piece of written feedback is bothering you and you can't tell why, it can help to talk it through with an AI tool before you respond. Paste in the comment along with what you were trying to do, and ask the model to help you separate the substance of the feedback from your reaction to it, or to draft a clarifying question you could ask the person who wrote it. This is processing, not outsourcing. The goal is to get yourself to a place where you can respond clearly, not to have AI write the response for you. Anything you send back should still be in your own words.

## When feedback styles differ

Norms around how feedback gets delivered vary widely across cultures, industries, and workplaces. Some teams default to direct, immediate critique as a sign of respect for your time. Others build in much more cushioning, expecting you to read the substance between the lines. Some workplaces voice disagreement openly in meetings; others raise it privately afterward. None of these are wrong, but the gap between two styles can lead to misreadings: a learner used to indirect feedback can hear directness as harshness, and a learner used to directness can mistake warmth for approval.

The protocol is designed to be portable across these differences because it focuses on what you do as the receiver, not on how the other person delivers their input. Listening, clarifying, and acknowledging work whether the feedback comes wrapped in qualifiers or stripped down to a single sentence. If you're unsure how to read what was said, the clarify step is the tool: "I want to make sure I understand the priority here. Is this something you'd like us to change before next week, or a longer-term consideration?" That kind of question moves past the delivery style and gets at the substance.

---

## When feedback isn't useful

Receiving feedback well does not mean accepting every piece of it. Some feedback is based on a misunderstanding of the work, or reflects a preference that won't land the project where it needs to go. Some is too vague to act on, or is more about the person than the work. Research on workplace feedback finds that learners and professionals from underrepresented groups receive vaguer, more personality-focused feedback than their peers — comments on "tone" or "presence" without anything specific to change. That kind of feedback is harder to use, and it's worth recognizing when you're getting it.

The clarify step is your best tool here. A question like "Can you point me to a specific moment where you noticed that?" or "What would a stronger version of this look like to you?" turns vague feedback into something concrete, or surfaces that there isn't anything concrete behind it. The decide-later step gives you the space to weigh feedback against everything else you know about the work, including other input, the brief, and your team's judgment.

What the protocol asks of you in the moment is restraint, not agreement. The cost of waiting to respond is small. Arguing in the moment, by contrast, loses you the information the other person was trying to give you, and makes it harder for them to bring you the next thing.
