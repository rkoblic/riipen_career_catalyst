# Receiving feedback well

Every professional who produces work for someone else gets feedback on it. From a manager on a draft proposal, from a colleague on a slide they're presenting tomorrow, from a client on a recommendation that misses what they actually needed. The skill of taking that feedback in without immediately defending against it is one of the more reliable predictors of how quickly someone grows in a role. It's also one of the harder things to do well.

This page covers how to receive feedback in a way that keeps you learning from it. The protocol applies in two places this week, and in many more across the rest of the course and your career: when a teammate reads your draft and tells you what's not working, and when your employer responds to the work your team brings to a check-in meeting. Both situations call for the same set of habits.

---

## Why this is harder than it sounds

Receiving feedback on something you've spent real time building is genuinely uncomfortable. The instinct when someone evaluates your work is to explain, defend, or deflect, to manage how you're being perceived rather than to hear what's being said. That instinct is human and understandable. It's also counterproductive.

A defensive response closes off the information you need. When a teammate says "this section's main point isn't landing for me," or an employer says "this framing doesn't quite match what we were looking for," they're handing you a course correction. If your response is to justify the choice you made, you may walk away having technically heard the feedback without actually receiving it. The feedback then quietly costs you twice: once because you didn't use it, and once because the person who gave it now knows you don't really take input.

The protocol below won't make the discomfort go away. It creates a structure that keeps you listening when the instinct is to argue.

---

## The reception protocol: Listen → Clarify → Acknowledge → Decide later

**Listen.** Hear the feedback before you respond to it. Don't start formulating your reply while the other person is still talking. If you catch yourself thinking "but we chose that because...," pause and listen to the end. Most feedback gets more specific or more nuanced as the person keeps explaining, and the version you would have argued with after the first sentence often isn't the version they actually meant.

**Clarify.** Before reacting, make sure you understand what's being said. "Can you say more about what you mean by framing?" or "Is the issue with the scope, the approach, or how we've communicated it?" Questions like these show you're taking the feedback seriously and they give you actual information to work with. Asking a clarifying question is not the same as pushing back on the feedback. You're trying to understand it more precisely.

**Acknowledge.** Name what you heard. "So the concern is that our recommendation doesn't address the operational constraints you mentioned earlier" tells the other person you understood them and gives them the chance to confirm or correct. Acknowledging is not the same as agreeing. You're confirming that the message landed, not committing to act on it.

**Decide later.** Don't commit to a response or a course of action in the moment. You don't have to agree with every piece of feedback, and you don't have to decide what to do with it on the spot. Your job during the conversation is to hear and understand. The decision about what to do with the feedback happens after, with your team or on your own, once you have time to weigh it against everything else you know about the work.

## When feedback styles differ

Norms around how feedback gets delivered vary widely across cultures, industries, and workplaces. Some teams default to direct, immediate critique as a sign of respect for your time. Others build in significantly more cushioning, expecting you to read the substance between the lines. Some workplaces voice disagreement openly in meetings; others raise it privately afterward. None of these are wrong, but the gap between two styles can lead to misreadings: a learner used to indirect feedback can hear directness as harshness, and a learner used to directness can mistake warmth for approval.

The protocol is designed to be portable across these differences because it focuses on what you do as the receiver, not on how the other person delivers their input. Listening, clarifying, and acknowledging work whether the feedback comes wrapped in qualifiers or stripped down to a single sentence. If you're unsure how to read what was said, the clarify step is the tool: "I want to make sure I understand the priority here. Is this something you'd like us to change before next week, or a longer-term consideration?" That kind of question moves past the delivery style and gets at the substance.

---

## When feedback isn't useful

Receiving feedback well does not mean accepting every piece of it. Some feedback is based on a misunderstanding of the work. Some reflects a preference that won't land the project where it needs to go. Some is too vague to act on, and some is about the person rather than the work. Research on workplace feedback consistently finds that learners and professionals from underrepresented groups receive vaguer, more personality-focused feedback than their peers — feedback that comments on "tone" or "presence" without identifying anything specific to change. That kind of feedback is harder to use, and it's worth recognizing when you're getting it.

The clarify step is your best tool here. A question like "Can you point me to a specific moment where you noticed that?" or "What would a stronger version of this look like to you?" turns vague feedback into something concrete, or surfaces that there isn't anything concrete behind it. The decide-later step gives you the space to weigh feedback against everything else you know about the work, including other input, the brief, and your team's judgment.

What the protocol asks of you in the moment is restraint, not agreement. The cost of waiting to respond is small. The cost of arguing in the moment is that you lose the information the other person was trying to give you, and you make it harder for them to bring you the next thing.
