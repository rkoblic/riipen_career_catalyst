# The Work-in-Progress Summary

Before any meeting that matters, project teams write things down. Not always for the client to read. Often for the team itself, to make sure everyone is aligned on where the work actually stands and what they're going to ask for. Project leads draft talking points before client calls. Consulting teams produce internal prep notes before stakeholder meetings. The act of getting the situation onto a page is what surfaces the gaps a team would otherwise walk into the meeting still carrying.

The Work-in-Progress Summary is your team's version of that practice. It is the checkpoint deliverable for this week, submitted before your Check-In #1 meeting. Its job is to make your team articulate its own situation in writing before the meeting, so that you walk in aligned with each other and clear on what you need.

---

## What this checkpoint is (and isn't)

The WIP Summary is a team submission, not an individual one. It is short, usually one to two pages. It is a working document for your team and the course. It is **not** a document the employer reads. The employer will not receive it, and you should not send it to them. It is not a pre-read, an agenda, or an artifact you'd put in front of a client.

The point of the writing is not to communicate to anyone outside your team. The point is the act of writing itself: putting your team's actual situation on the page is what makes the gaps visible. You then carry what you learned from that exercise into the meeting verbally, in your own words, in response to whatever the employer raises.

The submission is completion-based. It is verified, but not graded for quality. What "completion" means here is described in the criteria below.

---

## What goes in the summary

The WIP Summary has four sections. Each one exists for a reason that's about your team, not about the employer. Understanding those reasons will help you fill the summary in honestly rather than performatively.

### Completed work

Name specifically what your team has actually produced so far: sections drafted, research completed, decisions made, components built. The reason this section matters is that vague memory of progress is almost always rosier than reality. "We've been working on the report" turns into a much shorter list once you have to write down what's actually finished. Teams that go through this exercise routinely discover that less is done than anyone thought, which is exactly the thing you'd rather find out before walking into a meeting than during one.

### Current status

Where is the project right now? What's drafted, what's in progress, what hasn't been started yet? The value of writing this down is that it gives your team a shared picture before the meeting starts. Two people on the same team can have very different ideas about what state the work is in, and those differences are much better caught now, in writing, than discovered in real time when one teammate contradicts another in front of the employer.

### Challenges encountered

An honest account of blockers, gaps, and unexpected complications. This is the section most teams are tempted to soften or skip. It is also the section that does the most work for you. Blockers that don't get written down tend to either get forgotten in the meeting or hedged into invisibility once the conversation starts and the team is trying to project competence. Writing them down in advance makes them harder to drop. You walk in with the list in front of you, you know which ones you decided as a team to raise, and the ones you raise come out clearly because you already worked out how to describe them.

### Specific questions or feedback needs

What does your team need from the employer in order to move forward? Write the questions out precisely. The reason this matters is that the act of writing a question precisely is what reveals whether your team actually knows what it's asking. "We need feedback on our research" survives as a thought but collapses the moment you try to put it on the page. Trying to write it forces a more useful version: "We've drafted the competitive landscape section but we're unsure whether to include [Competitor X]. Should we treat them as a direct competitor?" That precision happens through revision, in your team, before the meeting. It almost never happens in real time when the employer asks "anything else?"

---

## What the completion criteria require

A summary that says everything is progressing smoothly and offers no specific questions does not meet the criteria. Not because there is a requirement to manufacture problems, but because every project at this stage has open questions and unresolved decisions. A summary without any is either not honest or not looking closely enough.

The criteria are:

- An honest assessment of current progress, including what is not done.
- At least one specific challenge or uncertainty.
- At least one targeted question for the employer.

A short summary that covers these three things genuinely is enough. Length is not the standard.

---

## Using AI on the summary

AI can help you organize and clarify the summary after your team has agreed on its substance. Restructuring for clarity, tightening language, making sure the four sections read in a logical order. Those are appropriate uses of AI as a production partner.

What AI cannot do is the part of the exercise that actually matters: the thinking and surfacing the writing is supposed to force. The whole point of putting your situation on the page is that the act of writing reveals what your team has been carrying around vaguely. If AI generates the words for you before your team has had the conversation, you skip the surfacing entirely and end up with a document that sounds plausible and tells you nothing new. Have the team conversation first. Write it down together. Then, if you want, hand the result to AI for cleanup.

---

## Submission

**Work-in-Progress Summary** — team submission, due before your Check-In #1 meeting.

Submit as a team before the meeting begins. The submission is a completion-based checkpoint: verified, not graded for quality. A summary submitted after the meeting has already happened cannot serve its purpose, since the purpose is to prepare your team for the conversation. Late submissions do not meet the standard.

**AI disclosure:** Required. If AI was used to assist with drafting or organizing the summary, include a one-sentence disclosure describing how. If AI was not used, include a one-sentence statement to that effect. The substance of the summary must come from your team's actual project status regardless of whether AI helped with language or structure.
