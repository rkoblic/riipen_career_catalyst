# Reviewing your teammates' work

You'll review pieces of your teammates' work this week. This page is the framework for doing that well: how to read a draft critically and how to give feedback your teammate can use.

Reading drafts well is also a core managerial habit, and it's become more valuable as AI tools produce more first drafts than ever. The same critical reading (does this hold up, does the evidence support what it's being used for) separates strong use of AI from blind trust in it.

[VIDEO: Structural feedback vs. line editing]

The video above walks through the difference between structural feedback and line editing, using two examples. The framework below is what to run through each time you review a teammate's piece, whether that's a section of a report, a slide in a deck, or another piece of the team's deliverable.

You'll do this kind of review more than once across the rest of the project, not just this week.

---

## Start with the big question

Before you zoom in on details, ask one thing: does this piece do what it's supposed to do?

Every section, slide, or component of your team's deliverable has a job. A research findings section has to give a client a clear picture of what your team learned. A recommendations section has to give them something they can act on. A competitive landscape section has to leave the reader knowing where the organization stands.

Read the piece once with that job in mind. If the answer is yes, even imperfectly, the four lenses below will help you sharpen it. If the answer is no, the feedback your teammate needs isn't line-level. It's a conversation about what the piece is for and how to refocus it before more time goes into polishing the wrong thing. That conversation is harder to have, and more valuable, than any number of margin comments.

---

## Four lenses for sharpening a piece

Once the piece is doing the right job, run it through four lenses to sharpen it, in this order. Structure first, language last.

**Clarity.** Can someone who doesn't know the project follow what's being said? Where do they have to work to understand it?

*What this looks like:* A process improvement team's section on workflow bottlenecks opens mid-analysis, assuming the reader already knows which workflow it's about. Or a slide in a marketing strategy deck shows three campaign metrics without naming what they're measuring. In both cases, a reader unfamiliar with the project can't follow it from the first line.

*What this does not mean:* Stripping out terminology a client will know, or flattening the substance. Clarity is about whether the piece carries its own context, not about dumbing it down.

**Argument structure.** Does the piece have a clear claim or takeaway? Does each piece of evidence connect to that claim, or does the reader have to make the connection themselves?

*What this looks like:* A social media strategy section presents three statistics about competitor engagement, then jumps straight to a recommendation. The connection between the data and the recommendation is never stated. The reader has to infer why those numbers justify the approach.

*What this does not mean:* That every section needs the same shape. A recommendation memo, a research findings section, and an executive summary are each structured differently. The question is whether the structure the piece has is doing work for the reader.

**Evidence quality.** Is the evidence specific and credible? Does it actually support the claim it's attached to?

*What this looks like:* A community outreach section recommends a peer ambassador program because "similar programs have shown positive results." No programs are named, no results cited. The recommendation reads as a guess, not a finding.

*What this does not mean:* A footnote on every sentence. Evidence quality is about whether the evidence does the job the claim asks of it, not about volume.

**Tone.** Is the language appropriate for a client audience? Watch for language that's too casual or too opinionated for a professional deliverable.

*What this looks like:* A process improvement section concludes that "the organization's approach to quality control is frankly outdated and reflects a lack of investment in modern practices." The assessment may be accurate, but the language editorializes. A client-facing version names the gap and its consequence without the editorializing.

*What this does not mean:* Tone policing. This isn't about whether your teammate sounds like you would, or about smoothing every voice on the team into the same one. Personal style is theirs. What you're checking is whether the language fits a piece a client will read.

## One more pass: catching AI-assisted errors

If your teammate used AI to help with any part of the piece (drafting, summarizing, pulling stats, generating examples), make one more pass with that in mind. AI tools produce confident-sounding errors, and the person who used the tool is often the worst person to catch them. The text reads as familiar, so the eye slides over the wrong things.

A few questions to bring to that pass:

- Are statistics, dates, and named sources real? If the piece cites a percentage, a study, or a named expert, can you confirm it in a quick search?
- Are quoted phrases accurate, or paraphrased into something the original source didn't actually say?
- Does any claim feel suspiciously specific? Fabricated details often sound more precise than the underlying knowledge would warrant.
- Are there generic phrases that could apply to any organization? AI sometimes fills in plausible-sounding boilerplate where the writer hadn't done the thinking yet.

This isn't policing your teammate's AI use. It's catching the kinds of errors AI introduces, the same way you'd catch a typo or a missing citation. Verifying each other's work is one of the most valuable things peer review does in an AI-assisted workflow, and it's one of the reasons the skill is in such high demand professionally.

## What actionable feedback looks like

Useful feedback has three qualities: it's specific, it points to the place in the piece it's about, and it gives your teammate something to work with, not just a verdict.

- **Hands down a verdict:** "This section is confusing."
- **Names the reviewer's confusion:** "I'm not totally following how the finding in the third paragraph connects to the main claim in the opening. It might help to add a sentence that makes the logic clearer."

- **Closes the conversation:** "Not enough evidence here."
- **Asks an opening question:** "Is there any data from the site audit that would support this recommendation directly? The environmental footprint figures might be a good fit."

Notice the framing in both of the actionable versions. One names the reviewer's own confusion rather than declaring the piece broken ("I'm not following" rather than "this isn't connected"). The other is framed as a question rather than a directive. Feedback is often subjective, and framing it this way opens a conversation instead of handing down a critique. You're a reader telling your teammate how the piece landed for you and inviting them to talk about it, not ruling on whether it works.

It also helps the feedback get used. If a comment reads as a verdict, even feedback that's technically correct can put your teammate on the defensive and stall the work. A few habits help. Frame observations from your own perspective as a reader ("I got a bit lost here" rather than "this is unclear"). Ask a question when you're not sure ("was this meant to connect to the earlier point?"). And before suggesting a change, acknowledge the thinking behind the choice your teammate made. None of this softens the substance of the feedback. It just keeps the door open for a conversation instead of an argument.

The goal isn't to rewrite your teammate's work. It's to give them enough to improve it themselves. One or two structural observations per piece, referenced to specific places and framed in a way they can take in, is more useful than a comprehensive redline.
