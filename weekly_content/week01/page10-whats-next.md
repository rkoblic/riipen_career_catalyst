# What's next

This week has been the foundation. Starting next week, you'll be assigned to a team and a project, and the work begins with two tasks: writing your Team Charter together, and sending your team's first message to your employer contact.

---

## The team charter and employer outreach

The Team Charter is a written agreement about how your team will work together: communication norms, decision-making, roles, and accountability. The employer outreach is your team's first professional contact: a short message through the Riipen platform that introduces your team and requests the kickoff meeting.

Before your first team meeting, take some time with this resource on how teams establish norms and working agreements. Reading about team norms before meeting your team is intentional. If you first encounter these ideas inside the charter conversation, you're learning the concept and trying to apply it under social pressure at the same time. Reading in advance gives you time to form your own positions before group dynamics kick in.

Pay attention to the difference between assumed norms (what people expect but never say out loud) and explicit norms (what the team actually discusses and agrees on). Most team friction comes from the gap between the two.

[CURATED LINK: "How to Create a Team Charter" — Center for Creative Leadership — https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/what-is-this-team-for-and-why-am-i-here/] Covers what team charters are, why they matter, what goes into them, and how to build one. Practical and clearly written (~8 min read).

When you meet with your team, you'll work together to complete a Team Charter. The charter covers:

- **Communication norms.** How and when the team will communicate, plus expected response times.
- **Decision-making.** How the team will resolve disagreements when consensus isn't possible.
- **Roles and responsibilities.** A shared understanding of who takes point on what and how work will be distributed.
- **Accountability.** What happens when someone misses a deadline or doesn't follow through. This is the hardest part of the charter to write and the most important.
- **Meeting norms.** When the team will meet, and how the team will handle missed meetings.

You don't need to fill out the charter now. Reviewing it in advance just lets you arrive at the team meeting with the categories already in mind, so the conversation can focus on positions rather than procedure.

## How to prepare

These questions are worth a few minutes of your time before your first team meeting. Arriving at the charter conversation with your own positions already articulated, even to yourself, reduces the tendency to default to whoever speaks first.

- **What kind of teammate do you want to be?** Think about past group experiences, both good and bad. What did the best collaborators do that made the team work? What behaviors frustrated you?
- **What matters to you in how a team works together?** Some people prioritize reliability. Others care most about open communication, or about how decisions get made. Knowing your own priorities helps you advocate for them.
- **What's your default under pressure?** When a deadline is tight or something goes wrong, do you take over, go quiet, push back, or something else? Understanding your default reaction is the first step to managing it.
- **What do you need from your teammates to do your best work?** This is the question most people never ask out loud, and it's one of the most useful things you can bring to a charter conversation.
