# Professional Engagement

Most graded components in this course assess an artifact you produced — a research document, a presentation, a reflection. Professional Engagement is different. It's a score that captures *how you worked* across the entire semester: whether you showed up reliably for your team, communicated clearly, contributed substantively, and handled feedback and conflict with maturity. The other deliverables show what you produced. Professional Engagement shows how you worked alongside the people who depended on you.

The reason this component exists as its own grade is the same reason professional roles include performance reviews and 360-feedback in addition to project outcomes. In professional practice, your reputation is built less from any single deliverable than from the pattern of how you show up across many of them.

> [!key]
> **No single submission produces this score.** Professional Engagement is composed at the end of the semester from three evidence streams collected at different points: your peers' evaluations of you, your employer's feedback on the team, and your instructor's observations of your professional behavior across the course.

---

## What contributes to your score

Three sources feed Professional Engagement, with the weights below.

| Source | Weight | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| Peer evaluations | 50% | Your individual contribution patterns — reliability, follow-through, collaboration — observed by the teammates closest to your day-to-day work |
| Employer feedback | 25% | Your team's professionalism in client interactions — communication quality, responsiveness, preparedness |
| Instructor observation | 25% | Your professional behavior across the course — checkpoint patterns, communication, feedback responsiveness, judgment |

Peer evaluations carry the highest weight because your teammates see the parts of your contribution that no one else does. The instructor doesn't sit in on your team's working sessions; the employer sees the team as a unit, not who actually did what. Peers know who did the work, who followed through, and who collaborated. The other two sources round out the picture: the employer's view of how the team handles itself professionally, and the instructor's view of patterns visible from outside the team.

---

## Source 1: Peer evaluations (50%)

You'll complete two structured peer evaluations during the course:

- [**Mid-Project Peer Evaluation**](../week08/page2-peer-evaluation-1.md) (Week 8) — formative. The feedback you receive from your teammates is yours alone. It's not a group discussion item, and it's anonymized.
- [**End-of-Project Peer Evaluation**](../week15/page5-end-of-project-peer-evaluation.md) (Week 15) — summative. The same instrument, plus a contribution-allocation step where teammates indicate how the work was shared.

The end-of-project evaluation weighs more heavily than the mid-project one, on the logic that the longer arc of working together produces more reliable observations than the first half does on its own.

Each evaluation rates your teammates across four dimensions: **Reliability**, **Contribution**, **Constructive Engagement**, and **Accountability**. The full instrument is explained on the linked pages.

> [!info] A note on honesty
> Peer evaluations only work as a fair grade component if people complete them honestly, including the hard parts. Inflating ratings to be nice doesn't help the teammate it's about — they don't learn anything from a generic "great teammate" rating — and it weakens the grading mechanism for everyone. The course design assumes you can write specific, professional feedback even when it's uncomfortable. That assumption is itself a skill you're building.

---

## Source 2: Employer feedback (25%)

Your employer completes two short surveys during the project:

- **Mid-project survey** (around Week 8). Three rating dimensions — **Communication**, **Preparedness**, **Responsiveness** — plus qualitative feedback.
- **End-of-project survey** (after the final presentation). The same dimensions, plus an overall partnership-quality signal.

You will not see the employer's ratings directly. The end-of-project survey weighs more heavily than the mid-project one, for the same reason as peer evaluations.

Employer feedback is a **team-level** input. The employer sees the team as a unit and rates it accordingly. Every team member starts from the same employer score. Your instructor may adjust an individual team member's employer component up or down by a modest amount when peer evaluations make clear that one teammate's contribution diverged substantially from the team's overall pattern, but the default is that everyone on the team gets the same employer-component score.

What the employer is rating is straightforward: whether your team is easy to work with. Did you respond to emails in a reasonable time? Did you arrive at meetings prepared? Was the communication clear? These are the same things any client notices in any professional engagement.

---

## Source 3: Instructor observation (25%)

This component captures what your instructor observes across the course. Unlike peer and employer feedback, which use structured rating instruments, instructor observation is a holistic score informed by patterns the instructor sees over the full 15 weeks. Four kinds of evidence inform it:

- **Checkpoint patterns.** Were team checkpoints (Kickoff Summary, Direction Check Summary, Work-in-Progress Summary, Draft Deliverable) submitted on time, with genuine team effort?
- **Communication quality.** Were your emails and questions clear, professional, and timely? Did you reach out proactively when something needed clarification?
- **Feedback responsiveness.** When your instructor gave formative feedback on checkpoints or graded work, did the next iteration show evidence the feedback had been taken on?
- **Professional judgment.** How did your team handle conflict, missed expectations, or moments of pressure? Did the team communicate early when something was off-track?

The instructor's role is intentionally different from the other two sources. Peers see the inside of the team. Employers see the outside. The instructor sees the seams: the moments when something starts to wobble, the responses to feedback, the patterns over time that neither peers nor the employer have a frame for.

---

## How the inputs combine

At the end of the semester, your instructor combines the three component scores using the weights above to produce a single Professional Engagement score. The math is mechanical; the judgment comes in calibrating edge cases — a missing employer survey, a peer evaluation that diverges sharply from observed behavior, an accommodation that affected one of the inputs.

The specific behaviors that produce strong scores on the peer evaluation dimensions are explained in detail on the Mid-Project Peer Evaluation page in Week 8 and the End-of-Project Peer Evaluation page in Week 14. Reviewing those before Week 8 is the most useful preparation.

## What this means for your week-to-week work

Professional Engagement is unusual as a grade component because there's no single moment you can prepare for. You can't write a strong Professional Engagement submission the night before it's due, because there isn't one. The score is produced by patterns: what your teammates have come to expect from you, what your employer has seen across multiple touchpoints, what your instructor has observed in checkpoints and communications across 15 weeks.

The practical implication is short:

- **Be reliable in small things.** Show up to meetings, respond to messages, follow through on what you said you'd do. Most of what builds a strong Professional Engagement score is the unglamorous stuff.
- **Communicate early when something's off** — a deadline you can't meet, a piece of feedback you don't understand, a tension on the team. Proactive communication is what your instructor and employer are watching for.
- **Take feedback seriously.** When you receive formative feedback (from peers, from employer touchpoints, from your instructor's comments on checkpoints), the next version of your work should show evidence you engaged with it.
- **Write peer evaluations honestly.** The system depends on it, and the skill of writing specific, evidence-based feedback to a colleague is part of what the course is teaching you to do.
