# Making progress under uncertainty

Development rarely starts with everything you need. Recognizing which gaps actually block you and which ones you can build around is one of the more visible things you'll demonstrate during this project. So is what you do once you've recognized them: choosing between two viable directions when both have merit, deciding when a question is worth bringing to the employer versus resolving internally, and presenting the call back to your team and stakeholders so the work can move forward. This is the page to keep open while you draft.

## Three types of ambiguity (and why they require different responses)

Not all uncertainty is the same, and treating different kinds the same way leads to the wrong response. When development stalls, it's usually because of one of three things.

**Missing information**

A specific fact, data point, or input you genuinely don't have. The gap is real, and you can name it precisely.

What to do: targeted research to fill it, a specific clarifying question to a teammate or your team's employer contact, or a documented flag that acknowledges the gap and states what you assumed in its absence. The key word is *targeted*. Once a gap is named precisely, the fix usually names itself.

What not to do: treat a missing piece of supporting evidence as a reason to stop entirely. Most missing information is gap-filling, not structural. You can build around it and return later.

**Unclear expectations**

You have the material, but you're not certain what "done" looks like. This is usually a question about standard or direction, not about facts.

What to do: go back to the project brief, the deliverable rubric, or the employer's framing from the Direction Check. Look for comparable examples. What did a strong version of this kind of piece look like elsewhere? In most cases, unclear expectations are resolvable without asking anyone. If they're not, a specific question to your team ("I read the brief as asking for X. Does that match your reading?") is better than a general one.

**Conflicting priorities**

Different inputs (research findings, employer feedback, team assumptions) point in different directions, and you're not sure which one to follow.

What to do: name the tension explicitly, to yourself and to your team. "The employer mentioned X in the Direction Check, but our research suggests Y. How do we want to handle that?" Naming the conflict explicitly is often most of the work. Once it's on the table, deciding what to do is usually more straightforward.

## When to keep going vs. when to stop

These aren't two equally weighted options. In most development situations, keeping going is the right default. The question is when it isn't.

**Move forward when:**

- You can develop the piece and fill the gap later without restructuring what you've already done
- The missing piece is supporting detail, not the core claim or direction
- You can make a documented assumption and note it clearly in your draft ("Assuming X, which we'll verify by...") so a teammate reviewing it knows what to check

**Pause when:**

- The gap is structural. If you got the missing information and it contradicted your current direction, you'd have to start over.
- You're waiting on a teammate's work and you can't move forward until you have it. Escalate it through your team's shared channel rather than trying to push through alone.
- You've spent an hour researching and you're not learning anything new. At that point you likely have enough to start drafting, and more research is starting to look like stalling.

**Flagging a blocker without stalling the team**

When you're genuinely blocked, the way you flag it matters. Be specific: "I'm waiting on [specific thing] before I can complete [specific part of my piece]" is actionable. "I'm stuck" is not. Flag it in your team's shared channel, and then move to a different part of your work while you wait. Almost every piece of work has parts you can advance in parallel.

## Choosing between paths

When you've decided to pause and pick a direction, you'll usually find you have two viable options, not one. Both look reasonable. Both are defensible. The team is split, or you're split with yourself. This is the moment that trips most learners up over the life of a project.

A few questions move the call forward:

**Which option keeps the most options open?** When both paths are roughly equivalent on quality, pick the one that doesn't foreclose other choices later. A piece you can revise in either direction beats a piece you've committed to a single interpretation of.

**Which is reversible?** Some decisions you can change at low cost. Others lock in the rest of the work. If the choice is reversible, default to action: pick something, see what happens, course-correct. If the choice is hard to reverse (a recommendation framing, a structural claim, an assumption that drives the rest of your piece), spend more time on it.

**Which gets you to a testable position fastest?** A draft you can show to a teammate or bring to the employer is worth more than another hour of internal deliberation. Even a partly-wrong draft surfaces what's actually unclear in a way more thinking won't.

**Which costs less if it's wrong?** If both options seem roughly equal, ask which one is cheaper to redo. If Option A takes a day to recover from and Option B takes a week, lean toward A.

When none of these tests breaks the tie, pick one. The cost of further deliberation is usually higher than the cost of being wrong.

## When to bring it to the employer

Not every question is an employer question. Most things your team should resolve internally. A question worth bringing to the employer is one where:

- **The employer has information your team doesn't.** Their organization, their audience, the constraints they haven't documented. If the answer is something you can figure out, figure it out.
- **The decision shapes the deliverable's direction, not just its details.** "Should the recommendations be three or five?" is a team call. "Our research can support a recommendation for either segment A or segment B, and they imply different next steps. Which is more useful to you?" is an employer question.
- **You can't make the call confidently with what you have.** The bar isn't "we feel uncertain." It's "we've actually tried to resolve it and we can't."

A useful test: if you imagine asking the employer your question and the most likely answer is "you decide, you know the project better than I do," it isn't an employer question.

Timing matters too. Most questions are batchable. Save them for the next standing employer touchpoint (Check-In #1 next week is the first of these in the development phase). Questions that genuinely block work right now are the exception, not the default. A team that brings four or five specific, batched questions to a check-in shows they've been in the work. A team that messages the employer every time a question comes up reads as either dependent or under-prepared.

## Justifying and presenting your call

Once you've decided, you'll need to present the decision back. Not defensively, but as part of the work. Teammates need to know what you decided so they can build on it. The employer needs to see the reasoning behind a recommendation, not just the recommendation. Internal reviewers need to see what you considered and what you ruled out.

A four-part structure works for any decision worth presenting:

**What you decided.** State the call clearly. "We're framing the user research findings around X." "We're recommending approach A over approach B."

**What else you considered.** Name the alternatives briefly. This signals that the decision was deliberate and gives the reader something to push back on if they disagree.

**Why you decided this way.** The reasoning. What about your research, your team's context, or the employer's framing pointed toward this option? One or two sentences is usually enough.

**The tradeoff you accepted.** Every decision has a downside. Naming it shows you're not glossing over it. "This approach prioritizes X at the cost of Y." "This recommendation is stronger if [condition], and we're assuming that based on..."

The shorter you can make this, the better. Two or three sentences in a draft is usually enough. A long defense reads as nervousness. A short, clear one reads as confidence.

## Putting it together: a worked example

A community outreach team is drafting their recommended awareness campaign. Their research surfaced two viable strategic angles: emphasize the program's affordability (lower-friction, broader reach) or emphasize its outcomes (higher-trust, narrower reach). Both are defensible. Both are supported by their research. They can't move forward without making a call.

**Choosing between paths.** Working through the questions: neither path is more reversible than the other, and the team is roughly split on quality. The outcomes angle takes longer to validate but produces a stronger recommendation if it lands. The affordability angle is faster to test. They lean toward affordability for the draft because it gets them to a testable position fastest.

**Whether to bring it to the employer.** The team thinks yes. The employer knows which angle aligns with funding priorities and the audiences they're already engaging, and the team's research alone can't resolve that. They batch the question for Check-In #1 along with two others.

**Presenting the call.** In the draft, they include a short paragraph: *"We're framing the campaign around affordability rather than outcomes. We considered both. Affordability gives us a faster path to a testable recommendation and reaches a broader audience, at the cost of a less differentiated value proposition. We're assuming affordability aligns with the program's funding context, and would value confirmation at the next check-in."*

That paragraph names the decision, the alternative, the reasoning, the tradeoff, and what they want feedback on. Three sentences. The teammate reviewing the section knows where the logic came from. The employer arrives at the check-in with the question already framed and can answer it directly.
