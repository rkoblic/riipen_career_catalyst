# Starting development as a team

Most professional projects include a moment like the one your team is in this week. The direction is set, the work needs to get done, and the team has to figure out who is doing what, how the deliverable is structured, and how the pieces will fit together. That's the moment to slow down for a few minutes of planning, before anyone starts building in earnest.

Teams that take this setup work seriously tend to produce deliverables that hang together as one coherent piece of work. Teams that skip it tend to discover late in the process that their pieces don't quite fit: different terminology, different formatting, overlapping content, gaps where one person assumed another was covering something. Both approaches take about the same amount of total time. The difference is when in the week you spend it.

A note before you start: projects produce all kinds of deliverables. Some teams will be writing a research report. Others will be building a content strategy, a presentation deck, a campaign brief, a process map, a prototype, a training module, or some combination of these. The principles below apply to all of them. Where this page uses "document" or "sections" as shorthand, translate to whatever fits your specific deliverable: slides, scenes, screens, pages, modules, components.

---

## Plan the work together

At your standing team meeting this week, walk through these questions together before anyone starts working on their piece:

**What are the actual components of this deliverable?** Your project plan probably named some. Now is the time to look at them concretely. What's the full list of pieces — sections, slides, screens, scenes, assets — that need to exist by the time this is done? Are there any you missed, or things you listed that don't actually need to be there?

**Who owns which piece?** Look at your team's strengths, interests, and the work each person has already been doing. Someone who's been deep in the user research is the natural owner of the user research piece. Someone with strong writing or design instincts might be the right person for the executive summary or the opening slide. There's no formula. Talk through it together and aim for assignments that play to people's strengths and split the workload roughly evenly.

Each piece should have one named owner who is responsible for the first draft and for keeping it current as the project evolves. That doesn't mean they're the only person who touches it. Other teammates review, contribute, and edit. But without a clearly named owner, pieces stall, or get built twice, or get built once and then quietly forgotten.

**What does each owner need to deliver?** Roughly how big — word count, slide count, scene length, or whatever scale makes sense for your format? In what format or working file? By when? Ambiguous expectations now produce uneven work later. A few minutes spent agreeing on the rough shape of each piece saves a lot of friction down the line.

**Who handles what isn't piece-shaped?** Cross-cutting work isn't a section of its own, but it still needs owners: things like formatting and visual consistency, integration passes between pieces, agreed terminology and references, and the final polish at the end. Decide now who's holding those.

This is the most important conversation of the week.

---

## Set up your shared workspace

Once you know the shape of the work and who's doing what, set up the workspace you'll all be building in.

**One shared workspace, not separate files.** Whether your team is working in a shared document, a deck, a Figma board, a video project, or something else, keep everything in one place from day one. Merging independently-maintained files introduces version-control problems, formatting conflicts, and duplicate or contradictory content.

**Standards applied first.** The Formatting professional deliverables page in this week's module covers standards for written deliverables and the equivalents for decks, visuals, video, and mixed-format packages. Whatever format you're working in, agree on the standards (fonts, colors, slide masters, layout templates, file naming conventions) and set them up before content goes in. Applying standards retroactively is far more disruptive than setting them up correctly from the start.

**Agreed terminology documented.** What do you call your employer's organization? Their product or service? The population they serve? Inconsistent terminology across pieces signals that a deliverable was assembled, not authored. Decide on key terms before development begins and keep the list visible to everyone.

---

## Map dependencies between pieces

Some pieces of the deliverable depend on others being developed first. Your strategic recommendations can't be finalized until your research findings have a clear direction. The executive summary or opening slide can't be finished until the rest of the work is stable. Other components can proceed in parallel.

Spend a few minutes mapping these dependencies before development begins. Which pieces need to be built first? Which can proceed independently? Which will need a second pass once a dependent piece is further along? Knowing this in advance prevents one team member from stalling while they wait on someone else's work, and it prevents two pieces from committing to directions that turn out to conflict.

---

## Before you start development: setup checklist

- [ ] Team has used this week's standing meeting to plan the work together: components identified, ownership assigned, deliverable expectations agreed
- [ ] One shared workspace created (document, deck, Figma board, project file, or whatever fits your deliverable); all team members have access and edit permissions
- [ ] Standards established and applied to the working template before content is added (see the Formatting professional deliverables page for written and non-written formats)
- [ ] Agreed terminology documented and visible to the full team (in the workspace, in a shared note, or in your Team Charter)
- [ ] Dependencies mapped: which pieces must be developed before others can be built, which can proceed in parallel
