# From research to draft

By this point in the week, your team has met to plan the work, agreed on what the deliverable looks like, and assigned each person to a specific piece. You've done research for your assigned piece. Now you need to turn that research into a draft.

Most professional writing starts here rather than at a blank page: with notes, prior documents, and a stack of material the writer needs to make sense of. The gap between "I have a lot of material" and "I know what I'm going to say" is where the real analytical work happens, and it's the step that's easiest to rush. This page walks through how to close that gap for the piece you've been assigned.

A note on format: this page focuses on taking notes into written prose, since most Riipen projects produce some kind of written deliverable. If your team is producing a deck, video, prototype, or other format, the same notes → structure → produce sequence applies. Translate "outline" to "storyboard," "wireframe," or whatever fits your format.

---

## Why people stall between research and writing

Research and writing are different cognitive modes. Research is accumulative and exploratory. You're gathering, following leads, adding to what you know. Writing is selective and argumentative. You're deciding what matters, what order it goes in, and what claim you're making.

The step between them is synthesis: the process of finding the structure in your material before you commit to prose. Synthesis is easy to skip, and when you skip it, you get one of two things: a data dump (everything you found, in the order you found it) or paralysis in front of a blank page (because you haven't yet figured out what you're going to say).

The notes → outline → draft sequence is a discipline. Each step is genuinely different from the one before it, and trying to collapse them saves no time. It just moves the difficulty later, when you're more attached to what you've already written.

---

## From notes to outline: finding the structure in your piece

Before you can draft, you need to know what you're drafting. This four-step process turns your research notes into an outline you can actually write from.

1. **Review your notes once before organizing.** Read through everything you've collected without trying to sort it yet. You're getting a sense of the landscape: what you know, what keeps coming up, what feels central versus peripheral. Don't highlight or annotate on this pass. Just read.

2. **Identify recurring ideas and themes.** Look for patterns across your notes: ideas that appear in multiple sources, concepts that connect things you wouldn't have grouped together, questions that your research keeps circling back to. These recurring patterns become the building blocks of your piece. Within the section your team assigned to you, they'll show up as subsections, paragraphs, or key points. The structure comes from your material rather than from a predetermined shape you're trying to fill.

3. **Label each cluster with a working name.** Give each group of related ideas a name that describes the idea, not the source. "Three studies on pricing transparency" is a source label. "How pricing transparency affects trust" is an idea label. The distinction matters: when you draft, you're writing about ideas, and the sources support what you're saying.

4. **Look for what's missing.** Once you've clustered everything you have, ask: what would a reader of this piece want to know that my material doesn't answer? Gaps identified at the outline stage are fixable with a few minutes of targeted research. Gaps identified mid-draft cost significantly more.

---

## Is your outline ready to draft from?

An outline that looks complete isn't always one you can write from. Before you start producing prose, ask three questions:

- **What is this piece's main claim or purpose?** If you can't state the piece's main claim or purpose in one sentence, the outline isn't ready. A topic alone isn't enough; you need to know what you're saying about it.
- **Is the order defensible?** Can you articulate why A comes before B, beyond "it's the order I happened to write the points in"? If the order is arbitrary, a reader will feel it.
- **Are the transitions obvious?** What connects each part to the next? If the answer is "they're just sitting next to each other," you don't have transitions yet. A strong outline already knows how its parts connect.

If you can't answer all three, the outline needs more work. The most common production mistake is starting to write too early and having to restructure once you're already attached to what you've written.

---

## Moving from outline to prose

Your first draft just needs to get your ideas onto the page in a form you can revise. It doesn't need to be the version you'd submit. Knowing that takes some of the pressure off.

- **Work through your outline in order.** Following your outline sequentially keeps the logical flow intact and surfaces any setup that's missing. If a later part of your piece relies on something you were going to establish earlier, working in order catches it.
- **Draft the introduction last.** If your piece has an opening or framing paragraph, save it for the end. Introductions are genuinely hard to write before you know what you're introducing. Draft the body first, then come back to the opening.
- **Don't edit while drafting.** Read for comprehension as you go (make sure you understand what you just wrote), but save language-level edits for the revision pass. Editing while drafting interrupts your flow and tends to produce slower writing without making it better.
- **Use placeholders.** When you hit a gap, mark it and keep moving: `[EXPAND THIS]`, `[NEED DATA HERE]`, `[TRANSITION]`. A draft full of named gaps is more useful than a draft that stops at the first one.

## Using AI in the notes-to-draft process

AI can reduce the structural overhead of synthesis without doing the analysis for you. Three specific uses:

**Clustering raw notes**

Paste your notes into an AI tool and ask: *"Here are my raw research notes on [topic]: [notes]. Help me identify the three to five main themes or clusters across these notes. Don't summarize. Just group them and label each cluster."*

AI is good at the sorting step here. It can't generate the analysis or tell you what to say about each cluster. That part stays with you.

**Stress-testing the outline**

Once you have a draft outline, ask: *"Here is my outline for my assigned piece: [outline]. What gaps do you see? What questions would a reader have after reading this that the outline doesn't seem to answer?"*

Use the response as a prompt for your own judgment, not as a list of instructions to follow. Some of what AI flags will be real gaps. Some will reflect assumptions about your audience or context that it can't make for you.

**Checking sequence**

Before you start drafting, ask: *"I'm about to start drafting from this outline. Is there anything in the structure that seems out of order or that would be confusing to a reader?"*

Again, take the feedback as input rather than direction. You know your content and your audience better than a general-purpose tool does.

In all three cases, the intellectual work stays yours. AI can help you see structure, but it doesn't build it for you.
