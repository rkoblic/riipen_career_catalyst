# Reflection #2

You've just finished the Discovery phase. You did weeks of research, produced your first major team deliverable (the Context Analysis & Project Plan), and presented it to the employer in the Direction Check. Reflection #2 is the formal close of that phase. Before you turn the page into production, take stock of what those weeks actually taught you.

Where Reflection #1 looked at what it was like to enter a new professional situation, Reflection #2 looks at what it was like to do your first real piece of professional work in it. The most natural anchor is the process of building the Context Analysis & Project Plan: the team coordination, the research decisions, the moments where the path forward wasn't obvious and you had to act anyway.

---

This is your second DEAL reflection — an individual submission graded as part of the Reflection Portfolio. You worked through the framework once in Week 3, and the rubric and the four dimensions are the same. If you need a refresher on how DEAL works or what each stage is asking for, the Week 3 reflection page and the reflection portfolio framework are the references. This page focuses on what's different about this one: the experience pool you're drawing from, the competency it asks you to surface, and the higher bar for depth.

---

## The prompt

The Discovery phase asked you to do something harder than it might have appeared: make strategic sense of a problem you didn't fully understand, build a team-authored deliverable on a deadline, and adjust course based on what your employer told you. Choose a specific moment from the last couple of weeks where you encountered ambiguity: a moment where the path forward was unclear and you had to decide how to act without complete information.

The Context Analysis & Project Plan is the most likely anchor. It was your first major graded team deliverable, and producing it required navigating uncertainty together. Other valid moments include the research process itself, a team decision you made under pressure, the Direction Check with the employer, or a team dynamic that surfaced as you worked toward submission.

Use the DEAL framework to describe what happened, examine what your response to that ambiguity reveals about how you take initiative and navigate uncertainty, and articulate what you've learned about your own approach when the answer isn't obvious.

What matters is that you were in the experience, not observing it.

---

## What changes for your second reflection

The cognitive work of learning the framework is behind you. The expectation now is that you'll use the space that frees up for genuine depth in your examination.

The rubric is the same as Reflection #1: Descriptive Specificity, Critical Examination, Personal Accountability, and Articulation of Learning, equally weighted. But the benchmark has moved because you have a baseline. Your instructor will read this reflection with your first one in view. Growth is evidence of learning. Staying at the same depth isn't the same as meeting the standard again.

The most common way reflections plateau between the first and second attempt: holding the same level of depth in the Examine stage because it was sufficient before. It was sufficient as a first attempt. This is a second attempt. Push the examination further than felt necessary last time.

Personal accountability is where most first reflections fall short. That means examining your own role, decisions, and assumptions rather than narrating events from outside. If you placed yourself inside the experience in your first reflection, go deeper. If you didn't, this is the time to correct that.

## Using AI

AI was introduced as a thinking partner for reflection earlier in the course, and this is its second application. The boundary is the same: AI cannot supply the experience, the examination, or the articulated learning. A reflection that could have been written by any learner in any project hasn't met the standard regardless of what tools were used.

Where AI can genuinely help is in deepening the Examine stage. Three specific approaches:

- **Surfacing unnamed assumptions.** Describe your experience to an AI tool and ask: "What assumptions might I be making that I haven't explicitly named?" Use what it identifies as a prompt for your own thinking, not as conclusions to copy.
- **Pushing back on surface-level examination.** Share a draft Examine section and ask: "Where am I still on the surface here? What's a harder question I could be asking myself?" The friction is the point.
- **Anticipating a skeptical reader.** Ask the AI to ask you the questions a skeptical reader would have after reading your draft. Then answer those questions in your revision. Don't explain yourself to the AI; go back into the document and address them directly.

In all three cases, the intellectual work is yours. AI helps you see where you haven't gone far enough yet.

## Submission

**Length:** 800–1200 words. Under 700 words rarely achieves sufficient depth in the Examine stage. Over 1,400 words usually indicates the description is too broad. Pick one experience and go deeper, not wider.

**Structure:** Use the three DEAL stages as organizing sections. Headers are encouraged but not required; what matters is that a reader can tell where description ends and examination begins.

**Submission:** Individually through the Riipen platform.

**AI disclosure:** If you used AI at any point in your reflection process, include a brief description of what you used and how. If you did not use AI, a one-sentence statement to that effect is sufficient. Disclosure is required either way.
