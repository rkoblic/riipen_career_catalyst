# Researching your assigned piece

By this point, your team has met to plan the work, and you know which piece of the deliverable you're responsible for. Before you can draft, you need research that's specific to your assigned piece.

Some of that research is already done. Your team did broad context research during the Discovery phase: who the employer is, what they need, what the landscape looks like. That work gave you the foundation. But your assigned piece almost certainly needs research that goes deeper or narrower than what the team gathered together. A findings section needs actual findings. An implementation section needs to know what implementation looks like in practice. A user research section needs actual users. The Discovery research built context; your section needs substance.

This page focuses on research for written deliverables, but the same approach applies to a deck, prototype, video, or other format. Translate "section" to whatever piece of the deliverable you've been assigned.

---

## Define what your piece needs

Start from purpose. Before you go looking for material, answer two questions:

**What is this piece supposed to accomplish in the deliverable as a whole?** Not "the user research section," but "the user research section is supposed to convince the employer that the population we're serving has [specific characteristic], and that this characteristic should shape the recommendation." Knowing what your piece is *for* tells you what it needs to argue. Without that anchor, you'll research everything that might be useful and end up with too much.

**What does this piece need to answer or contain?** List the specific questions your piece needs to address, or the specific evidence it needs to provide. These become your research targets.

Then check: which of those questions can you answer from what your team already knows (the Context Analysis, kickoff notes, Direction Check feedback)? Cross those off. What remains is the research you actually need to do this week.

---

## Where to look

Section-level research generally pulls from a few different places:

- **What the employer has already given you.** Briefs, reports, internal documents, links from the kickoff. This is the cheapest source and the one that gets most under-used. Re-read everything the employer has shared with the lens of your specific piece.
- **Secondary research.** Published sources, industry reports, comparable cases, case studies of similar projects. Useful for context, benchmarking, and grounding your recommendations in evidence beyond the immediate project.
- **Primary research, where appropriate.** Interviews, surveys, document analysis, observation. More work and more time, but sometimes the only way to get what your piece needs, especially for user research, customer research, or any piece that needs evidence about people's behavior or experience.
- **The [Neuron tools directory](https://www.theneuron.ai/top-tools/)** introduced earlier in the course has research and information-gathering tools worth revisiting now that you're researching with a specific piece in mind.

Match the source type to the question. If your piece needs to argue something about a population, secondary research about similar populations is helpful but won't replace talking to a few of those people directly. If your piece needs to recommend an implementation approach, comparable case studies are exactly the right material.

## When you need a skill you haven't built yet

Production sometimes asks you to use a method you haven't learned yet, or write a kind of section you've never written before. That's a normal professional situation, and the response is the same as it is for working professionals: find a resource at the right level, learn just enough to do what your piece actually needs, and ask a colleague (or AI) when you get stuck.

**Methods you may need to use**

- **Qualitative interviews and thematic analysis,** if your section needs community member, user, or stakeholder voice.
- **Survey or quantitative data analysis,** if your section needs to work with numerical data like participation rates, budget figures, or demographic statistics.
- **Process or workflow mapping,** if your section needs to document or analyze how something currently happens, step by step.
- **Persona or user profile development,** if your section needs to characterize the people the deliverable is ultimately for.
- **Stakeholder mapping,** if your section needs to identify who has influence over a decision or whose buy-in is needed.

For qualitative interviews specifically (a common case in many projects), here are three resources to choose from. Pick whichever format works best for you.

- [**Stakeholder Interviews 101**](https://youtu.be/fekaYjH7Ysw?si=FFyVMLohlf0v2Qy2) (video) — Nielsen Norman Group. Short, accessible walkthrough of the purpose and structure of stakeholder interviews from the leading UX research organization.
- [**Customer Discovery: What Do You Ask, with Justin Wilcox**](https://youtu.be/OTkP2JDeGWM?si=BedFqeRfqKVgFzhU) (video) — Techstars. Practical guidance on the actual questions to ask during a discovery interview. Useful if you need to plan an actual conversation, not just understand the theory.
- [**Qualitative Interviews Tipsheet**](https://dism.duke.edu/files/2020/05/Tipsheet-Qualitative_Interviews.pdf) (PDF) — Duke Initiative on Survey Methodology. A concise reference covering practical tips for planning and running qualitative interviews. Useful as a quick checklist before and during interview work.

**Writing patterns you may need to use**

Different sections of a deliverable have different conventions. A findings section is built around evidence and themes. An implementation section is built around feasibility and sequencing. A risks section is built around honest acknowledgment of what could go wrong. An executive summary is built around the few things a busy reader most needs to know. If you've never written one of these before, look up two or three examples from comparable professional reports before you draft. Most of these conventions are learnable in 30 minutes of reading examples.

**Using AI to bridge a skill gap**

For methods or section types you've never used before, AI is a useful thinking partner. Try a prompt like *"I need to write a [risks section / executive summary / findings section] for a community engagement strategy report. What are the conventions for this kind of section, what mistakes do first-time writers usually make, and what does a strong example look like in plain language?"* The intellectual work of applying what you get to your own piece stays with you, but you don't have to start from a blank page.

## How much is enough?

The most common research mistake at this stage is doing too much. Section-level research is bounded by the section's purpose, and a piece doesn't need to be exhaustive on its topic. It needs to be sufficient for what the section is trying to argue.

Two practical tests:

- **Can you answer your piece's core questions?** Look back at the questions you listed in the first step. If you have an answer for each one (even if it's a working answer you might revise), you're ready to draft.
- **Are you still learning new things?** If you've spent an hour researching and the new material is just confirming what you already found, you're past the point of useful return.

Set a soft time budget before you start. There's space allocated for independent research in this week's project work. Treat it as a ceiling, not a target.

---

## Capture notes so synthesis is easier later

How you take notes now determines how hard the next step is. A few habits worth building:

- **Capture by idea, not by source.** Group what you find around the questions you're trying to answer, rather than around which source it came from. This makes the next step (turning notes into an outline) much faster.
- **Keep citations attached.** Even informal notes should record where each piece of information came from. You'll need this when you draft, and reconstructing it later is painful.
- **Mark relevance.** As you go, flag what's directly useful for your piece and what's interesting but tangential. Tangential material is fine to keep. It just shouldn't crowd out the central material when you sit down to draft.

If you've followed these habits, the next page (From research to draft) describes how to turn your notes into an outline.

## What this looks like in practice

Three brief examples of what section-level research looks like for different project types.

**Process improvement team: workflow analysis section.** Discovery research established what the operation does and what the employer thinks the problem is. The workflow analysis section needs to map the current workflow and identify bottlenecks. Research this week: re-read the employer's process documentation, interview two people who do the workflow, and look up one or two published frameworks for diagnosing operational bottlenecks. Roughly 90 minutes.

**Social media team: audience analysis section.** Discovery research identified the broad audience the nonprofit serves. The audience analysis section needs to define specific personas and explain how each one engages with content. Research this week: re-read everything the nonprofit shared about their existing audience, look at their current social media engagement data, find two or three published reports on similar nonprofit audiences, and draft three persona profiles with demographic and behavior detail. Roughly 90 minutes.

**Community outreach team: barriers to participation section.** Discovery research found that participation has been low and the center has hypotheses about why. The barriers section needs to identify and prioritize the actual barriers based on evidence. Research this week: re-read the kickoff notes for everything the center said about the community, look up two or three published studies on participation barriers in comparable programs, and document the assumptions being made where evidence is thin. Roughly 90 minutes.

In all three cases, the research is bounded, purpose-driven, and targeted at the section's specific questions. None of these team members are trying to research everything about their topic. They're researching what their piece needs, then drafting from there.
