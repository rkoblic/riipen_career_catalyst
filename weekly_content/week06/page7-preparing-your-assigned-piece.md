# Preparing your assigned piece

By this point, your team has likely met to plan the work, and you know which piece of the deliverable you're responsible for. Before you can draft, you need to prepare. That means getting clear on what your piece needs to do and assembling the material it will draw on, whether that material already exists or still needs to be gathered.

Different pieces require different mixes of research, synthesis, and analytical work. This page walks through how to prepare regardless of which mix your piece needs.

---

## What if your piece isn't ready to prepare yet?

Some pieces depend on work that hasn't happened yet. A recommendations piece needs findings to recommend from. An implementation roadmap needs a recommendation to implement. An executive summary needs everything else first. If your piece isn't ready to prepare this week, your job is to help with what does need to get done: running interviews, contributing to the team's synthesis, supporting whatever upstream work the deliverable depends on right now.

The same expectation will apply in reverse in later weeks. When your piece becomes the active work, the team members whose pieces are downstream of yours will be the ones contributing to it. Development teams move forward together, not as parallel tracks.

---

## Start from purpose

Before you go looking for material or start thinking through implications, answer two questions:

**What is this piece supposed to accomplish in the deliverable as a whole?** Not "the user research section," but "the user research section is supposed to convince the employer that the population we're serving has [specific characteristic], and that this characteristic should shape the recommendation." Knowing what your piece is *for* tells you what it needs to argue or demonstrate. Without that anchor, you'll gather everything that might be useful and end up with too much.

**What does this piece need to answer or contain?** List the specific questions your piece needs to address, or the specific evidence and reasoning it needs to provide. These become your preparation targets: the things you need to be ready to write about when you sit down to draft.

---

## Figure out what your piece actually needs

Different pieces of a deliverable need different kinds of preparation. Look at your list of questions and sort them roughly into three buckets:

**Questions your team already has material on.** For these, your work is synthesis: pulling the relevant bits from what the team already gathered, organizing them around your specific questions, and making sure you understand them well enough to write about them clearly. No new research needed, but the thinking work is real.

**Questions that need new information.** For these, your work is research: going out and finding material the team doesn't have yet. This could be secondary research (published sources, comparable cases, industry reports), primary research (interviews, surveys, observation), or re-reading source material the employer provided through a narrower lens.

**Questions that need thinking, not more material.** For these, your work is analytical: taking what you already have and working through what it means. What are the trade-offs between different options? What would a recommendation actually look like? How do different pieces of evidence fit together? These questions don't get answered by finding more information. They get answered by sitting with what you have and thinking carefully.

Most pieces need some of each. A strategy recommendation, for example, may need a small amount of benchmarking research (new information), a re-read of the team's Discovery findings (synthesis), and substantial thinking about how those findings translate into specific recommendations (analysis). Knowing the mix for your piece tells you where to spend your time this week.

---

## Where to find material

For the questions that need new information, or for pieces where research is the main activity, material generally comes from a few places:

- **What the employer has already given you.** Briefs, reports, internal documents, links from the kickoff. This is the cheapest source and the one that gets most under-used. Re-read everything the employer has shared with the lens of your specific piece.
- **What your team gathered during Discovery.** The Context Analysis, research notes, kickoff summary, Direction Check summary. Almost always contains material relevant to your piece that you didn't notice the first time around.
- **Secondary research.** Published sources, industry reports, comparable cases, benchmarking data. Useful for context, comparison, and grounding your reasoning in evidence beyond the immediate project.
- **Primary research, where appropriate.** Interviews, surveys, document analysis, observation. More work and more time, but sometimes the only way to get what your piece needs, especially for user research, customer research, or any piece that needs evidence about people's behavior or experience.
- **The [Neuron tools directory](https://www.theneuron.ai/top-tools/)** introduced earlier in the course has research and information-gathering tools worth revisiting now that you're preparing with a specific piece in mind.

Match the source type to the question. If your piece needs to argue something about a population, secondary research about similar populations is helpful but won't replace talking to a few of those people directly. If your piece needs to recommend an approach, comparable cases are exactly the right material. If your piece needs to make sense of trade-offs, the material is probably already in what the team has.

## When you need a skill you haven't built yet

Development sometimes asks you to use a method you haven't learned yet, or prepare a kind of piece you've never prepared before. That's a normal professional situation, and the response is the same as it is for working professionals: find a resource at the right level, learn just enough to do what your piece actually needs, and ask a colleague (or AI) when you get stuck.

**Methods you may need to use**

- **Qualitative interviews and thematic analysis,** if your piece needs community member, user, or stakeholder voice.
- **Survey or quantitative data analysis,** if your piece needs to work with numerical data like participation rates, budget figures, or demographic statistics.
- **Process or workflow mapping,** if your piece needs to document or analyze how something currently happens, step by step.
- **Persona or user profile development,** if your piece needs to characterize the people the deliverable is ultimately for.
- **Stakeholder mapping,** if your piece needs to identify who has influence over a decision or whose buy-in is needed.
- **Benchmarking and comparative analysis,** if your piece needs to position the employer against competitors or comparable organizations.
- **Trade-off or options analysis,** if your piece needs to weigh different strategic directions against one another.

For qualitative interviews specifically (a common case in many projects), here are three resources to choose from. Pick whichever format works best for you.

- [**Stakeholder Interviews 101**](https://youtu.be/fekaYjH7Ysw?si=FFyVMLohlf0v2Qy2) (video) — Nielsen Norman Group. Short, accessible walkthrough of the purpose and structure of stakeholder interviews from the leading UX research organization.
- [**Customer Discovery: What Do You Ask, with Justin Wilcox**](https://youtu.be/OTkP2JDeGWM?si=BedFqeRfqKVgFzhU) (video) — Techstars. Practical guidance on the actual questions to ask during a discovery interview. Useful if you need to plan an actual conversation, not just understand the theory.
- [**Qualitative Interviews Tipsheet**](https://dism.duke.edu/files/2020/05/Tipsheet-Qualitative_Interviews.pdf) (PDF) — Duke Initiative on Survey Methodology. A concise reference covering practical tips for planning and running qualitative interviews. Useful as a quick checklist before and during interview work.

**Writing patterns you may need to use**

Different pieces of a deliverable have different conventions. A findings piece is built around evidence and themes. An implementation piece is built around feasibility and sequencing. A risks piece is built around honest acknowledgment of what could go wrong. An executive summary is built around the few things a busy reader most needs to know. A strategic recommendation is built around a clear direction supported by reasoning. If you've never written one of these before, look up two or three examples from comparable professional deliverables before you draft. Most of these conventions are learnable in 30 minutes of reading examples.

**Using AI to bridge a skill gap**

For methods or piece types you've never used before, AI is a useful thinking partner. Try a prompt like *"I need to prepare a [risks section / executive summary / strategic recommendation] for a community engagement strategy project. What are the conventions for this kind of piece, what mistakes do first-time writers usually make, and what does a strong example look like in plain language?"* The intellectual work of applying what you get to your own piece stays with you, but you don't have to start from a blank page.

## How much is enough?

The most common preparation mistake at this stage is doing too much. Preparation is bounded by the piece's purpose. Your piece doesn't need to be exhaustive on its topic. It needs to be sufficient for what it's trying to argue or demonstrate.

Two practical tests:

- **Can you answer your piece's core questions?** Look back at the questions you listed in the first step. If you have a working answer for each one (even if it's one you might revise when you draft), you're ready to move on.
- **Are you still learning new things?** If you've spent an hour preparing and the new material is just confirming what you already have, you're past the point of useful return.

Set a soft time budget before you start. There's space allocated for independent preparation in this week's project work. Treat it as a ceiling, not a target.

---

## Capture notes so drafting is easier later

How you take notes now determines how hard the next step is. A few habits worth building:

- **Capture by idea, not by source.** Group what you find around the questions you're trying to answer, rather than around which source or line of thinking it came from. This makes the next step (turning notes into an outline) much faster.
- **Keep citations attached.** Even informal notes should record where each piece of information came from. You'll need this when you draft, and reconstructing it later is painful.
- **Mark relevance.** As you go, flag what's directly useful for your piece and what's interesting but tangential. Tangential material is fine to keep. It just shouldn't crowd out the central material when you sit down to draft.

If you've followed these habits, the next page (From research to draft) describes how to turn your notes into an outline.

## What this looks like in practice

Two teams, two different project types, two different ways this week plays out.

### Team 1: User research project

**Project:** A regional transit authority wants to understand why ridership among younger commuters has dropped over the past two years.

**Deliverable:** A user research report (~20 pages). Pieces: Context, User Research Findings, Recommendations, and Methodology & Executive Summary.

**This week:**

- **Findings owner.** Drafts the interview protocol with team input, recruits younger commuters, runs 3 of 5 interviews. Heaviest individual workload this week.
- **Context owner.** Contributes to the protocol conversation, then independently drafts the context piece from Discovery materials, since it doesn't depend on findings.
- **Recommendations owner.** Can't draft recommendations yet — there are no findings to recommend from. Helps shape the interview protocol and runs 1 interview to be close to the data.
- **Methodology & Executive Summary owner.** Recruits the remaining interview participants and runs 1 interview. Documents the protocol and recruitment approach as draft methodology content.

### Team 2: Strategy project

**Project:** A small B2B software company is deciding whether to expand from the Canadian market into the US Northeast and needs a clear recommendation for the board.

**Deliverable:** A market entry strategy recommendation (~15 pages plus appendix). Pieces: Market Opportunity Analysis, Risks & Constraints, Prioritized Recommendation, and Implementation Roadmap.

**This week:**

- **Market Opportunity Analysis owner.** Pulls together US Northeast market data from Discovery, benchmarks two or three comparable SaaS expansions, and drafts a working version of the opportunity picture. Heaviest individual workload this week.
- **Recommendation owner.** Can't draft a recommendation until the opportunity analysis is firm. Uses part of the standing team meeting to facilitate a synthesis conversation, captures the candidate strategic framings the team has been discussing, and contributes directly to the opportunity analysis.
- **Risks & Constraints owner.** Contributes to the opportunity analysis, where constraint-thinking sharpens the picture. Starts a candidate-risks list to bring to the team next week, once a recommendation is on the table.
- **Implementation Roadmap owner.** The most downstream piece — depends on a recommendation that doesn't exist yet. Contributes to the opportunity analysis and supports the recommendation owner with the synthesis conversation.

### The pattern

Notice what these two teams have in common. On both, one or two members are doing the heavy upstream work this week. The others are contributing to that work, not running their own parallel tracks. In later weeks, as different pieces become the active work, the contribution flows the other way. That's how development teams actually move forward.
