# Formatting professional deliverables

How a deliverable is presented shapes how its content is received. Clean, consistent presentation signals that its authors are organized and professional. Inconsistent presentation signals the opposite, often before a reader has spent much time with the content itself.

We're sharing this guidance at the outset of your production phase deliberately. The presentation habits you build now will persist through the rest of the project, and the internal review gate later in the course catches presentation problems but doesn't prevent them. Fixing inconsistent formatting under deadline pressure is significantly more disruptive than building good habits from the start.

Most of what follows focuses on written deliverables, since reports and similar documents are the most common form projects take. If your team is producing a deck, a prototype, a video, or another format, the same principles apply: consistency, clarity, hierarchy, and shared standards across team members. The section near the end of this page covers what those principles look like for non-document deliverables.

---

## Why presentation matters from the start

Readers form judgments about quality from visual hierarchy, white space, and consistency before they engage deeply with the content. For a team-authored deliverable, inconsistent presentation communicates something specific: that the pieces were produced independently and never integrated. That tends to be the experience the reader has, regardless of how good the underlying thinking was.

Two rubric dimensions are directly affected. Communication (which includes clarity, organization, and professional presentation) is worth 15% of your final grade. Deliverable Quality (which assesses completeness, scope alignment, and depth of development) is worth 25%. Presentation is the infrastructure both dimensions depend on, so it's worth getting right from the start.

## Standards for written deliverables

These are the conventions that most professional written deliverables (reports, briefs, plans, strategies) follow. Departures from them should have a reason.

**Headings and hierarchy**

Use a consistent heading structure throughout. H1 for the document title, H2 for major sections, H3 for subsections. Don't use a heading level for visual emphasis if it doesn't represent a structural relationship. Using H2 because you want something to look important, rather than because it's a major section, breaks the logic of the hierarchy and confuses navigation.

**White space**

Separate paragraphs with space. Walls of text signal that a document hasn't been edited for readability. Keep paragraphs short. Four to six lines is a reasonable target, and if a paragraph is running longer, look for the natural break.

**Tables**

Use tables to compare multiple items across consistent attributes. For a single list, a list is cleaner. Every table needs a brief descriptive title placed above it. Without one, the reader has to infer what they're looking at.

If you haven't built a professional table before, you don't need to design one from scratch. [**Google Docs**](https://docs.google.com) (free) has built-in table styles under Format > Table styles. Apply one to any table you insert and it will look clean without manual formatting. [**Canva**](https://www.canva.com) (free) also has comparison table templates in its template library. Both are free with no paid subscription required.

**Charts and visuals**

Every chart needs a title and clear axis labels. If a visual came from another source, cite it. Same standard as written content. Before including a visual, ask whether it communicates something more clearly than a well-written sentence or two would. If not, the sentence is probably the better choice.

Two free tools worth knowing:

- [**Datawrapper**](https://www.datawrapper.de): free, no account required. Paste in your data, choose a chart type, and it produces a clean, publication-quality chart you can download as a PNG. Takes about five minutes for a basic bar or line chart.
- [**Canva Graphs**](https://www.canva.com/graphs/): free tier. More visual control than Datawrapper, with pre-built chart templates you can customize. Useful if you want more design flexibility.

[**Google Sheets**](https://sheets.google.com) (free with any Google account) also produces serviceable charts if your data is already there. Apply one of the built-in themes to avoid the default styling.

**Callout boxes**

Useful for key findings, important caveats, or information a reader might want to reference without reading the surrounding section. Use them sparingly. If you call out everything, nothing stands out.

**Terminology and style**

Decide on terms before drafting begins and coordinate across the team. If the employer calls their product a "platform," use "platform" throughout, rather than mixing in "tool," "system," or "solution." Inconsistent terminology can read as careless and can also raise questions for a professional reader about whether the authors understand what they're describing.

The [Neuron tools directory](https://www.theneuron.ai/top-tools/) from earlier in the course is worth revisiting now that you're in production. The productivity and content creation categories both include tools relevant to deliverable work: chart builders, table generators, and formatting aids worth browsing.

## Standards for other deliverable formats

If your team is producing something other than a written document, the principles above still apply: visual hierarchy, consistency across team members' contributions, agreed terminology, sparing use of emphasis. What changes is how those principles show up in your specific format.

**Slide decks**

A deck has its own hierarchy. Slide titles function like H2 headings, and a consistent title style across slides matters as much as consistent headings in a document. Use a single template or master slide so layouts, fonts, and colors don't drift between sections. Common pitfalls include too much text per slide, mixing aspect ratios or background colors, and varying chart styles between slides built by different team members. Free starting points include [**Google Slides**](https://slides.google.com) templates, [**Canva** presentation templates](https://www.canva.com/presentations/templates/), and [**Slidesgo**](https://slidesgo.com) for free editable PowerPoint and Google Slides templates.

**Visual deliverables (process maps, infographics, prototypes)**

Consistency in shape libraries, color coding, and labeling conventions is what separates a professional visual from one that looks like an early sketch. Decide on a color palette and stick to it. Label everything that needs a label. A process map without consistent step labels is hard to follow even if the logic is correct. [**Figma**](https://www.figma.com) (free for individuals), [**Miro**](https://miro.com) (free tier), Canva, and [**Lucidchart**](https://www.lucidchart.com) (free tier) all support team collaboration and shared style libraries.

**Video**

A short video deliverable benefits from a consistent visual style across scenes: title cards, lower thirds, transitions, captions. If different team members are producing different scenes, agree on these elements before anyone records. Captions and a clean audio mix matter more than fancy editing. Canva and [**Lumen5**](https://lumen5.com) offer free templates for simple branded videos.

**Mixed-format packages**

Many projects produce more than one artifact: a report and a one-page summary, or a strategy doc and sample assets. When that's the case, treat the package as a whole. Shared color palette, shared terminology, shared core messaging across pieces, even when the formats differ. A reader who picks up any one piece should recognize it as part of the same body of work.

## Accessibility

Professional deliverables should be usable by people who navigate them in different ways: with a screen reader, with assistive zoom, with limited color perception, with captions instead of audio. Many accessibility practices are also just good design and make the work clearer for everyone. A few high-impact habits to build now:

**Use real headings, not styled text.** Heading levels aren't only for visual hierarchy. Screen readers use them to navigate the document. A "title" that's just bold text in a larger font won't be picked up; a properly tagged H2 will. Most word processors and slide tools have built-in heading or title styles. Use them.

**Check color contrast.** Text needs enough contrast against its background to be readable. The standard most professional organizations follow is WCAG AA: 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text. Light gray on white, light blue on darker blue, or yellow text on a busy background image will all fail. The free [**WebAIM Contrast Checker**](https://webaim.org/resources/contrastchecker/) takes a foreground and background color and tells you whether they meet the standard.

**Don't rely on color alone.** If you color-code something (categories in a chart, status in a table, types of steps in a process map), make sure the meaning is also conveyed by a label, icon, or pattern. A reader with color blindness, or anyone reading a printed black-and-white copy, should still be able to understand what each color represents.

**Add alt text to meaningful visuals.** Charts, infographics, and photos that carry information need a brief text description of what the visual shows. Decorative visuals don't need alt text. Word, Google Docs, and most slide tools have a built-in alt text field. Right-click the image and look for "Alt text" or "Description."

**Use descriptive link text.** "Click here" and "this article" tell a screen reader user nothing about where the link goes. "WebAIM's contrast checker" or "the Final Deliverables rubric" do.

**Caption your video.** If your deliverable includes video, include captions. They make the content accessible to deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers and to anyone watching without sound. Most video tools (Canva, Lumen5, YouTube, even iPhone Photos) can auto-generate captions; review them for accuracy before delivery.

## Using tools to review your work

These tools don't replace judgment. They catch mechanical problems so your attention goes to the decisions that actually require it.

**AI for consistency review**

The formatting checklist below is the strongest input you can give an AI tool for this kind of review. Two prompts worth using before you submit your piece or merge with teammates:

*Reviewing your own piece against the checklist:* Paste your draft into an AI tool along with the formatting checklist from this page, and ask: "Review my draft against this checklist. For each item, tell me whether it's met, partially met, or not met, and where you can, give me a specific example of any issue." AI is well-suited to this kind of mechanical pattern-matching, and the checklist gives it a clear standard to apply rather than its own opinion.

*Reviewing across team contributions:* Before merging, paste two or three team members' sections into an AI tool and ask: "These were written by different people on the same team. Identify the most noticeable inconsistencies between them in tone, terminology, formatting, structure, and accessibility, and suggest how the team should harmonize them." Catching these before assembly is significantly easier than fixing them after.

If your deliverable isn't a written document, you can still use both prompts on the text content: slide titles and bullets, video scripts or captions, or the labels and copy from a prototype.

**Other tools**

- [**Grammarly**](https://www.grammarly.com) or [**Hemingway Editor**](https://hemingwayapp.com) (both free tiers available): catch readability issues, passive voice, and sentences that have grown too long to follow.
- **Google Docs spelling and grammar checker:** basic but reliably catches obvious errors before submission.
- **Google Docs Outline view:** opens in the left panel and shows your heading hierarchy at a glance. Useful for confirming structure before you hand a piece off.
- **Word's Navigation Pane:** the equivalent in Microsoft Word. Also lets you verify heading levels across a merged document before submission.

## Formatting checklist

Use this before submitting your piece to the shared workspace, and again when the team merges work for delivery. Some items below are specific to written deliverables; adapt them as needed for your format.

**Structure and hierarchy**

- [ ] Heading or title hierarchy is consistent throughout (no levels skipped)
- [ ] No heading or title is used purely for visual emphasis without representing a structural relationship
- [ ] Headings use real heading styles, not just bold or larger text
- [ ] Each section ends with a transition or bridge to what follows; no abrupt stops

**Visual consistency**

- [ ] Paragraph or block spacing is consistent throughout
- [ ] Paragraphs are six lines or fewer; longer paragraphs are split at natural breaks
- [ ] The deliverable uses the same fonts and sizes throughout
- [ ] Page margins or slide layouts are consistent
- [ ] All team members' contributions use the same styles (verify before merging)
- [ ] Running headers, footers, or slide footers, if used, are consistent throughout
- [ ] Page numbers or slide numbers are present where appropriate

**Tables, charts, and visuals**

- [ ] All tables have descriptive titles placed above them
- [ ] All charts have titles and clearly labeled axes
- [ ] All data sources are cited for visuals and statistics
- [ ] Callout boxes or visual emphasis are used sparingly (no more than one per section)

**Language and style**

- [ ] Terminology is consistent throughout (coordinate with your team before producing)
- [ ] All lists use parallel grammatical structure (all items begin with the same part of speech)
- [ ] Capitalization is consistent within headings and lists
- [ ] Acronyms are defined on first use
- [ ] Numbers under ten are spelled out; numbers 10 and above use numerals (unless a different convention applies to your deliverable type)

**Accessibility**

- [ ] Color contrast meets accessibility standards (WCAG AA: 4.5:1 for body text)
- [ ] Color is not the only way meaning is conveyed in charts, tables, and visuals
- [ ] Meaningful visuals have alt text; link text describes the destination
- [ ] Any video content includes captions

**Final review**

- [ ] Piece has been run through an editing tool (Grammarly, Hemingway, or AI consistency check) before submitting
- [ ] The deliverable has been reviewed in print preview, PDF view, or full-screen presentation view before submission
