# Articulating your experience

Every job application, interview, graduate school essay, and LinkedIn update you write from here will ask you some version of the same question: what have you done, and what can you do? The answer most learners default to — "I worked on a group project in a course" — is the answer that does the least work. The answer this module builds is the one that turns the project into a professional credential rather than a line on a transcript.

This is the last module in the course, and it is forward-facing by design. The competencies the course has built — across communication, professionalism, critical thinking, teamwork, and career and self-development — are real, and they travel to the next professional context you enter. The articulation skill is what makes those competencies visible to the next employer, graduate program, or professional network you engage with. A strong project poorly described reads as a minor credential. The same project described well reads as evidence of professional capability.

The rest of this page is how to describe it well.

## What a strong description actually sounds like

[VIDEO: From "I helped" to "I led"]

The video walks through a concrete before-and-after example of the same project described two different ways. Same work, same role, same outcome. Very different impression.

The weak version sounds like this: "I worked on a group project for a credit union. We did some research and made recommendations."

The strong version sounds like this: "I led the market research workstream for a regional credit union, translating three months of customer interview data into a strategic recommendations deck that informed the client's membership strategy heading into the next fiscal year."

What changed between the two is not the work. The work is the same. What changed is three specific moves, which are the moves underneath every strong description of project experience:

- **Specificity.** Not "a project" but "the market research workstream for a regional credit union." Not "some research" but "three months of customer interview data." The specifics are what make the description read as real.
- **Ownership.** Not "we did" but "I led." Not "we made" but "I translated." Naming your role specifically signals that you know what your contribution was. Vague collective language ("we," "the team") in a professional context reads as not having owned a piece of the work.
- **Outcome.** Not "we made recommendations" but "a strategic recommendations deck that informed the client's membership strategy." The description names what the work produced, not just what the work was.

All three moves apply to any format of project work. The specific language changes depending on whether you worked on a research report, a content strategy, a campaign brief, a prototype, a training module, or a mixed-format deliverable. The underlying moves are the same.

### A quick before-and-after

**Weak:**
> Helped out on a group consulting project for a nonprofit. Did research and put together a report.

**Strong:**
> Partnered with a community nonprofit on a donor engagement strategy, leading the competitive landscape analysis and drafting two of the five sections of the final recommendations report.

Same project. Different impression. The first sentence could describe a two-week effort by someone who skimmed the material. The second describes a semester of substantive professional work.

## Articulating for different contexts

The three moves — specificity, ownership, outcome — apply everywhere. What changes from context to context is the length and register.

### Resume bullets

Short. Outcome-first when possible. One line each. Lead with the verb, name the scope, name the result. Two or three bullets per project is typical. A resume bullet is not a complete description; it is a hook that earns a longer conversation.

A usable bullet tends to follow a shape like *action — scope — result*. For the credit union project: "Led market research workstream for a regional credit union (3-month engagement), synthesizing customer interview data into a strategic recommendations deck that informed the client's membership strategy."

If the outcome is not yet known — the employer has not published the decisions they made based on the work — describe what the work produced, not what the client did with it. "Developed strategic recommendations informed by three months of customer research" is still a legitimate outcome statement.

### Interview answers

Longer than a resume bullet, shorter than a reflection. Usually structured around a specific moment, with enough detail that the interviewer can picture the work. The STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is one common format, but the goal is not to follow a template. The goal is to tell a short, specific, professional story.

When you are asked "tell me about a project you worked on" or "describe a time you had to handle [X]," a strong answer has a specific shape: a minute or so of context (what the client needed, what your team was asked to do), a minute or so of action (what you specifically did, not what the team did collectively), and a short outcome (what the work produced, and what you took away from it). The answer does not need to be dramatic. It needs to be specific.

The defend-your-work standard from the Final Presentation applies in interviews too. Whatever you say you did, you should be able to speak to in more detail if asked. "Led the market research workstream" invites the follow-up question "what did that actually involve?" — and a strong answer to that follow-up is where the interview is often won.

### LinkedIn and networking

Somewhere in between resume and interview. Narrative enough to read as a real person's experience, specific enough to signal real skill. A strong LinkedIn project description tends to be two to four sentences — enough to name the client's sector, what you and the team produced, and what you took from the experience in a transferable form.

One thing to check before you post or describe the project publicly: what your employer is comfortable with you naming. Most are fine with their sector being referenced ("a regional credit union," "a mid-sized nonprofit in the community housing space") but some prefer not to have their name used directly. When in doubt, describe the sector and the work rather than the specific client.

## Using AI for professional self-presentation

This is the last AI application the course introduces. It is also one of the most useful beyond this course.

### What this use case is

Using AI to strengthen how you describe your own work. Specifically: sharpening resume bullets, practicing interview responses, deciding which accomplishments to lead with for a specific role or context. Each of those tasks is a translation task — you already have the raw material (the work you actually did), and the task is turning it into language the next professional context will recognize.

### Why this is different from AI-assisted reflection

AI-assisted drafting is off-limits for reflections because the reflection is about identifying what the experience produced in you. Only you can do that work. Professional self-presentation is different. The learning has already happened. The task is packaging and communicating it. A learner who uses AI to turn "I helped with a project for a credit union" into the stronger version is articulating what they actually did, not fabricating what they did not do.

### Prompt strategies that tend to work

Three patterns that produce useful output. All of them rest on the principle you have been applying all semester: AI is a drafting partner, not a generator.

- **Sharpening a draft bullet.** "Here is a draft resume bullet describing a project I worked on. Strengthen it by making the scope, my specific role, and the outcome more concrete — without fabricating anything. Ask me questions if you need details to make the bullet stronger."
- **Selecting which accomplishments to lead with.** "Here are three accomplishments from a recent project. I am applying for [role in sector X]. Which one should I lead with for this role, and why? What should I emphasize about each in the application?"
- **Practicing interview responses.** "Interview question: tell me about a time you worked on a team that disagreed. Here is my draft answer. Where is it weak? What would a stronger version include?"

The pattern across all three: you provide the substance. AI pushes on the framing.

### The one boundary

AI should not fabricate accomplishments, credentials, or outcomes. The defend-your-work standard applies here the same way it applied in the Final Presentation. If a resume bullet names an outcome, you should be able to speak to how that outcome was measured. If an interview answer describes a contribution, you should be able to describe it in more detail when asked. An AI-generated bullet that names something you did not do will not survive contact with a real interview.
