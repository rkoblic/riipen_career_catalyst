# GRADED: End-of-Project Peer Evaluation

The evaluation you write for each teammate is the last piece of feedback they carry out of this project. Generic feedback ("good teammate, easy to work with") tells them nothing. Specific behavioral feedback, honestly given, is something a colleague can act on months or years after the fact. The professional skill this evaluation builds is writing feedback that is specific enough to be useful and honest enough to be credible, calibrated across the full project arc rather than the last thing you remember. When you are later asked to write feedback on a coworker in a performance review or a 360, evidence-based feedback will already be how you write.

This is the second of two peer evaluations in the course, parallel to the [**Mid-Project Peer Evaluation**](../week08/page2-peer-evaluation-1.md) you completed earlier. The instrument is the same (Continue/Start/Adjust qualitative feedback plus BARS ratings on the same four dimensions), but the scope expands to the full fifteen-week arc. It is a required individual deliverable, and your ratings of each teammate become one of the five graded items that together represent **Professional Engagement** in your final grade — see [**How Professional Engagement Is Assessed**](https://riipen.instructure.com/courses/107/pages/graded-professional-engagement) for the full picture. Your specific ratings are not shared back with teammates: qualitative feedback is anonymized, BARS ratings come back as a per-dimension average, and the closure questions stay with your instructor.

The **End-of-Project Self-Evaluation** is a separate, parallel survey on the Riipen platform where you rate yourself on the same four BARS dimensions. Complete it the same week as this peer evaluation. It is not graded and does not affect your teammates' scores; your instructor uses it to flag moments where the self-view and the peer view diverge meaningfully.

---

## The assignment

The End-of-Project Peer Evaluation has three parts, using the same framework as the Mid-Project Peer Evaluation.

- **Part A: Qualitative feedback.** Three prompts per teammate using Continue/Start/Adjust, each with at least two sentences referencing specific behavior.
- **Part B: BARS ratings.** Four dimensions per teammate (Reliability, Contribution, Constructive Engagement, Accountability), each with a specific behavioral evidence example.
- **Part C: Closure questions.** Two short questions about team dynamics, visible only to your instructor.

Every rating requires specific behavioral evidence. If you are uncertain between two ratings on a dimension, select the lower one and explain why in your evidence.

---

## How you'll be assessed

This is one of five graded items that together make up Professional Engagement — see [**How Professional Engagement Is Assessed**](https://riipen.instructure.com/courses/107/pages/graded-professional-engagement) for the full picture.

There is no "correct" rating to give a teammate; the standard is that your ratings are grounded in specific behavioral observations and that your evidence supports them. Ratings without evidence, or with evidence that describes impression rather than observation, are flagged for instructor review.

---

## How to do it well

### Evaluating contributions that evolved over time

Teammates' contributions change across a fifteen-week project. This is normal, and it is also the part of the evaluation where credibility and usefulness get made or lost. Three patterns tend to show up:

- **The teammate who started slowly and stepped up.** Someone who was quiet or uncertain in the early weeks and became a substantial contributor in the Development or Refinement phase is not the same as a teammate who coasted throughout. A strong evaluation captures both the slow start and the step up, because both are part of the story.
- **The teammate who led early and faded.** Someone who was organized and active in the first half of the project and became harder to reach or less responsive in the final weeks. The team's early impression is usually more positive than the late-project reality. The evaluation should describe the arc honestly, including what changed and roughly when.
- **The teammate who was steady throughout.** Easy to under-rate because there is no single moment to point to. A strong evaluation for a steady teammate names the consistency itself as a specific behavior: showed up prepared to every meeting, delivered agreed work on time across the whole project, raised concerns early rather than late.

In each case, the rating and the evidence need to reflect the full arc, not the endpoint. Scan the project phase by phase (early formation weeks, the production push in the middle, refinement and final deliverables, presentation preparation) and note at least one specific behavior per phase for each teammate. If you find a phase where you have no specific evidence for a teammate, that is useful information — it may mean the teammate was in the background that phase, or it may mean you were.

### What counts as behavioral evidence

Behavioral evidence is an observable action and its effect on the team's work.

- **Not evidence — Impression:** "I felt like he was engaged."
- **Evidence — Observable action and its effect:** "He circled back to his own draft after two teammates flagged the same structural issue and revised it within a day."

### Using this week's experiences

If you started early in Week 14, you should already have evidence gathered across the project arc. This week is for writing the evaluation itself, with the rehearsal, Final Presentation, and team closing now available as your final sources of evidence. If you took notes during any of those events, reread them before you start rating.

- **The rehearsal** surfaced how teammates handle feedback under pressure, whether they delivered on commitments between the outline and the deck, and how they showed up to a first full run-through. Often a clearer signal on Constructive Engagement and Contribution than earlier phases were.
- **The Final Presentation** surfaced who did what they said they would do, how handoffs worked, who handled Q&A well and who deferred, and how the team coordinated under live pressure. Evidence on Reliability, Contribution, and Accountability.
- **The team closing** surfaced individual acknowledgments. What your teammates chose to name about each other often captures patterns that did not have a single moment to point to across the earlier weeks (the steady contributor, the quiet teammate who carried more than was visible in real time).

### Completing each part well

**Part A: Qualitative feedback.** The standard is the same as the Mid-Project evaluation; what shifts is the time horizon. Your Mid-Project feedback landed while your teammate still had half the project to act on it. Your End-of-Project feedback lands when the project is over and becomes most useful in their next team. Feedback pitched at that level is what transfers: behavior described in terms that would still make sense to a teammate you never worked with. If evidence on a particular prompt is thin, name the thinness rather than padding.

**Part B: BARS ratings with behavioral evidence.** One rating per dimension per teammate, each supported by a specific behavioral example. A strong evidence prompt names what happened, when it happened (roughly), and what effect it had on the team's work. A weak one describes how you felt about the teammate. The scale descriptors are unchanged from the Mid-Project evaluation; that page is the reference if you want to revisit what separates a 4 from a 3, a 3 from a 2.

**Part C: Closure questions.** Two brief questions visible only to your instructor. These capture patterns or observations about team dynamics that the team should not have to negotiate but that the instructor can use as context for grading and future cohort design. Keep your answers brief and specific. If nothing of note happened, a one-line "nothing of note" is a fine answer.

**End-of-Project Self-Evaluation (separate survey).** Rate yourself on the same four BARS dimensions. Submit it the same week as this peer evaluation. Behavioral evidence is encouraged though not required. Self-evaluation tends to distort in two directions: over-rating, usually defensive, shows up as 4s across the board without supporting evidence; under-rating, often a modesty reflex, shows up when the self-rating is noticeably lower than the teammates' ratings. The self-evaluation is not graded and does not affect your teammates' scores. Your instructor uses it to flag moments where the self-view and the peer view diverge meaningfully. The ratings hardest to give yourself honestly are usually the most informative.

## Using AI

The same boundary as the Mid-Project evaluation applies.

**AI is permitted for organizational work.** Taking your notes on a teammate and asking AI to help sort them by BARS dimension, flag where evidence is thin, or point out inconsistencies between your qualitative feedback and your ratings is fair use. Strip names and other identifying details out of your notes first. "This teammate" or "Teammate A" is enough for the sort to work, and keeps a colleague's behavior out of a third-party service's logs. Pasting a real person's name into an LLM alongside an evaluation of them is the kind of thing many workplace AI policies prohibit, and it is worth treating with the same caution here.

A prompt that works once you have anonymized notes on a teammate's behavior:

> Here are my notes on this teammate from across the project. Help me sort these into the four BARS dimensions (Reliability, Contribution, Constructive Engagement, Accountability). For each dimension, flag whether the evidence is strong enough to support a specific rating, or whether I need to think more before rating.

Read the AI's sort critically. It can miss nuance about whether a specific behavior belongs in Contribution or Constructive Engagement, and the judgment is yours.

**AI cannot generate the evaluation itself.** The observations, ratings, qualitative feedback, and closure answers must all reflect your genuine assessment. The learning here happens in the act of evaluating — thinking carefully about what you observed and figuring out how to say it usefully. AI-generated evaluations bypass that process and produce feedback your teammate cannot act on.

## Submission

**Submission:** Individually through the Riipen platform. 

**AI disclosure:** Required. A one-line statement describing any AI use is sufficient. If AI was not used, a one-line statement to that effect is fine.

---

## What you'll receive

Feedback arrives after the course ends. Do not expect to see it before the course closes.

You'll see anonymized versions of Parts A and B: the Continue/Start/Adjust comments your teammates wrote about you (anonymized — you see what was said but not who said it), and your averaged score on each of the four BARS dimensions. The closure questions (Part C) and your separate self-evaluation stay with your instructor.

Unlike the Mid-Project evaluation, there is no structured team processing session for this feedback. It arrives after the course has closed and the team has dispersed. Read it alone. The observations that converge across multiple teammates are the most reliable signal — start there.

---

The habit of writing feedback that is specific and credible across the full project is something you will use in every team you join from here.
