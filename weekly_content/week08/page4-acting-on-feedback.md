# Acting on feedback during production

Once your team is in production, feedback arrives constantly. It comes from employer check-ins, async messages between meetings, internal team review, and your own observations as the work develops. Most of it is small and easy to absorb. Some of it asks your team to shift direction in a way that takes time, coordination, or rethinking. This page is what to do when that happens.

In any batch of feedback you receive, most items are already sorted for you by the way they came in. The answers to the specific questions you brought into a meeting are mostly act-on by default. That was the point of asking. The triage matters most for everything else: observations the employer offered beyond your questions, tangents that came up, comments that were opinions rather than direction. For those, your team needs to decide what's worth acting on, what's worth noting, and what to consciously set aside.

---

## Three categories

Before you change anything in your work, sort each piece of feedback into one of three buckets:

| Category | What it is | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Act on** | Feedback that changes what you produce: scope corrections, missing analysis, clear direction changes | Add it to your revision plan with an owner and target date |
| **Note** | Context that informs but doesn't change the current work: organizational background, upcoming changes, validation of your approach | Log it in your project record so it's available when relevant |
| **Set aside** | Scope-adjacent preferences, tentative suggestions, or comments that conflict with the project brief | Document the decision so the team can see it was a deliberate choice |

A few examples of how this sorts in practice:

A community outreach team's employer says: "Your target population section focuses on adults 25 to 45, but we've actually been trying to reach older residents. The 25 to 45 group is secondary." Scope correction. **Act on** before drafting continues.

A process improvement team's employer says: "We're piloting a new ticketing system in one department. It's not rolled out yet, but it might change the workflow in six months." Useful context for recommendations. **Note**.

A social media strategy team's employer says: "Personally, I've always liked the idea of a weekly newsletter, but that's just me." A preference outside the project brief. **Set aside**.

---

## Build a quick revision plan

Once you've identified your act-on items, convert them into a concrete plan before anyone starts revising. A simple format is enough:

| Feedback item | Source | Revision required | Owner | Target date |

Two practices that protect the team during a production-heavy week:

**Assign one owner per item.** When a whole team owns something, no individual is on the hook for it getting done. Someone needs to be accountable for each revision, even if other teammates contribute.

**Decide what kind of revision it is before estimating effort.** Some revisions are bounded: a citation needs to be added, a section opening needs to be tightened, a piece of data needs to be updated. Others reach further into the work: the main argument of a section needs to change, a finding the team built recommendations on turns out to be wrong, the logic linking several sections together needs rethinking. Those two situations need very different amounts of time and team coordination.

A quick test for distinguishing them: if you made this change and didn't tell any of your teammates, would they notice a problem in their own sections? If yes, the revision is structural and needs to be flagged before anyone starts. If no, the section owner can usually handle it on their own.

A process improvement team whose employer says "the current-state workflow you've described doesn't match how the team actually operates in Q1" is looking at a structural change. Almost everything built on that workflow needs revisiting. The same team whose employer says "the statistic you cited for error rates is from 2021, and we have more recent data I can send" is looking at a targeted change. One person updates one figure.

Most teams underestimate the structural revisions and overestimate the targeted ones. A section that needs to be reargued from the beginning is not a quick fix, and treating it like one is how teams end up with a partially revised document the day before it's due. When the team is unsure which kind a particular revision is, a five-minute conversation tends to be worth more than the hour spent revising in the wrong direction.

## Connect it to the Change Log

Each act-on row of your revision plan becomes a Change Log entry once the revision is done. The Change Log your team started after revisiting the Final Deliverable is the place where these get tracked.
