# AI as a production partner

Earlier in the course, AI was most useful as a research tool. It helped you scope unfamiliar territory and stress-test your approach. In the production phase, its most useful role shifts. AI becomes a production partner: a tool for reviewing and tightening work you've already built.

This is a different use than drafting from scratch. The value comes from asking AI to evaluate logic and structure: places where the argument is underdeveloped, where transitions are missing, where a claim isn't backed by the evidence in the same section.

---

## What AI is well suited to in this phase

**Structural review of a section you've drafted**

Paste a section and ask: "Identify any places where the argument is weak, the transitions are unclear, or the evidence doesn't support the claim being made." This mirrors what a good editor does: read for logic and structure, not just for sentences.

**Gap identification after revision**

After making changes based on employer feedback, paste the revised section and ask: "I've revised this section based on feedback that [describe the feedback]. Does the revision address the original concern? What, if anything, still needs attention?" This is most useful when the feedback was directional but not specific about exactly what to change.

**Self-editing before handing off**

Before sending a section to a teammate or merging into the shared document, ask: "Flag any sentences in this paragraph that are longer than they need to be or that repeat what the previous sentence already said." This catches the kind of prose bloat that accumulates during drafting and is easy to miss when you're close to your own work.

**Catching AI-isms in AI-assisted work**

If you've used AI to help draft or refine a section, it often leaves fingerprints: em dashes everywhere, "not X but Y" constructions, parallel triplets ("clear, concise, and compelling"), and closers that sound more confident than the evidence warrants. Ask AI to find them: "Scan this paragraph for phrasing patterns that read as AI-generated. Look for em dashes, contrastive 'X isn't Y, it's Z' constructions, rhetorical triplets, and hyperbolic closers. Flag each and suggest a more human revision." Fixing the flags by hand is usually faster than you'd expect, and reading them sharpens your eye for the next draft.

---

## Working with AI well in this phase

**Use more than one model for feedback.** Different AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot) are trained differently and have different default styles. Pasting the same section into two or three of them and asking for structural review produces a richer read than any single one. You don't have to act on everything each suggests. Some suggestions will contradict each other, and some will be cosmetic changes that don't actually improve the work. Use your judgment. You can even ask one model to help you think through which of another's suggestions are worth acting on.

**Give the model permission to say nothing.** LLMs are built to be helpful, which means they'll almost always find something to critique even if the writing is solid. If you paste in a strong paragraph with no major issues and ask "what should I change?" you'll get a list of changes anyway. When you want the honest answer, give the model explicit permission to pass: "If this paragraph is fine as-is, say so rather than suggesting changes."

---

## What AI can't do in this phase

AI cannot tell you whether a revision addresses the employer's actual concern. It wasn't at the meeting. If you ask AI to evaluate your revised section without explaining what the original feedback was and why you made the changes you did, you'll get a generic quality review, not a meaningful assessment of whether the feedback was addressed.

The standard is the same as earlier in the course: if you can explain and defend the work, it's yours. If AI produced something you can't account for, it doesn't belong in your deliverable.
