# Processing feedback and revision

The overview video introduces the core question: for each piece of feedback, what would actually change in your deliverable if you acted on it? This module gives that question a structure you can use in your team's response planning session — a three-category framework that turns a feedback conversation into a concrete revision plan.

## Sorting employer feedback

Sort feedback into three categories:

**Act on** — feedback that changes what you produce. This includes corrections to scope (you've covered the wrong thing), gaps in your analysis (you've missed something important), and direction changes the employer signaled clearly ("I'd want to see more on X"). Act-on feedback takes priority. Assign an owner and a deadline for each item before your team disperses.

**Note** — feedback that informs but doesn't change the current work. This includes context the employer shared about their organization, observations about the broader landscape, and commentary that validates your current approach. Note it in your project record — it may become relevant later, especially when you're writing the Change Log.

**Set aside** — feedback that doesn't apply to your project's scope or your team's deliverable, preferences that conflict with more authoritative direction from the project brief, or comments the employer offered tentatively that you've decided not to pursue. Consciously setting this aside (rather than quietly ignoring it) keeps your decision-making visible to the team.

---

**What each category looks like in practice:**

*Act on — a community outreach team hears: "Your target population section focuses on adults 25–45, but we've actually been trying to reach older residents who are more established in the neighborhood. The 25–45 group is secondary for this initiative." This changes the scope of a section. It needs to be acted on before drafting continues.*

*Note — a process improvement team hears: "We've actually been piloting a new ticketing system in one department — it's not rolled out yet, but it might change how the workflow looks in about six months." This is useful context for the recommendations section. It doesn't require a revision now, but it belongs in the team's project record.*

*Set aside — a social media strategy team hears: "Personally, I've always liked the idea of a weekly newsletter, but that's just me." If the project brief focuses on social channels and the employer hasn't formally expanded the scope, this is a preference to acknowledge and set aside, not an instruction to act on.*

## Building a revision plan

Sorting feedback is useful only if it leads to a concrete plan. A revision plan converts your act-on items into specific tasks with owners and target dates.

Three things that make a revision plan actually work:

**Assign one owner per item.** "The whole team" owns nothing. If a feedback item touches a section that one person is primarily responsible for, that person owns the revision.

**Distinguish revision type before you estimate time.** A targeted edit takes an hour. A structural revision takes a day. Knowing which you're dealing with before you start protects the production schedule. The next page covers this distinction in detail.

**Note it in your Change Log.** This revision plan is the seed of the Change Log documenting how employer feedback was incorporated into your deliverable. Keep it in a shared document your team can reference throughout the project.
