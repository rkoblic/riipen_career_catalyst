# Presentation fundamentals

The Final Presentation closes the project. It's different work from the deliverable document, and the constraints are tighter. A document can be read at the audience's own pace and skimmed to the parts that matter to the reader. A presentation can't be. The audience hears it once, in the order you choose, for as long as you take.

Those constraints reshape how the team chooses what to include. The test isn't what fits in the time available. It's what the audience needs to walk away with, and what the team has to say to get them there.

This page walks through the fundamentals of professional presentations and introduces AI as a new kind of structuring tool. At the bottom, you will draft the first version of your team's presentation outline. That outline is working material for now. Refinement and rehearsal come later.

The concepts this page covers are also the four dimensions the Final Presentation is graded on at the end of the course: narrative structure, visual support, speaker coordination, and Q&A performance. Open [**the Final Presentation rubric**](URL_TBD) alongside this page to see the exact assessment language. Reading it now gives you a clearer picture of where the work needs to end up.

## Knowing your audience

The employer has been in every meeting that shaped this project. By the time your team presents, they already know the scope, the research, and most of what's in the deliverable (including the conclusions and recommendations, which they have already seen and given feedback on).

The presentation is not delivering new information. It is doing three other things.

- **Signaling what matters most.** The deliverable contains the full set of findings. The presentation is where your team takes a position on which two or three of them matter most, and why. That emphasis is the work of the presentation; it is not visible in the document.
- **Assembling the project into a single argument.** The audience has seen the pieces. They have not seen them delivered as one thing, in the order your team chose, in the shape of an argument. A good presentation is the first time the project holds together as a story rather than a sequence of meetings and drafts.
- **Asking the client to act.** A document sits on someone's desk. A presentation happens in a room with the client in it, and closes by asking them to do something with the work. Professional presentations end on the ask, not on the findings.

Most teams plan a presentation as if the audience needs to be brought from zero to the findings. Your audience isn't at zero. Planning from where they are leaves more time for synthesis and the ask.

### The hidden audience

The employer is usually not the only person watching. Your classmates and instructor are part of the audience, and in some projects the employer brings additional colleagues to the session (a manager, a board member, or a department lead who has not been in the earlier meetings).

A few things shift when the audience mix changes. You may need a short piece of context at the start for anyone who hasn't been in the room before. Technical language that your employer contact understands may need to be translated, or left out entirely, for other listeners. The stakes of a specific recommendation may sound different to someone who has to execute it than to someone who has been watching the project all semester.

Ask your employer contact who is likely to be in the room. If the answer changes what your presentation should include, that's useful to know before the outline is drafted.

## Narrative arc: telling a story, not reporting activities

The most common failure mode in a team presentation is a chronological march through what the team did. The audience hears what the team produced but not what it means for them. A story-shaped presentation organizes around the audience's question instead: what did you find, and what should we do about it?

For this project specifically, that means leading with the problem your team's work was solving and closing on what the client should do about it. In between, pull forward the two or three findings that shifted the team's thinking most. Your deliverable contains the full set of findings. The presentation is where the team signals which ones matter most.

Several well-known resources cover how professional presenters structure a presentation as an argument rather than a report. Watch the one below before your team drafts the narrative arc in the outline.

**Watch:** [TED's secret to great public speaking](https://youtu.be/-FOCpMAww28) — Chris Anderson (TED curator), ~8 min. The four-part framework for building a talk around a single through-line, giving the audience a reason to care, building the idea from concepts they already know, and making the idea worth sharing.

**Go deeper:** [10 Tips for Achieving Presentation Zen](https://presentationzen.com/blog/storytelling-and-presentation-zen-tips) — Garr Reynolds, ~8–10 min read. Picks up where Anderson leaves off. Where Anderson teaches structure, Reynolds teaches narrative craft: conflict, change, resolution, and what filmmakers and storytellers know about holding an audience.

## Visual clarity: slides support the speaker

A slide is a visual cue for what the speaker is saying. The speaker is the primary channel. When slides try to carry the message on their own, the audience reads the screen instead of listening.

A useful test for your team as you build slides: any slide that works as an email attachment without a speaker present is probably doing too much. Most slides should hold a title, a number or two, one image or chart, or a few words that anchor what's being said. Everything else belongs in the speaker notes, which only the team sees.

Professional designers have written extensively on what makes a slide work. The resource below is a good starting point before your team starts building slides.

**Watch:** [Create Slides People Will Remember](https://youtu.be/OeV2fHEM4RI) — Duarte, ~2 min. A tight primer on slide development: use slides selectively, write the speech before the slides, keep each slide to one concept readable in three seconds, and use visuals rather than projected text.

## Speaker coordination: a unified presentation

Team presentations often feel like three separate presentations taped together. Each speaker prepared their own section in isolation, with their own pacing and emphasis. Strung in a row, the sections don't share a voice or a through-line, and the audience can tell.

Preventing that takes deliberate coordination, not just practice. The practice helps individual delivery. The coordination is what makes the presentation feel like one thing: explicit handoff language between speakers, each section referencing the earlier ones by name, and a one-sentence recap as each new speaker opens.

Rehearse the handoffs as a separate pass. Most teams rehearse their own sections and then do one full run-through. The transitions are the first thing to crack in that run because they have not been practiced on their own. The resource below covers coordination patterns for multi-speaker presentations in more depth.

**Watch:** [8 Tips for How to do a Group Presentation](https://youtu.be/rF13U3CNDPY) — Lighthouse Communications, ~6 min. Covers transitions between speakers, a three-meeting rehearsal cadence (planning → table read → formal rehearsal), how non-speaking team members should behave on stage, and Q&A dynamics (including the "pile-on" effect to avoid).

## Using AI to structure a presentation

Presentations are a new place to apply AI in this course. Earlier weeks introduced AI as a quality reviewer for whole-document evaluation and as a production partner for section-level refinement. This week introduces a third role: AI as a structural planner for presentation content.

The raw material for a presentation is different from a document. Your team is working with the deliverable plus everything you know about the audience, the project history, and the argument. AI can help organize that material into a shape, as long as the team decides what the shape should carry.

There's a specific caution. AI-generated presentation language tends toward generic polish: "Today we'll walk you through our findings, which have significant implications for your organization." That kind of sentence sounds professional and says almost nothing. Your team's voice and findings have to drive what goes into the script. AI can draft a scaffold; the team has to fill it in.

### Prompt strategy: structuring a narrative

One of the more useful things AI does at this stage is help the team see the shape of their own argument. The team has been inside the work for weeks and has lost the ability to see it from outside. A prompt that opens the shape back up sounds like this:

> Here are our findings and our recommendations. What is the narrative arc that connects them? What should the presentation lead with, and what should it close on? What is the single thing the audience needs to leave the room believing?

Read what comes back as a set of options, not an answer. The team has context about the employer and the project that the AI doesn't. Use the response as a draft the team can argue with.

### Prompt strategy: drafting speaker notes

Once the outline exists, AI can draft first-pass speaker notes from your findings and recommendations. Keep the prompt tight:

> For this slide, draft speaker notes based on the underlying finding. Keep it conversational, not scripted. About forty-five seconds of narration.

The speaker then rewrites the notes in their own voice. Speaker notes that sound like AI sound obvious when delivered live. The team is the one who was in the meetings, and the audience can hear it when the voice behind the words is the team's rather than a tool's.

### Tools that turn an outline into slides

Several tools can build a first-pass slide deck once your team has the outline:

- [**Claude**](https://claude.ai) **and** [**Gemini**](https://gemini.google.com)**.** General-purpose AI assistants. Paste the outline and key messages and they can draft slide titles, speaker notes, and suggested visuals slide by slide. Claude can also render a working HTML slide deck live in the conversation.
- [**Gamma**](https://gamma.app)**.** Purpose-built for AI slide generation. Paste your content and it produces a designed deck you can edit.
- [**Beautiful.ai**](https://www.beautiful.ai)**.** Design automation. You outline the slides; it handles visual layout using a template system.

Use them to accelerate the production step, not to replace the structural thinking. A deck produced from a one-line prompt will look polished and say very little, the same failure mode as the language caution above. Teams that have already written the key messages and narrative arc get usable output; teams that feed in a vague prompt get polished filler.

A workable pattern: draft the outline manually. Feed the outline (not the full deliverable) into one of these tools to produce a first-pass deck. Revise by hand from there, keeping the voice, emphasis, and ordering your team chose through the outline.

One more reason this matters: anything on a slide is something the employer may ask about during or after the presentation. A slide generated by AI that no team member can explain becomes a liability in the room. A question about a claim on a slide no one built is hard to answer well. If a slide is in the final deck, someone on the team has to be able to defend what it says. Q&A preparation comes later in the course; this is the first step toward it.

### A reminder

AI hasn't been in the meetings or read the full deliverable, and it can't make judgment calls about what matters most to this employer on this project. Its best use this week is structural. Narrative judgment, emphasis, and the choice of what to leave out all belong to the team.

## Apply this now: draft your presentation outline

Use the template below to draft a working outline of the presentation. The outline is not submitted this week. Refinement and rehearsal happen a couple of weeks from now, and the outline is submitted as a checkpoint at that point. Drafting now gives your team something to argue with early, rather than building a presentation from scratch when the deadline is closer.

The outline should include four things.

- **Key messages.** Write three to five things the audience should take away, phrased as conclusions rather than topics. "We identified three segments the client is under-serving" is a message; "segmentation findings" is a topic. Messages are what the presentation delivers; topics are the shape of the deck.
- **Narrative arc.** Write one sentence each for the problem the project addressed, what the team found, and what the client should do with it. If the team can't produce the one-sentence version, the argument isn't yet clear enough to present. The full-length version gets built from these three sentences.
- **Visual approach.** Decide section by section which sections need a slide and which work better without one. Slides aren't required for every section. A short section where the speaker is making a concrete recommendation sometimes works better with nothing on screen, because the audience listens more closely when there's nothing to read.
- **Speaker assignments.** Decide who presents each section and how the handoffs between speakers are cued. Include a one-sentence handoff line at each transition. These shape how each speaker opens and closes their section, so they're worth drafting now.

[TEMPLATE: Presentation outline — Key Messages (3–5 bullets), Narrative Arc (one sentence each for problem, findings, recommendations), Visual Approach (section-by-section slide vs. said-only decisions), Speaker Assignments (who presents what, with handoff language at each transition).]

A draft that has all four sections, even roughly, is the goal for this sitting. Leaving the outline half-complete (key messages without speaker assignments, or assignments without handoffs) means the team will have to do both kinds of thinking from scratch later. Finish a full first pass now, then revise through rehearsal.
