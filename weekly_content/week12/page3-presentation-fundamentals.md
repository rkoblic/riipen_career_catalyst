# Presentation fundamentals

A final presentation is the only moment in the project where your team's work gets compressed into a short window and delivered live. A document can be read at the audience's own pace, re-read when something is unclear, and skimmed to the parts that matter to the reader. A presentation has none of those affordances. The audience hears it once, in the order you choose, for as long as you take.

That changes what the presentation is for. The question is no longer what to include. Most of your project's findings cannot fit. The question is what the audience should leave the room believing or doing, and what has to be said out loud for that to happen.

This page walks through the fundamentals of professional presentations and introduces AI as a new kind of structuring tool. At the bottom, you will draft the first version of your team's presentation outline. That outline is working material for now. Refinement and rehearsal come later.

## Knowing your audience

The employer has been in every meeting that shaped this project. The Kickoff, the Direction Check, two working check-ins, and the Near-Final Review. By the time your team presents, they already know the project's scope, the research, the deliverable, and most of what's inside it.

What they do not yet know is what your team has concluded and what your team recommends they do. Those are what the presentation delivers.

The starting position for planning is the opposite of what most teams instinctively do. Most teams plan a presentation as if the audience needs to be brought from zero to the findings. Your audience is not at zero. Planning from their real starting position frees up time to spend on the parts that genuinely haven't been said yet: the synthesis, the implications, and the recommendations.

### The hidden audience

The employer is usually not the only person watching. Your classmates and instructor are part of the audience, and in some projects the employer brings additional colleagues to the session (a manager, a board member, or a department lead who has not been in the earlier meetings).

A few things shift when the audience mix changes. You may need a short piece of context at the start for anyone who hasn't been in the room before. Technical language that your employer contact understands may need to be translated, or left out entirely, for other listeners. The stakes of a specific recommendation may sound different to someone who has to execute it than to someone who has been watching the project all semester.

Ask your employer contact who is likely to be in the room. If the answer changes what your presentation should include, that is useful to know before the outline is drafted, not after.

## Narrative arc: telling a story, not reporting activities

The most common failure mode in a team presentation is a chronological march through what the team did. The audience hears what the team produced but not what it means for them. A story-shaped presentation organizes around the audience's question instead: what did you find, and what should we do about it?

For this project specifically, that means leading with the problem your team's work was actually solving, pulling forward the two or three findings that shifted the team's thinking most, and closing on what the client should do with them. Your deliverable contains the full set of findings. The presentation is where the team signals which ones matter most.

## Visual clarity: slides support the speaker

A slide is a visual cue for what the speaker is saying. The speaker is the primary channel. Slides that stand on their own end up doing the speaking the speaker should be doing, and the audience reads the screen instead of listening.

A useful test for your team as you build slides: any slide that works as an email attachment without a speaker present is probably doing too much. Titles, numbers, one image or chart, a few words that anchor what is being said. Everything else belongs in speaker notes (which are for the team, not the audience).

## Speaker coordination: a unified presentation

Team presentations often feel like three separate presentations taped together. Each speaker prepared their own section, rehearsed their own pacing, and chose their own emphasis. Strung in a row, the sections do not share a voice or a through-line, and the audience can tell.

Preventing that takes deliberate coordination, not just practice. The practice helps individual delivery. The coordination is what makes the presentation feel like one thing: explicit handoff language between speakers, each section referencing the earlier ones by name, and a one-sentence recap as each new speaker opens.

Rehearse the handoffs as a separate pass. Most teams rehearse their own sections and then do one full run-through. The transitions are the first thing to crack in that run because they have not been practiced on their own. ## Using AI to structure a presentation

Presentations are a new place to apply AI in this course. The quality reviewer role from earlier weeks was for whole-document evaluation. The production partner role was for section-level refinement. This week introduces a third role: AI as a structural planner for presentation content.

The reason presentations are a distinct application is that the raw material is different. Your team is not starting from the deliverable. You are starting from the deliverable plus everything the team knows about the audience, the project history, and the argument. AI can help organize that material into a shape, as long as the team is the one deciding what the shape should carry.

The caution is specific. AI-generated presentation language tends toward generic polish — "Today we'll walk you through our findings, which have significant implications for your organization" — that sounds professional and says almost nothing. The sentences read fine and leave the audience with no clearer sense of what the presentation is about. Your team's voice, and your team's specific findings, have to drive what goes into the script. AI drafts a scaffold. The team fills in what the scaffold is carrying.

### Prompt strategy: structuring a narrative

One of the more useful things AI does at this stage is help the team see the shape of their own argument. The team has been inside the work for weeks and has lost the ability to see it from outside. A prompt that opens the shape back up sounds like this:

> Here are our findings and our recommendations. What is the narrative arc that connects them? What should the presentation lead with, and what should it close on? What is the single thing the audience needs to leave the room believing?

Read what comes back as a set of options, not an answer. The team has context about the employer and the project that the AI cannot have. Use the response as a draft the team can argue with, not a structure to adopt.

### Prompt strategy: drafting speaker notes

Once the outline exists, AI can draft first-pass speaker notes from your findings and recommendations. Keep the prompt tight:

> For this slide, draft speaker notes based on the underlying finding. Keep it conversational, not scripted. About forty-five seconds of narration.

The speaker then rewrites the notes in their own voice. Speaker notes that sound like AI sound obvious when delivered live. The team is the one who was in the meetings, and the audience can hear it when the voice behind the words is the team's rather than a tool's.

### A reminder

AI has not been in the meetings. It has not read the full deliverable. It cannot make judgment calls about what matters most to this specific employer in this specific project. Its best use this week is structural. Narrative judgment, emphasis, and the choice of what to leave out all belong to the team.

## Apply this now: draft your presentation outline

Use the template below to draft a working outline of the presentation. The outline is not submitted this week. Refinement and rehearsal happen a couple of weeks from now, and the outline is submitted as a checkpoint at that point. Drafting now gives your team something to argue with early, rather than building a presentation from scratch when the deadline is closer.

The outline should include four things.

- **Key messages.** Three to five things the audience should take away. Phrased as conclusions, not topics. "We identified three segments the client is under-serving" is a message. "Segmentation findings" is a topic. Messages are what the presentation delivers; topics are just the shape of the deck.
- **Narrative arc.** The problem the project addressed, what the team found, and what the client should do with it, one sentence for each. If the team cannot write the one-sentence version, the argument is not yet clear enough to present. The full-length version gets built from these three sentences, not the other way around.
- **Visual approach.** Section by section, what needs a slide and what is better said without one. Slides are not required for every section. A short section where the speaker is making a concrete recommendation sometimes works better with nothing on screen, because the audience has to listen rather than read.
- **Speaker assignments.** Who presents each section, and how the handoffs between speakers are cued. Include a one-sentence handoff line at each transition. These are worth drafting now because they shape how each speaker opens and closes their section.

A draft that has all four sections, even roughly, is the goal for this sitting. Leaving the outline half-complete (key messages without speaker assignments, or assignments without handoffs) means the team will have to do both kinds of thinking from scratch later.
