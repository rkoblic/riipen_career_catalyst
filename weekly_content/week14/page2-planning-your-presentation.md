# Planning your presentation

The concepts of a strong presentation — audience, narrative arc, visual clarity, speaker coordination — are already covered on the Presentation Fundamentals page from earlier in the course. This page is the operational version. Who presents what, how your team handles questions, how the outline moves from working draft to submitted checkpoint, and how the rehearsal block actually gets used.

A team presentation is harder than a solo one in a specific way: three or four people who have each lived different parts of the project have to show up in the room sounding like one voice telling one story. That does not happen by accident. It happens because the team decided in advance how the pieces fit, who carries which piece, and what each speaker does when the unexpected shows up. The rest of this page is how to make those decisions.

---

## Deciding who presents what

The default move in most teams is to split speaking time evenly and assign whichever section each person wrote. That is a reasonable starting point. It is also rarely the strongest arrangement by the time your team has spent weeks in the material.

The better question is who knows each section well enough to answer the harder question that sits behind it. Your employer is not going to ask your team to read the slide out loud. They are going to ask why this recommendation over a different one, what you considered and rejected, what assumption you made that you are still not fully sure about. The speaker who knows the reasoning behind a section, not just the words in it, is the one who should be carrying it.

A few things that tend to help:

- **Open and close deliberately.** The first speaker sets the tone and signals what the presentation is about. The last speaker lands the ask and handles the first question. Neither role is where you assign whoever is most nervous. Put the steadiest speakers at the beginning and the end, and put the speakers who need more room to warm up in the middle.
- **Match speaker to material, not to authorship.** If one teammate drafted the research section but a different teammate can speak to why the findings matter to this specific employer, the second one probably presents it. Authorship is a reasonable tiebreaker once the more important question is answered.
- **Do not split time evenly just because it feels fair.** Some sections carry more weight in the argument and deserve more time. Some sections only need two sentences and a slide. Time allocation should match the shape of the narrative.

## Handling team dynamics during the presentation itself

What your team rehearses is a run where everything goes according to plan. What your team also needs a plan for is the moment when something does not. A teammate loses their place. A teammate gets a question they cannot answer. A piece of tech fails and the next slide is not on screen.

What the employer remembers is how the team handles those moments. A few patterns that hold up under pressure:

- **Agree on a quiet recovery cue.** If a speaker stalls mid-sentence, the next speaker in the rotation steps in with a calm handoff, or the current speaker takes a breath and asks for a second. Either pattern works; what matters is that the team agreed on it in advance so nobody has to invent a response on the spot.
- **Route questions to the speaker closest to the content, not to whoever is talking.** In Q&A, the speaker on stage does not have to be the one who answers. A short "I can let my teammate take that, she built the model" is a stronger move than guessing. Decide in rehearsal who is the primary answerer for each section, and who backs them up.
- **Keep the non-speaking teammates visibly part of the presentation.** Teammates who are off-mic but on camera or on stage are still part of what the audience sees. Agree as a team on what attention looks like for the non-speakers: no phones, no side conversations, no visible disengagement from what the current speaker is saying. This is coordination work that is easy to forget until it reads badly on the day.

## Handling Q&A

[VIDEO: Saying I don't know well]

The video walks through how the defend-your-work standard actually plays out when the employer asks a question the team is not ready for. What follows on the page is the underlying moves, laid out so the team can prepare before the rehearsal.

The Q&A portion of the presentation is the moment where the team's own understanding becomes visible. Every answer given under live questioning is evidence that the team did the work itself and can explain why it chose what it chose. Preparation turns that visibility into an asset rather than a risk. Treat the Q&A as the part of the presentation where the team's judgment shows up most clearly.

### Answering without fabricating

There are three things to know before the Q&A that most learners do not practice until it is too late.

- **A direct answer beats a polished one.** "Our main concern with that option was cost" is stronger than a long preface about how the team considered many factors. Professional audiences read hedging as evasion even when it is not.
- **"I don't know" is a complete sentence, as long as you add what you do know.** The professional move when a question lands on something the team did not investigate is to name the edge of what you know and describe how you would find out the rest. "We did not look into the regulatory side of that question directly. If we did, we would start with the filings available on the state registry and talk to someone in compliance. Our recommendation assumes the current regulatory framework holds." That answer is more credible than a guess.
- **If two teammates know something different about the same question, only one answers.** Multiple overlapping answers make the team sound uncertain. Agree in advance on who takes each kind of question. Backup voices can add a single sentence if the primary answer missed something, but not an alternative version of the same answer.

### Handling pushback on a recommendation

Some questions are not requests for information. They are challenges. The employer disagrees with a recommendation, or wants to know why the team did not recommend something they expected. The instinct under pressure is to either cave or get defensive. Neither is the right move.

What usually works is a short structure in three parts. First, acknowledge what the question is pointing at without restating it. Then name the specific thing in your analysis that supports the recommendation. Close by asking a question back that moves the conversation toward the employer's actual concern.

For example, if the employer says "I'm not sure this will work with our current staffing," a strong response sounds like: "That is a concern we thought about when we shaped the recommendation. We built it around the capacity the current staffing can realistically handle in the first quarter. Is the worry about first-quarter capacity, or is there a staffing change coming up we did not plan for?" That answer treats the pushback as a real question rather than an attack, and gives the team new information to work with.

If the pushback turns out to be a correction the team should accept, accept it. Holding a recommendation the employer has just given you a good reason to revisit makes the team look less thoughtful, not more confident.

### Using AI to anticipate questions

The stress-testing strategy from earlier in the course extends directly into Q&A preparation. The prompt the team has used on drafts and recommendations works on the presentation itself.

> Here are the three main recommendations and the evidence we built them on. What would a skeptical employer ask about each one? What is the question we would least like to get, and how would we handle it if it did?

Run the prompt before the rehearsal, not during. What you get back is a list of candidate questions to practice answers to. Practice those answers out loud, not just in writing. An answer that reads fine on the page often comes apart when spoken in front of an audience for the first time, and the rehearsal is the first place that surfaces.

## What the rehearsal is for

A rehearsal is not a read-through of the script, and it is not a test of whether your team has memorized anything. The rehearsal is the first and only time the presentation exists as one continuous thing before the employer sees it. What gets rehearsed is the seams, not the content.

There are four things the rehearsal block is there to surface, in roughly this order:

- **Timing.** Run the whole presentation once, end to end, with a clock running. Do not stop to fix things mid-run. Most presentations come in long on the first run; finding out before the day gives the team a chance to cut rather than rush.
- **Transitions between speakers.** The Presentation Fundamentals page flagged that handoffs are the first thing to crack in a full run-through because they have not been practiced inside the individual sections. Rehearsal is where that guidance gets applied. A targeted pass on just the handoffs — each speaker says only the last sentence of their section and the first sentence of the next — is usually enough to land them.
- **Narrative coherence across sections.** Listen for whether the presentation tells one story or four. Does each section connect to the one before it? Does the closing land on what the opening promised? A listener outside the team is the best signal for this. If your rehearsal includes teammates not presenting certain sections, use their listening attention deliberately.
- **Q&A practice out loud.** Run the anticipated-question list from the AI prompt above. Practice the answers as they would be given in the room, not as notes on a page. Questions you thought you had a good answer for often surface as weaker than expected once spoken, and the rehearsal is where that surfaces.

After the full run-through and the targeted passes, leave time for one more run of whatever broke the hardest. One focused re-run of a weak section is more useful than a second full run-through that will only reveal the same problems.

Rehearsal feedback stays inside the team. What gets fixed is what the team saw and heard itself, plus whatever a teammate in the listening seat flagged. This is not the place for new ideas about content — the content is the content at this stage. What changes in the rehearsal is delivery, pacing, and coordination.

## Finalize and submit the Presentation Outline

The outline is a completion-based checkpoint, not something the employer sees. Your experience manager or instructor may review it as a support and accountability mechanism: a chance to confirm the team has done deliberate planning before the deck build, and to catch thin spots while there is still time to fix them. The deck build inherits whatever decisions the outline made or didn't.

### What the finalized outline should include

The outline started a couple of weeks ago as a working draft, with four parts: key messages, narrative arc, visual approach, and speaker assignments. The finalized version is the same four parts, with two things that were not fully decided in the draft:

- **Speaker assignments resolved.** Who presents each section and who is the primary answerer for questions about it, plus the handoff line between each speaker. "Over to Maya, who will walk through the analysis" is a handoff line. "Maya will talk about research" is not — it is a label, not a cue.
- **Visual decisions made section by section.** Every section should have a decision about whether it has a slide and what that slide does. Some sections are better without one, because the audience has to listen rather than read. The outline should show where the team landed, not leave it open.

Your team can reuse the template from the earlier outline draft. No new template is needed — what changes is the level of decision inside each part.

### How this differs from the working draft

The earlier version of the outline was working material. It existed so your team had something to argue with, not something to present from. The finalized version is the thing the deck build will be built on. If the team is still holding open questions about narrative or ordering at this point, the best use of the outline-finalization time is to close them. Starting the deck build with open structural questions means every slide decision comes back to the same unresolved fight.

If the team's thinking has shifted since the draft — the employer's feedback from recent check-ins landed differently, a piece of analysis came out stronger than expected, one of the findings has become less central — this is where those shifts get incorporated. The outline is allowed to be different from the draft. It should be different, if the team's understanding has moved.

### Cross-check against the Final Presentation rubric

Open [**the Final Presentation rubric**](URL_TBD) with the finalized outline in hand. The outline's narrative arc and key messages feed the rubric's narrative structure dimension, the visual approach feeds visual support, and the speaker assignments feed speaker coordination. Q&A performance is assessed live and does not have an outline counterpart, but the anticipated-question list from earlier on this page is the closest equivalent. Any outline section that reads thin against the rubric language is where to spend revision time before the deck build starts.

### AI on the outline

The Presentation Fundamentals page introduced AI as a structural planner and gave a prompt for generating narrative-arc options on a blank canvas. Outline finalization is a different use of the same strategy: reading an outline that already exists and pressure-testing it before the deck build starts. A prompt that tends to work at this stage:

> Here is our finalized outline: key messages, narrative arc, visual approach, and speaker assignments. Read it as someone who has not been in any of our meetings. Where does the argument stop holding together? What is a listener likely to still be confused about at the end? What would you cut if time ran short?

Read what comes back as pressure on the structure, not as edits to adopt. Places where the AI loses the thread are often places where a live audience will too, and those are worth fixing before the outline is locked.
