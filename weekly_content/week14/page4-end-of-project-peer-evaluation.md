# End-of-Project Peer Evaluation

**The End-of-Project Peer Evaluation is submitted next week, not this week.** Like Reflection #5, it finalizes during the final week alongside the Final Presentation, and the final week is already full. It is also bigger than it looks. If your team has three other members, that is three separate evaluations. Each one includes qualitative feedback and four BARS ratings, supported by specific behavioral evidence drawn from across the full project. The work adds up quickly. If you start this week with an instructions review and evidence gathering, next week's work becomes finalization. If you wait, it becomes construction under deadline pressure, which is when evaluations get thinner.

The evaluation you write for each teammate is the last piece of feedback they carry out of this project. For most teammates, you are describing behavior that became a habit over fifteen weeks. What you write about that behavior is what they take with them into the next team they join. Generic feedback ("good teammate, easy to work with") tells them nothing they could not have guessed. Specific behavioral feedback, honestly given, is something a colleague can actually act on.

That is the professional skill this evaluation builds. Writing feedback that is specific enough to be useful, honest enough to be credible, and calibrated across a full project arc rather than the last thing you remember. The rest of this page covers what is different about evaluating the full project, how to gather evidence across fifteen weeks, and how to use this week to set up a strong final submission next week.

---

## How this differs from the Mid-Project Peer Evaluation

The instrument is the same. The Mid-Project Peer Evaluation you completed earlier in the course used the Continue/Start/Adjust framework for qualitative feedback and BARS (Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scales) across four dimensions: Reliability, Contribution, Constructive Engagement, and Accountability. The End-of-Project evaluation uses the same framework and the same four dimensions.

Three things change.

- **The scope expands from the first phase of the project to the full arc.** You are evaluating behavior that happened fifteen weeks ago as well as behavior from this week. That is a harder memory task, and the evaluation's credibility depends on how specifically you can reference behavior from different points in the project rather than averaging into a vague impression.
- **The weight carries further.** The End-of-Project evaluation contributes more to the Professional Engagement component of the final grade than the Mid-Project evaluation did. The instrument is the same, but the stakes for your teammates' grades are higher, which raises the standard on how carefully you calibrate your ratings.
- **The feedback is more useful the more specific it is.** Your teammate read your Mid-Project feedback at a point when they still had half the project left to act on it. Your End-of-Project feedback lands when the project is over. It becomes most useful to them in their next team, in their next professional context. Feedback pitched at that level — describing behavior in terms that would still make sense to a teammate you never worked with — is what transfers.

The rubric specifics, the Continue/Start/Adjust prompt language, and the BARS scale descriptors are unchanged from the Mid-Project evaluation. The resource page from the earlier evaluation is the reference if you want to revisit the calibration examples before you start.

## Evaluating contributions that evolved over time

The complication that did not exist in the Mid-Project evaluation is that teammates' contributions change across a fifteen-week project. This is normal. It is also the part of the evaluation where credibility and usefulness get made or lost.

Three patterns that tend to show up and need specific handling:

- **The teammate who started slowly and stepped up.** Someone who was quiet or uncertain in the early weeks and became a substantial contributor in the Development or Refinement phase is not the same as a teammate who coasted throughout. A strong evaluation captures both the slow start and the step up, because both are part of the story. A rating that only reflects one phase misrepresents the arc.
- **The teammate who led early and faded.** The opposite pattern. Someone who was organized and active in the first half of the project and became harder to reach or less responsive in the final weeks. This pattern is harder to rate because the team's early impression of the teammate is usually more positive than the late-project reality. The evaluation should describe the arc honestly, including what changed and roughly when.
- **The teammate who was steady throughout.** The steady contributor is easy to under-rate because there is no single moment to point to. A strong evaluation for a steady teammate names the consistency itself as a specific behavior — showed up prepared to every meeting, delivered agreed work on time across the whole project, raised concerns early rather than late.

In each case, the rating and the evidence need to reflect the full arc, not the endpoint. The safest move is to scan the project phase by phase (the early formation weeks, the production push in the middle, the refinement and final deliverable weeks, the presentation preparation) and note at least one specific behavior per phase for each teammate. If you find a phase where you have no specific evidence for a teammate, that is useful information — it may mean the teammate was in the background that phase, or it may mean you were.

### What counts as behavioral evidence

The same standard as the Mid-Project evaluation applies. Behavioral evidence is an observable action and its effect on the team's work. "I felt like he was engaged" is not evidence. "He circled back to his own draft after two teammates flagged the same structural issue and revised it within a day" is evidence.

If you are uncertain between two ratings on a dimension, the Mid-Project rule still holds: select the lower rating and explain why in your evidence. A careful downward rating with specific reasoning is more credible than a generous rating without evidence.

## Using AI

AI is permitted for the organizational work of assembling evidence across fifteen weeks. It is not permitted to generate observations or assign ratings. The same boundary as the Mid-Project evaluation applies, restated because the organizational support is more useful when you have more material to sort.

A workable prompt once you have notes on a teammate's behavior:

> Here are my notes on [teammate] from across the project. Help me sort these into the four BARS dimensions (Reliability, Contribution, Constructive Engagement, Accountability). For each dimension, flag whether the evidence I have is strong enough to support a specific rating, or whether I need to think more before rating.

What comes back is a structured starting point, not an evaluation. Read the AI's dimension-by-dimension sort critically — it can miss nuance about whether a specific behavior belongs in Contribution or Constructive Engagement, and the judgment on where a behavior sits is yours to make. Dimensions where the AI flags thin evidence are usually accurate, and those are the dimensions to pay attention to during the rehearsal and Final Presentation this week and next.

Observations and ratings must be your own. AI disclosure is required on the submission next week.

## Begin the evaluation this week

Try to spend about 45 minutes on this this week. You are setting yourself up for a strong submission next week, when the rehearsal and Final Presentation will add the last behavioral observations you need. The evaluation itself gets written after those.

Three moves, in roughly this order:

- **Revisit the instructions.** Open the Mid-Project Peer Evaluation page and the instructions for the End-of-Project Peer Evaluation. The Continue/Start/Adjust prompt language, the BARS scale descriptors across the four dimensions, and the specificity standard for evidence are the same. Familiarity with the framework is what keeps your evidence focused on behavior rather than impression.
- **Gather evidence across the full arc.** For each teammate, scan the project phase by phase and note specific behavioral examples. At this stage you are gathering observations, not assigning ratings. Aim for at least one piece of evidence per phase per teammate, and note where evidence is thin. Your Change Log, team meeting notes, and any notes-to-self you kept during earlier check-ins and working sessions are useful memory aids for the earlier phases. Reach for the specific, not the general. "She reliably brought drafts to the shared folder two days before each team sync" is evidence. "She was reliable" is not.
- **Build a short noticing list for the final week.** For each teammate, write down which dimensions have thin evidence and what you want to watch for during the rehearsal and Final Presentation. Those two events are the last sources of behavioral evidence you will have. Going into them with specific attention ("I need another observation on Constructive Engagement for teammate X") is more useful than trying to reconstruct observations after the fact.
