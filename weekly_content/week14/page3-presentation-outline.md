# The Presentation Outline

Professional teams don't build a deck and then figure out what it says. They decide what the presentation has to do first — the message, the order, who carries which part — and let those decisions shape every slide that gets built. The outline is where those decisions get recorded before the deck build begins.

The Presentation Outline is your team's version of that practice. It is the checkpoint deliverable this week, submitted before your team starts building the deck. Team submission, completion-based. Not graded for quality. Not shared with the employer. Your experience manager or instructor may review it as a support and accountability mechanism — a chance to confirm the team has done deliberate planning, and to catch thin spots while there is still time to fix them.

---

## What this checkpoint is (and isn't)

The outline is a team submission, not an individual one. It is an internal working document. It is **not** the deck, and it is not a script. The employer will not receive it, and you should not send it to them.

The submission is completion-based: verified, not graded for quality. The Final Presentation itself is what gets graded against the rubric next week. The outline's job is to make sure the deck build starts from resolved decisions rather than open questions, and to give the team a record of what those decisions were when the deck inevitably tries to drift from them.

Drafting the outline is also a separate piece of work from the planning conversations on the previous page. The previous page is where the team works through the decisions — who speaks where, how Q&A gets handled, what the rehearsal needs to surface. This page is where those decisions get written down in a form the team can build from.

---

## What goes in the finalized outline

The outline started a couple of weeks ago, on the Presentation Fundamentals page, as a working draft with four parts: key messages, narrative arc, visual approach, and speaker assignments. The finalized version is the same four parts, with the two pieces that were not fully decided in the draft now resolved.

Your team can reuse the template from the earlier outline draft. No new template is needed. What changes is the level of decision inside each part.

### Key messages

Three to five things the audience should take away, phrased as conclusions rather than topics. "We identified three segments the client is under-serving" is a message; "segmentation findings" is a topic. The working draft was where these got proposed; the finalized version is where the team commits to which messages the presentation is actually built around. If the team is still holding more than five candidate messages at this point, the outline-finalization sitting is where the cut happens.

### Narrative arc

One sentence each for the problem the project addressed, what the team found, and what the employer should do with it. The finalized version is the sentences your team can defend, not the first draft of them. If the team can't produce the one-sentence version cleanly, the argument isn't yet clear enough to present. The full-length version of the presentation gets built from these three sentences, so they have to land before the deck build starts.

### Visual approach

Section by section, the decision about whether the section has a slide and what that slide does. Some sections are better without one, because the audience has to listen rather than read. The outline should show where the team landed on each section, not leave the decision open. "TBD" on visual approach means the deck build inherits the decision, which is the wrong place for it to get made.

### Speaker assignments

Who presents each section, who is the primary answerer for questions about it, and the handoff line between each speaker. The previous page covered how to make these calls. The outline is where the calls get written down.

A handoff line is not a label. "Over to Maya, who will walk through the analysis" is a handoff line. "Maya will talk about research" is a label. Write the actual sentence each speaker will say at the transition. That sentence is what the next speaker hears as their cue, and what the audience hears as the seam between sections.

---

## What the completion criteria require

An outline that names topics instead of messages, leaves visual decisions open, or assigns speakers without handoff lines does not meet the criteria. The criteria don't require polish — they require that the structural decisions are actually decided.

The criteria are:

- **Key messages** as conclusions, not topics. Three to five.
- **Narrative arc** with one defendable sentence each for problem, findings, recommendation.
- **Visual approach** with a slide-or-no-slide decision on every section.
- **Speaker assignments** with a written handoff line at each transition, and a named primary answerer per section.

A short outline that resolves these four things genuinely is enough. Length is not the standard.

Before submitting, open [**the Final Presentation rubric**](URL_TBD) with the finalized outline in hand. The narrative arc and key messages feed the rubric's narrative structure dimension, the visual approach feeds visual support, and the speaker assignments feed speaker coordination. Q&A performance is assessed live and does not have an outline counterpart; the anticipated-question list from the previous page is the closest equivalent. Any outline section that reads thin against the rubric language is where to spend revision time before the deck build starts.

## Using AI

The Presentation Fundamentals page introduced AI as a structural planner for generating narrative-arc options on a blank canvas. Outline finalization is a different use of the same strategy: reading an outline that already exists and pressure-testing it before the deck build starts. A prompt that tends to work at this stage:

> Here is our finalized outline: key messages, narrative arc, visual approach, and speaker assignments. Read it as someone who has not been in any of our meetings. Where does the argument stop holding together? What is a listener likely to still be confused about at the end? What would you cut if time ran short?

Read what comes back as pressure on the structure, not as edits to adopt. Places where the AI loses the thread are often places where a live audience will too, and those are worth fixing before the outline is locked.

What AI cannot do is make the decisions inside the four parts. The team is the one who has been in the meetings, sat with the employer feedback, and built the analysis. Which messages to lead with, which speaker carries which section, which sections need a slide — those calls are the team's, and the act of making them together is part of what the outline-finalization sitting is for. If the team hands the decisions to AI, the deck build inherits assignments and emphases nobody on the team chose, and the rehearsal is where that breaks.

## Submission

**Presentation Outline** — team submission, due before your team starts building the deck this week.

Submit as a team through the Riipen platform. The submission is a completion-based checkpoint: verified, not graded for quality. An outline submitted after the deck build has already started cannot serve its purpose, since the purpose is to resolve structural decisions before the build inherits them. Late submissions do not meet the standard.

**AI disclosure:** Required. If AI was used to pressure-test the outline or generate candidate framings, include a one-sentence disclosure describing how. If AI was not used, include a one-sentence statement to that effect. The decisions inside the outline must come from your team regardless of whether AI helped with structural review.
