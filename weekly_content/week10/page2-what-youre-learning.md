# What you're actually learning

Several weeks of project work produce more learning than most people can name. You can describe what you built: the research, the analysis, the sections your team assembled. What's harder to describe is what you've actually gotten better at. Which specific professional skills you now have that you didn't have at the start.

This gap is normal, and it's also a problem. Hiring conversations, performance reviews, and graduate applications all ask about transferable skill, not project summaries. "We built a context analysis for a nonprofit" is a project summary. "I got better at scoping ambiguous client needs into researchable questions" is a transferable skill, and it's the kind of thing a hiring manager or admissions committee actually cares about.

This module is about closing the gap while the work is fresh. The first part is about noticing what you've learned. The second is about putting it in a form that travels beyond this specific project.

### A noticing exercise

Three prompts to use on your own project work. They're designed to surface specific learning from specific situations. Reflection prompts ask you to examine what happened. These ask you to notice what's stuck.

- **What did you do this week that you couldn't have done when the project started?** The task itself probably hasn't changed. You've done research, writing, or meeting prep multiple times across this project. What's different is the judgment and approach inside the task. What are you doing now that you didn't know to do before?
- **What moment surprised you, and what did the surprise reveal?** Surprise is a reliable signal that your prior mental model didn't fit. What was your model, what did the situation show you, and what's your updated model?
- **Where did you change your mind about how something should work?** A scope decision, a team dynamic, a research direction. The shift itself is the learning.

Write a few sentences for each, in whatever form feels natural. The point is to get specific learning on paper before you try to make it transferable.

## Making your learning transferable

Even once you've noticed what you've learned, there's a second move: getting it into a form that travels beyond this specific project. This is where most people stall.

"I learned how our client runs their operations meetings" is a real piece of learning, but it's tied to one client. Strip the client and the project type, and nothing remains. A transferable version of the same learning might read: "I learned that operations meetings in under-resourced organizations often function differently from what management theory describes. The formal agenda matters less than the context people are carrying into the room." That version can travel. A reader who's never worked with this client can use it.

### The strip-the-proper-nouns test

If your learning statement still reads useful after you remove the client name, project type, and sector, it's transferable. If removing them makes the statement disappear, it's still situation-specific.

Run the test on anything you write down. It's fast and it catches most of the gap.

### Why this matters for your reflection work

The reflection portfolio is assessed on four dimensions, each weighted equally. The fourth one, Articulation of Learning, is what this module is preparing you for. Strong reflections move beyond description. They articulate what the experience taught the writer in a form that survives being read by someone who wasn't there.

This becomes the dimension to watch in your two remaining reflections, both of which are designed around transferable learning rather than specific events. Noticing and abstracting are skills. They get better with practice, and this week is when to start practicing.
