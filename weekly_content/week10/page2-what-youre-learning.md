# What you're actually learning

Several weeks of project work produce more learning than most people can name. You can describe what the team built. Naming the specific professional skills you now have that you didn't have at the start is harder.

That gap shows up later. When a hiring manager or admissions committee asks what you took away from a project, they want to hear a transferable skill. A project summary doesn't give them that. "We built a context analysis for a nonprofit" is a project summary. "I got better at scoping ambiguous client needs into researchable questions" is a skill, and that's what they're listening for.

This module is about closing the gap while the work is fresh: noticing what you've learned, and naming it in a way that holds up outside this project.

### A noticing exercise

Below are three prompts you can use to reflect on your own project work and identify what's changed in how you work because of the experience.

- **What did you do this week that you couldn't have done when the project started?** The task itself probably hasn't changed. You've done this kind of work many times by now. What's different is the judgment and approach inside the task. What are you doing now that you didn't know to do before?
- **What moment surprised you, and what did the surprise reveal?** Surprise usually signals that your prior mental model didn't fit the situation. What was your model? What shifted?
- **Where did you change your mind about how something should work?** This could be anywhere you used to assume one thing and now think differently. The shift doesn't have to be dramatic. It might be a scope choice, or how you read a teammate's pushback.

Write a few sentences for each, in whatever form feels natural. The point is to get the learning on paper before you try to make it transferable.

## Making your learning transferable

Even once you've noticed what you've learned, there's another step: naming it so it holds up outside this project. Most people stall here.

"I learned how our client runs their operations meetings" is a real piece of learning, but it's tied to one client. Strip the client and the project type, and nothing remains. The transferable version of the same learning sounds different:

- **Tied to one client:** "I learned how our client runs their operations meetings."
- **Holds up outside the project:** "I learned that operations meetings in under-resourced organizations often function differently from what management theory describes. The formal agenda matters less than the context people are carrying into the room."

A reader who's never worked with this client can use the second version.

### The strip-the-proper-nouns test

If your learning statement still reads useful after you remove the client name and the project type, it's transferable. If removing them makes the statement disappear, it's still situation-specific.

Run the test on anything you write down. It's fast and it catches most of the gap.

### Why this matters for your reflection work

The reflection portfolio is assessed on four dimensions, each weighted equally. The fourth one, Articulation of Learning, is what this module prepares you for. Strong reflections go past description. They name what the writer learned in terms a reader outside the situation can still use.

This is the dimension to watch in your two remaining reflections, which both ask for transferable learning. Starting your practice now means you'll be further along when you sit down to write them.
