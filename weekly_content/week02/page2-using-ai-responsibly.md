# Using AI Responsibly

Every professional field is figuring out how to work with AI right now. The people who stand out are the ones who use it well: they know how to ask it the right questions, how to spot when it's wrong, and how to use it to develop their thinking on a problem.

This page introduces the approach you'll use for the rest of the course.

## Three Principles for Prompting Well

Many people use AI the way they'd use a search engine: type in a vague question, hope for a decent answer. That works sometimes. But in a professional context, you need more reliable results. These three principles will help.

### 1. Give AI Enough Context Before You Ask

AI doesn't know your project, your team, or your situation unless you tell it. Before asking a question, share the relevant background: your project brief, your role on the team, what you're trying to accomplish, and any constraints you're working within.

Instead of: *"How do I write a project plan?"*

Try: *"I'm on a student consulting team working with a mid-size nonprofit on their donor engagement strategy. We need to write a project plan that includes scope, milestones, and risks. What should we include, and what are common mistakes teams make at this stage?"*

The more context you give, the more useful the response.

### 2. Ask for Options, Not Answers

Ask AI to generate possibilities, surface tradeoffs, or challenge your assumptions rather than handing you a single "right" answer.

Instead of: *"What should our team's communication plan be?"*

Try: *"What are three different approaches a small team could use to stay coordinated on a 15-week project? What are the tradeoffs of each?"*

This gives you something to discuss as a team rather than something to copy.

### 3. Always Ask: What Am I Assuming Here?

AI will confidently reinforce your assumptions if you don't push back. Build a habit of asking AI to challenge your thinking: *"What assumptions am I making in this approach?"* or *"What's the strongest argument against this plan?"*

> [!key]
> This matters most when AI gives you something that already sounds right. That's the moment you're least likely to push on it — and the moment you most need to.

## Good Prompts vs. Weak Prompts

Here are some common project tasks and what better prompting looks like in practice.

### Researching an Organization

- Weak: *"Tell me about Company X."*
- Better: *"I need to prepare for a kickoff meeting with Company X, a [sector] company that [brief description]. What should I know about their industry, competitors, and recent developments? Flag anything I should verify independently."*

### Preparing Questions for a Client Meeting

- Weak: *"What questions should I ask in a meeting?"*
- Better: *"My team is meeting with an employer contact for the first time to discuss a project about [topic]. We want to understand their goals, success criteria, and constraints. What are the most important questions to ask, and which ones should we prioritize if we run short on time?"*

### Drafting a Professional Email

- Weak: *"Write an email to our employer."*
- Better: *"Draft an introductory email from a student team to an employer contact at [Company X]. We need to introduce our team members briefly, reference the project brief on [topic], and request a 60-minute kickoff meeting. Tone should be professional but warm, since this is our first contact."*

### Organizing Research Notes

- Weak: *"Summarize these notes."*
- Better: *"Here are research notes from our team members on [topic]. Identify the three to five most important patterns across these findings. For each pattern, explain what it might mean for how we approach the project. Flag any contradictions between team members' findings."*

> [!info] THE PATTERN
> Across all four, the better prompts share three moves: they give AI context up front, ask for a structured response, and ask it to flag what you should verify or think about further.

## Evaluating What AI Gives You

AI will always give you an answer. The real question is whether that answer is any good. Here's what to watch for.

**Check for unsupported claims.** AI often presents assertions as if they're well-established facts. Ask yourself: is this backed by something I can verify, or is AI just stating it confidently? If a claim matters to your work, find the original source.

For example, AI might tell you that "nonprofits with structured volunteer programs see 40% higher retention." That's a specific number presented as fact. Search for where it came from. If you can't trace it to an actual study, don't use the figure.

**Watch for hallucinated sources.** AI sometimes invents citations: real-sounding journal articles, reports, or statistics that don't actually exist. If AI cites a source, look it up. If you can't find it, it may not be real.

A common version of this: AI cites "a 2023 Brookings Institution report on small business sustainability practices." When you search the Brookings site directly, no such report exists. The institution is real, but the report it supposedly published isn't.

**Test for recency.** AI's training data has a cutoff. If you're researching a fast-moving industry or a recent development, AI may be working with outdated information. Cross-check against current sources.

For example, AI might describe the current head of marketing at your employer's company — someone whose LinkedIn shows they left the role 18 months ago. Or it might describe a regulation that was repealed last year as if it were still in effect.

**Notice when AI is agreeing with you too easily.** If every response confirms what you already think, you're probably not pushing hard enough. Ask AI to argue the other side or identify weaknesses in your approach.

Say you ask whether a digital-first outreach strategy makes sense for your nonprofit client, and AI generates an enthusiastic plan without flagging the obvious risk: their donor base skews older and prefers mail. Reframe the question. *"What's the strongest case against a digital-first approach for this audience?"* gets you the pushback you actually need.

In this course, if you can't explain and defend something AI helped you produce, it shouldn't be in your work.

## Bias in AI output

AI generates text based on patterns in its training data, and that training data reflects the biases of the world that produced it. This means AI output can reinforce stereotypes, overrepresent dominant perspectives, and underrepresent marginalized communities.

This matters for your project work. If you're researching a community, AI may reflect assumptions about that community that don't match reality. If you're analyzing an audience, AI may default to demographic generalizations that flatten the people you're trying to understand. If you're developing recommendations, AI may favor approaches that worked for well-resourced organizations and miss what's relevant for smaller or differently structured ones.

A few things to watch for:

- **Default assumptions about who the "user" or "audience" is.** AI often assumes a mainstream, English-speaking, urban, relatively affluent audience unless told otherwise. If your project serves a different population, you need to specify that explicitly and check whether the output actually reflects their context. *Example:* you ask AI to draft a survey for community center members, and the questions assume reliable home internet, comfort with online forms, and English literacy — even though your employer's actual members include older adults, multiple language groups, and people without home broadband.
- **Missing perspectives.** AI may not surface issues that matter to specific communities but don't appear frequently in mainstream sources. If your research feels like it's missing something, it probably is. *Example:* you ask about barriers to healthcare access, and AI returns the well-documented mainstream answers (cost, insurance coverage). It says little about transportation distance and provider shortages — the issues that actually dominate in rural communities — because those topics are underrepresented in its training data.
- **Stereotyping in examples and recommendations.** AI-generated examples about specific demographics, industries, or regions may rely on stereotypes rather than evidence. Treat these the same way you'd treat any unsupported claim: verify or remove. *Example:* you ask AI to suggest marketing approaches for "small business owners in immigrant communities," and it returns recommendations built on stock assumptions about culture and family rather than anything tied to the specific communities your employer actually serves.

The fix isn't to avoid AI for these tasks. It's to treat AI output about people and communities with the same critical eye you'd apply to any single source. Cross-reference against primary sources, local data, and direct input from the people your project is meant to serve.

## When not to use AI

AI is permitted for most course activities, but "permitted" doesn't mean "helpful." There are times when using AI is counterproductive even when it's allowed.

**When you're building a skill for the first time.** If you've never written a professional email, having AI draft one for you means you never learn how. Draft it yourself first, struggle with the phrasing, and then use AI to refine.

**When AI would shortcut the thinking.** Some tasks are designed to develop your judgment: weighing tradeoffs, making decisions under uncertainty, evaluating what matters most. If you ask AI to make those calls for you, you get an answer but you don't develop the skill. You'll know this is happening when you accept AI's suggestion without forming your own opinion first.

**When the value is in the process, not the output.** Team discussions, reflection writing, and synthesis meetings are valuable because of what happens in your head during the process. A charter only works as a working agreement when your team has actually negotiated the terms together; the document that AI hands back doesn't carry the same weight, even if the language is identical.

This doesn't rule AI out for these tasks. The right time to bring it in is after you've done the thinking yourself.
