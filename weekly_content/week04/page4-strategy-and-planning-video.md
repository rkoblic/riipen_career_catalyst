# Video Script: The "so what" move

Say your team is working on a social media strategy. Your research found that three direct competitors have shifted to short-form video in the past year, and the two that moved first saw the strongest audience growth. The employer is still posting static images.

Here's how you build the chain. The finding is that competitors who adopted short-form video saw significantly stronger engagement and follower growth, and the employer's current format doesn't match what the audience responds to. What that means is there's a format shift happening in this market, and the employer is behind it. The gap isn't about posting frequency or content quality. It's about format.

Then you name what you're assuming, because every recommendation rests on assumptions and the employer needs to see them. You're assuming the employer has the willingness and capacity to create video content, and that the audience behavior you observed in competitors' accounts is transferable to the employer's own audience. And then you make the recommendation concrete: a 90-day short-form video pilot on TikTok and Instagram Reels, starting with two posts per week, with performance benchmarks at 30 and 60 days.

The recommendation traces directly back to the research, the assumptions are named explicitly, and the employer now has something specific to react to. They might say "we can't do video, we don't have the skills." Now you know, and you can adjust before you've spent weeks building a plan around it.

Now community outreach. Your research found that published studies consistently identify transportation and scheduling, not awareness, as the primary barriers to youth program participation. The employer's current model relies on flyers and website promotion.

The finding is that the real barrier probably isn't awareness. It's access. What that means is that increasing marketing, which is what the current model focuses on, probably won't move the needle on participation. The problem is structural, not informational.

The assumption matters here. You're assuming the barriers identified in published research apply to this community, and you haven't confirmed that with local data or direct input from the families the organization serves. Naming that lets the employer push back if their experience says otherwise. The recommendation: a satellite-location pilot at community centers accessible by public transit, paired with adjusted programming times that reflect when the target youth population is actually available.

If the employer comes back and says "transportation isn't the top issue in our area," that's incredibly valuable feedback, and you'd rather hear it now than after you've built an entire strategy around the wrong barrier.
