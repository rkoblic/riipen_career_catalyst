# Week 15 — Presenting Your Work

## Decisions encoded in this plan (vs. design doc)

This plan departs from the Week 15 design doc on several points, based on the terminology and framing conventions established across Weeks 7–14 and cross-referenced against `guides/actual-content-map.md` and `guides/ai-arc.md`.

1. **"Peer Evaluation #2" → "End-of-Project Peer Evaluation"** throughout learner-facing content. Pairs with "Mid-Project Peer Evaluation" for the Week 8 instrument. Per the terminology convention, numbered peer-evaluation labels are replaced with evergreen names.
2. **Specific grade percentages replaced with assessment-component names.** The design doc cites 15% for Final Presentation, ~5% for Reflection #5 (as part of the 25% Reflection Portfolio), and 70% / 50% / 20% for the End-of-Project Peer Evaluation's contribution to the Professional Engagement component. Per the terminology convention, learner-facing content uses component names ("the Final Presentation carries significant weight in the final grade," "the Reflection Portfolio component," "the Professional Engagement component") without citing specific percentages. Weights can be accurate internally but should not appear as student-facing numbers.
3. **AI-as-Socratic-partner framing.** Design doc says "introduced Week 1, applied in every reflection." Per `guides/ai-arc.md`, Week 1 introduces AI policy and the defend-your-work standard, not the Socratic partner pattern specifically. Page 3 frames it as "the strategy you have been using since Reflection #1," consistent with Weeks 12 and 14.
4. **"Continue/Start/Adjust" framework reference in 15.08 needs verification.** The design doc lists this as a component of the End-of-Project Peer Evaluation instrument ("qualitative feedback (Continue/Start/Adjust)"). Week 14 flagged the same reference as possibly Week-8-specific; `guides/actual-content-map.md` describes the Week 8 Mid-Project Peer Evaluation as BARS-only without naming a Continue/Start/Adjust framework. Before drafting Page 4, confirm against actual Week 8 produced content and the published End-of-Project Peer Evaluation instructions. If Continue/Start/Adjust is not part of the instrument, remove the reference and describe qualitative feedback in whatever form the instrument actually uses.
5. **Evergreen employer-touchpoint terminology.** Proper event names (Kickoff, Direction Check, Near-Final Review, Final Check-In, Final Presentation) are used; numbered check-in labels are not. The Direction Check reference on Page 2 uses the event name, consistent with the actual-content-map convention.
6. **Presentation slide AI disclosure framing (design doc line 107).** The design doc says "Presentation slides should include an AI disclosure if AI was used in their creation." This is a new disclosure site that does not clearly exist in prior weeks' produced content or in the AI arc document. Flag for Rachel's review: confirm whether presentation-slide AI disclosure is an established expectation, and if so, where (on-slide, in speaker notes, in submission form). Page 2 includes the requirement but leaves the exact mechanism as `[TBD]` pending confirmation.
7. **Weekly team check-in rhythm framing.** 15.09 async coordination — if referenced at all in Page 1's suggested order — carries the convention from Weeks 10, 12, 13, 14. This is the final week of the rhythm; framing acknowledges the end, not a continuation.
8. **Course-close framing added to Page 5.** The design doc has no explicit "course close" activity. Page 5 (Articulating Your Experience) adds a brief course-close coda because the course's final page should acknowledge the ending — the career articulation skill is the forward-facing substance, but the closing frame gives students a place to land. The coda is lean by design; it does not invent a new activity.

All other design doc activities stand as written.

## Content Architecture

Week 15 is the course's closing week and its most submission-heavy: three graded submissions (Final Presentation, Reflection #5, End-of-Project Peer Evaluation), the final employer touchpoint, and the team's formal closing. Following the consolidation pattern established in Weeks 7, 9, and 11 for meeting-anchored pages, the Final Presentation arc (prep + presentation + closing debrief) folds into a single page rather than three — students have been through four prior employer touchpoints and do not need the Week-3-style before/during/after scaffolding. The two individual-submission finalization activities (Reflection #5 and End-of-Project Peer Evaluation) each get their own page because the submission instruments are substantive and require findability — students are finishing work begun in Week 14, and the pages house the prompts, finalization guidance, AI disclosure, and submission logistics in one place. The final module (15.03 Articulating Your Experience) combines with a brief course-close coda on Page 5, closing the course on a forward-facing skill rather than a summary. The week reduces to **five content pieces**: Week Overview → The Final Presentation → Reflection #5 Finalization → End-of-Project Peer Evaluation Finalization → Articulating Your Experience.

### 2. The Final Presentation
**Type:** Assignment prompt with embedded guidance
**Absorbs:** 15.02, 15.04, 15.05, 15.06

Single consolidated page covering the entire Final Presentation arc — day prep, final polish, the presentation itself, and the post-presentation debrief and team closing. Follows the Week 11 Near-Final Review consolidation pattern (one page, not three). The Final Presentation is longer, higher-stakes, and more formal than the Near-Final Review, but by Week 15 students have presented to the employer four times and have completed a full rehearsal in Week 14 (14.09). They do not need Kickoff-week scaffolding; they need a clear, well-organized page that houses the whole arc on presentation day. The team closing at the end is treated as a designed activity, not an afterthought, per Design Decision #4.

**Sub-elements:**

**What this presentation is**
- Summative assessment of oral presentation; one of only two formal student presentations in the course (the other was the Direction Check in Week 5); the culmination of the three-week presentation arc (conceptual in Week 12, operational in Week 14, delivery here) (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Rubric assessment: narrative structure, visual support, speaker coordination, and Q&A performance; graded against the Final Presentation rubric; significant weight in the final grade (AI-generated text, ~150 words). Do not cite the specific percentage; use "significant weight" or "the Final Presentation component of your grade."

**Presentation Day Prep** (absorbs 15.02; opening mindset section)
- Video: the mindset move for presentation day — calm confidence built on preparation, not cramming; what "ready" actually looks like on the day; naming presentation nerves as normal and redirectable (AI-generated video, ~1–2 min). Candidate title: *"Ready, not polished."* Framing video to scatter across this text-dense page.
- Handling nerves: what's useful (short walk, breath work, reviewing opening lines) and what's not (last-minute slide changes, re-reading the full deck, over-caffeinating) (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Final run-through: what's appropriate at this point — practicing the first 30 seconds, confirming transitions, re-reading speaker notes — and what's not — rebuilding slides, rewriting sections, adding new content (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Continuity with Week 14: the rehearsal was the real preparation; this is about being ready to deliver it (AI-generated text, ~100 words). Brief — reference, don't re-teach.

**Final Presentation Polish** (absorbs 15.04)
- What this session is *for*: targeted adjustments based on what the Week 14 rehearsal surfaced — not a full rehearsal; practicing opening and closing, confirming timing, reviewing the anticipated-questions list (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Teams that are overhauling slides at this point are doing something wrong. Naming the failure mode (12.02 territory: late-stage changes carry more risk than benefit at this point) (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- Using AI for final Q&A simulation: generate challenging questions from the presentation content, practice answers out loud — not write them down — as the last rehearsal step (AI-generated text, ~150 words). Extends the Week 14 Q&A strategy.

**The Presentation** (absorbs 15.05)
- Format: 60-min hold; 20–25 min presentation + 15 min Q&A + 10 min employer feedback + 5–10 min wrap/handoff/acknowledgments (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- What to capture in notes during the employer feedback window: what resonated, what questions they asked, what their reaction suggested about the work (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- The Q&A section: the defend-your-work moment. Answers must come from genuine understanding. The strategies from Week 14 Page 2 apply — naming the edge of what's known, using "here's how I'd find out" rather than guessing, accepting legitimate correction without collapsing the recommendation (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- AI disclosure on presentation materials: `[TBD per Rachel's review — confirm disclosure mechanism for slides]` — if confirmed, AI disclosure applies to slide creation and speaker notes if AI was used; follow the disclosure format used on prior graded submissions (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Post-Presentation Debrief and Team Closing** (absorbs 15.06)
- Two parts, sequenced deliberately: (1) capture employer feedback while fresh, (2) structured team closing (AI-generated text, ~120 words)
- The debrief: what resonated, what was challenged, what surprised the team. Add to the Change Log; this is the last entry (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- The team closing: what we accomplished together, what we learned from each other, individual acknowledgments of teammate contributions. This is the last time the team works together as a team — giving the experience a proper ending matters for closure and for feeding the next two submissions (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Light closing template (Template): *We did this together* (2–3 accomplishments) | *I learned this from each of you* (one specific thing per teammate) | *I want to acknowledge* (one recognition per teammate).
- How the closing feeds what's next: the individual acknowledgments surface behavioral evidence that strengthens the End-of-Project Peer Evaluation; the reflection on what the team accomplished surfaces material for Reflection #5's articulation of learning (AI-generated text, ~120 words)

**Time check:** 15.02 (15 min) + 15.04 (60 min) + 15.05 (60 min) + 15.06 (45 min) = 180 min → ~2,600 words text (~13 min reading) + 1 video (~2 min) + closing template (~3 min) + the presentation and closing time themselves (~105 min of actual meeting/activity time) + polish session (~60 min). Reading ≈ 15 min; the rest is the activities themselves. ✓

### 3. Reflection #5 — Finalize and Submit
**Type:** Reflection prompt (finalization page)
**Absorbs:** 15.07

Finalizes the reflection begun in Week 14 Page 3 (Transfer + Reflection #5). Self-contained — students should not need to flip back to Week 14 to find the prompt, AI guidance, or submission logistics. The page's distinctive job is framing the finalization move specifically: the presentation experience and team closing may have surfaced final insights worth incorporating before submission; students should draft the Articulate + Learn stages with the fullest experiential pool available rather than closing the reflection before the course's culminating moments occur.

**Sub-elements:**

**What you're finalizing**
- The prompt, restated: the single most important thing you learned about how you work, learn, or engage professionally across the full 15-week arc. Not about the project's subject matter — about you as a professional (AI-generated text, ~200 words). Full prompt, not just a reference to Week 14.
- Reflection #5 calibration: the highest depth expectation in the portfolio; precise description, probing examination, articulated learning specific enough to be actionable and broad enough to transfer. The distance between this reflection and Reflection #1 should be evident in every rubric dimension (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Rubric reminder: the same four dimensions as every reflection (Descriptive Specificity, Critical Examination, Personal Accountability, Articulation of Learning), each equally weighted (AI-generated text, ~100 words)

**Incorporating this week's experience**
- What the Final Presentation and team closing may surface: employer feedback on the team's work is one of the richest final sources of material; the team closing's individual acknowledgments are another. Both can sharpen the anchor experience identified in Week 14 — or, occasionally, reveal a better anchor (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- When to change the anchor vs. when to strengthen the one you chose: change it if a more significant moment emerges from the presentation or closing; strengthen it if the original anchor holds and the new material adds texture rather than replacing it. Do not change the anchor just because the most recent experience is the most vivid — recency bias is the documented failure mode (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- From Week 14 draft to final submission: the Describe + Examine stages should be largely in place; this session is for Articulate + Learn, which is where the articulated learning claim is made (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**AI guidance for finalization**
- AI-assisted drafting remains off-limits for Reflection #5. AI cannot supply the experience, the identification of what changed, or the articulation of transferable learning (AI-generated text, ~100 words)
- AI as Socratic partner for the final push: the strategy you have been using since Reflection #1. Example prompt for this stage: "Push back on this articulated learning. Is it transferable or is it project-specific dressed up? What would it look like if I applied it in a completely different professional context?" (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- AI disclosure required on submission (AI-generated text, ~50 words)

**Submission**
- Submitted via Riipen. Submission instructions: `[PLATFORM: TBD]`
- Final checklist before submit (AI-generated text, ~120 words):
  - All four DEAL stages written
  - Anchor experience is specific, not generic
  - Articulated learning is stated in words that would hold up in a professional conversation
  - AI disclosure statement is accurate
  - The version you're submitting is the version you meant to submit

**Time check:** 15.07 (90 min) → ~1,520 words text (~7–8 min reading) + ~80 min drafting/finalization. ✓

### 4. End-of-Project Peer Evaluation — Finalize and Submit
**Type:** Assignment prompt (finalization page)
**Absorbs:** 15.08

Finalizes the evaluation begun in Week 14 Page 4. Self-contained — page houses the instrument description, the finalization guidance, AI disclosure requirements, and submission logistics so students don't flip back to Week 14. Page's distinctive job: walking students through the four-part instrument at the level of detail needed to complete it well. This is the largest single time allocation of the week (150 min) and the most complex instrument in the course — specific behavioral evidence per teammate per dimension, contribution allocation with unequal distribution requirement, self-evaluation, and closure questions visible only to the instructor.

**Sub-elements:**

**What you're finalizing**
- The instrument, four parts — restated in full: (A) BARS ratings on four dimensions with behavioral evidence per rating, (B) contribution allocation (100 points divided among teammates, unequal distribution required), (C) self-evaluation on the same four dimensions, (D) closure questions visible to the instructor only (AI-generated text, ~250 words). Confirm part structure against published instructions before drafting.
- Full-arc evaluation: the prompt framing is explicitly "Considering the entire project from Week 1 through Week 15" — students evaluate the full arc, not just the final weeks. A teammate who started strong but disengaged in the Refinement phase is assessed on the full pattern, not the ending. A teammate who stepped up late is similarly assessed across the arc (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- What changed since Week 14: the rehearsal, the presentation, and the team closing all generated new behavioral evidence. Incorporate it — especially for dimensions where Week 14 flagged thin evidence (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Incorporating the final week's evidence**
- The rehearsal (Week 14, 14.09) as evidence source: how teammates handled feedback, whether they delivered on commitments between the outline and the deck, how they showed up under the pressure of first run-through (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- The presentation (15.05): who did what they said they'd do; how handoffs worked; who handled Q&A well and who deferred (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- The team closing (15.06): the individual acknowledgments moment is an evidence source — what teammates said about each other, and what they chose to acknowledge, are signals worth incorporating (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Completing each part well**
- Part A (BARS ratings with behavioral evidence): one specific behavioral example per dimension per teammate; prefer quoted or near-quoted examples over impressions; if evidence is genuinely thin on a dimension, name the thinness rather than padding (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Part B (contribution allocation): 100 points among teammates, unequal distribution required — the instrument does not let students rate everyone equally. Think of this as the "who carried more" question, not a personal judgment; the ratings should track the evidence from Part A (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Part C (self-evaluation): apply the same standard to yourself; the same dimensions, the same evidence expectation. Self-evaluation tends to distort in two directions — over-rating (defensiveness) and under-rating (modesty). The goal is accurate (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Part D (closure questions): these are instructor-only signals about team dynamics. Use them to surface anything the public-facing parts of the evaluation cannot capture — patterns, concerns, observations that the team should not have to negotiate but the instructor should know (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**AI guidance**
- AI is permitted to help organize observations into the four dimensions and flag thin evidence; it is not permitted to generate the observations, ratings, allocations, self-evaluation, or closure answers. The evaluation must reflect the student's genuine assessment (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- AI disclosure required on submission (AI-generated text, ~50 words)

**Submission**
- Submitted via Riipen (or the platform location used for Mid-Project Peer Evaluation). Submission instructions: `[PLATFORM: TBD]`
- Final checklist before submit (AI-generated text, ~150 words):
  - Each teammate has BARS ratings with specific behavioral evidence per dimension
  - Contribution allocation sums to 100 points and is unequal
  - Self-evaluation is complete and calibrated to the same standard as teammate evaluations
  - Closure questions are answered candidly
  - AI disclosure statement is accurate
  - The submission reflects full-arc observation, not just the final weeks

**Time check:** 15.08 (150 min) → ~2,150 words text (~10–11 min reading) + ~140 min evaluation completion (approximately 45–50 min per teammate at ~3 teammates + self-evaluation + closure questions). ✓

### 5. Articulating Your Experience
**Type:** Standalone module + course close
**Absorbs:** 15.03

The course's final page. Combines the Articulating Your Experience module (15.03) — the last instructional content of the course — with a brief course-close coda. The module teaches the forward-facing skill of translating the project experience into professional language for resumes, interviews, LinkedIn profiles, and networking conversations; the course close acknowledges the ending and points students toward post-course next steps. Positioned at the end of the week's sequence because career articulation is easier when students have finished reflecting on and evaluating the experience — the raw material has been processed, and the task is packaging.

**Sub-elements:**

**Why this matters** (opening frame)
- The professional relevance: every job application, interview, graduate school essay, and LinkedIn update from this point forward will ask students some version of "what have you done, and what can you do?" The Articulating Your Experience skill is what turns the project into a professional credential rather than a line on a transcript (AI-generated text, ~200 words)

**What employers want to hear** (absorbs 15.03)
- Video: the before/after transformation — "I helped with a project for a credit union" vs. "Led market research synthesis for a regional financial institution, translating customer data into a strategic recommendations deck that informed the client's membership strategy." What changes: specificity, ownership, outcome. Anchor the video on this concrete transformation (AI-generated video, ~1–2 min). Candidate title: *"From 'I helped' to 'I led.'"* Scatter candidate for a text-dense page.
- The three moves: specificity (name what you actually did, not the category it belongs to), ownership (name your role specifically — led, drafted, synthesized, presented), outcome (name what the work produced, not what the work was) (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Weak vs. strong resume bullet (Scenario card, ~80 words): one before/after pair showing the three moves applied.

**Articulating for different contexts**
- Resume bullets: short, outcome-first, one line. Lead with the verb; name the scope; name the result. Two or three per project is typical (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Interview answers: longer, conversational, structured around a specific moment. The STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) or a simpler equivalent; the goal is a story that makes your contribution concrete (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- LinkedIn and networking: somewhere between the two; narrative enough to read as a person's experience, specific enough to signal real skill. Name the client's sector (not the client, unless permitted); name what you and the team produced; name what you learned in a transferable form (AI-generated text, ~200 words)

**Using AI for professional self-presentation** (the final AI skill of the course)
- The new AI application: using AI to refine professional framing — strengthening resume bullets, practicing interview responses, identifying which accomplishments to lead with for a specific role or context (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Distinct from AI-assisted reflection (which is off-limits) because the task is packaging and communicating learning you have already done, not generating the learning itself. A student who uses AI to turn "I helped with a project for a credit union" into the stronger version is articulating what they actually did — not fabricating what they didn't (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Prompt strategies: "Here is a draft resume bullet. Strengthen it by making the scope, ownership, and outcome more specific — without fabricating." / "Here are three project accomplishments. Which one should I lead with for a role in [X]?" / "Interview question: tell me about a time you worked on a team that disagreed. Here's my draft answer. Where is it weak?" (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- The boundary: AI should not fabricate accomplishments, credentials, or outcomes. The defend-your-work standard applies — if a resume bullet names an outcome, the student must be able to speak to how that outcome was measured (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Course close** (forward-facing coda)
- Brief acknowledgment of the course arc: 15 weeks of building, reflecting on, presenting, and articulating professional work. The course ends here; the professional work continues (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- What comes after: forward pointers as appropriate — Riipen alumni community, post-course resources, LinkedIn recommendations from the employer if offered, how to reference this project in future applications. Specific pointers depend on platform; keep content flexible to what's actually available (AI-generated text, ~200 words). Flag: specific post-course resources depend on Riipen's platform offerings — coordinate before drafting.
- The professional arc this course served: the course has built competencies across five areas (Communication, Professionalism, Critical Thinking, Teamwork, Career & Self-Development); those competencies travel to the next professional context. The Articulating Your Experience skill is what makes them visible to the next employer or program (AI-generated text, ~180 words)

**Curated link candidate** — practitioner piece on translating project experience into resume/LinkedIn/interview language. Criteria: practitioner, not academic; stable URL; ~6–8 min read. Probably available in career-writing or early-career professional development material. (Curated link + framing)

**Time check:** 15.03 (20 min) → ~2,200 words text (~11 min reading) + 1 video (~2 min) + scenario card (~2 min) + curated link (~6–8 min). Reading + media ≈ 20–23 min; thinking time fills the rest. Course close coda does not add additional activity time — it is framing attached to the final module. ✓
