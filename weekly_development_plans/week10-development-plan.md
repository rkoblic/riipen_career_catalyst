# Week 10 — Connecting Theory and Practice
## Content Development Plan

---

## Decisions encoded in this plan (vs. design doc)

This plan departs from the Week 10 design doc on three points, based on a cross-check against `guides/actual-content-map.md`:

1. **Activity 10.04 (Introducing the Change Log) is cut.** The Change Log was introduced in Week 6 page 3 ("The Change Log: a component to start now") and has been referenced in Weeks 8 and 9. The design doc's premise that students "have been informally tracking feedback since Week 7 debriefs" is out of date — the concept is already live and is being treated in Week 9 content as an established running record. There is nothing to introduce here. The 15 minutes freed lands back in the week's recovery margin; the week drops from 505 min to 490 min, which is consistent with Design Decision #5 (Recovery by design).
2. **"Status Update to Employer" is cut from 10.10.** The design doc references it as a standing prompt that "continues" this week, but it was never introduced — Week 8 explicitly cut it per the Week 8 CHANGELOG ("if anywhere, it belongs earlier in the course"). It cannot "continue" in Week 10. 10.10 retains the Weekly Progress Check-In and the between-week async coordination but drops the Status Update bullet.
3. **"Development Check-In #2" / "Progress Review #2" terminology is replaced with evergreen phrasing throughout.** Following the convention established in Week 9 ("the check-in with your employer," "your last check-in," "the working-session check-in you just had"). Numbered labels like "Development Check-In #2" read as internal-spec references and break if sequence changes. No learner-facing Week 10 text uses a numbered check-in label.

All other design doc activities stand as written.

## Content Architecture

Week 10 is a **Pattern C (production week with no major anchor)** with a secondary Pattern E flavor — three conceptual modules (10.02, 10.03, 10.05) are genuinely standalone because they serve *future* work (Reflection #4 in Week 12, Reflection #5 in Week 15, and AI-as-quality-reviewer use from Week 11 onward), not a Week 10 deliverable. There is no employer touchpoint and no graded submission this week. Modules 10.02 and 10.03 are two halves of the same move (surface tacit learning → extract transferable principle), so they consolidate into one page rather than two. Module 10.05 is a distinct skill topic and gets its own page. The week reduces to **four content pieces**: Week Overview → What You're Actually Learning → AI as Quality Reviewer → What's Next. Project work (team sync, production block, internal review gate, async coordination) is no-content time.

### 2. What You're Actually Learning
**Type:** Standalone module (two-part)
**Absorbs:** 10.02, 10.03

The week's most distinctive content. Combines surfacing tacit learning (10.02) and extracting transferable principles (10.03) into a single page because they are sequential moves in one skill: first you notice what you've learned that wasn't on your list, then you abstract it into a form that travels. Splitting them across two pages would force learners to flip between halves of the same conceptual arc. This page is explicitly positioned as preparation for the Articulation of Learning dimension in the reflection rubric, which is assessed in Reflections #4 and #5.

**Sub-elements:**

**Why this matters** (opening frame)
- The gap most students notice in week 10: you can describe what you've produced but struggle to name what you've actually gotten better at (AI-generated text, ~200 words). Lead with the professional relevance — hiring conversations, performance reviews, and graduate school applications all ask candidates to articulate transferable skill, not project summaries. Open with the purpose, not the failure mode. Framing video candidate if text feels dense.

**Part 1 — What are you actually learning?**
- The tacit-learning gap: why ten weeks of project work produces more learning than most students can name, and why "we made a deliverable" isn't an answer to "what did you learn?" (AI-generated text, ~300 words)
- A noticing exercise: three prompts students can use to surface specific learning from specific situations (e.g., "what did you do this week that you couldn't have done in Week 1?", "what moment surprised you, and what did the surprise reveal?", "where did you change your mind about how something should work?") (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Video: anchoring the conceptual move — the difference between "I learned Excel" and "I learned how to translate ambiguous client requests into a research plan" (AI-generated video, ~1–2 min). Candidate title: *"What you learned isn't what you did"*

**Part 2 — Making your learning transferable**
- The move from specific to transferable: what makes a piece of learning travel to a new context, and why situation-specific learning ("I learned how our client runs meetings") without abstraction doesn't transfer (AI-generated text, ~300 words)
- The "strip the proper nouns" test: if your learning statement still reads useful after you remove the client name, project type, and sector, it's transferable (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- Weak vs. strong transferable-learning examples (Scenario card, ~80 words): show one pair — e.g., "I learned how to write the context analysis for [Client X]" (situation-specific) vs. "I learned how to scope ambiguous client needs into a research plan" (transferable).
- Connection to the reflection rubric: Articulation of Learning is one of the four equally weighted reflection dimensions; this module is what prepares students to score well on it in Reflections #4 and #5 (AI-generated text, ~150 words). Brief pointer back to the reflection portfolio framework in `/rubrics`.

**Curated link candidate:** a short practitioner piece on learning transfer or expertise articulation. Criteria: not academic, not about "soft skills" generically, practitioner-focused (e.g., career writing, design practice, professional development), stable URL, ~5–8 min read. See flags below.

**Time check:** 10.02 (20 min) + 10.03 (20 min) = 40 min total → ~1,400 words text (~7 min reading) + 1 video (~2 min) + scenario card (~2 min) + curated link (~5–8 min) + thinking time (~20 min). Distributes across both modules. ✓

### 3. AI as Quality Reviewer
**Type:** Standalone module
**Absorbs:** 10.05

The final AI skill-building module in the course. Introduces the quality reviewer role — AI as a fresh reader of work the team has already built, rather than as a generator or production partner. Framed as the capstone of the AI skill progression (see `guides/ai-arc.md`). Distinct from the production partner role introduced in Week 8: production partner helps while you're building; quality reviewer helps after you have something complete enough to evaluate. Students will apply this through Weeks 11–15, so this module teaches the prompts and the practice; the application happens independently.

**Sub-elements:**

**Why this matters** (opening frame)
- The shift from "AI helps me build" to "AI helps me evaluate": what quality review is *for* in professional work, and why you hand complete drafts to colleagues rather than sketches (AI-generated text, ~200 words). Open with what quality review does, not with what AI can't do.
- One-line capstone note: this is the last new AI skill the course introduces — from here, students apply the full toolkit (research prompting, Socratic partner, production partner, quality reviewer) independently through the remaining weeks (AI-generated text, ~40 words). Not a separate section — folded into the opening frame.

**When to use quality review — starting this week**
- Brief pointer that the 300-min production block and the internal review gate this week are the first place to apply the tool; it isn't future prep (AI-generated text, ~120 words). Connects the module to the production work happening the same week rather than letting it read as Weeks 11–15 prep only. Names a specific use: running the fresh-reader prompt on assembled sections before the internal review gate surfaces the same problems manually.

**What the quality reviewer role is**
- How quality review differs from production partner use (Week 8): input is a complete draft, not a section; the ask is evaluative, not generative; the output is a list of problems, not a rewrite (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Four things a quality reviewer prompt is good at: (1) reading for consistency across team-authored sections, (2) flagging logical gaps and unsupported claims, (3) identifying tone mismatches between sections, (4) surfacing where an unfamiliar reader would lose the thread (AI-generated text, ~250 words)

**Prompt strategies**
- Three prompt templates students can adapt (AI-generated text, ~250 words):
  - *Fresh reader prompt:* "Read this document as someone who has never heard of this project or client. Where do you get lost? Where do you have to re-read?"
  - *Consistency prompt:* "Does this read as one coherent piece, or like sections written by different people? Flag the seams — voice shifts, terminology drift, structural gaps — and point to specific paragraphs."
  - *Weakest-link prompt:* "What are the three weakest points in this argument? Be specific about why each is weak — is it evidence, reasoning, scope, or framing?"
- What to do with the output: treat AI's list as questions to investigate, not verdicts to act on; use your team's judgment to decide which flags are real (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Working with AI well in this role**
- Give the model explicit permission to say nothing — LLMs default to being helpful and will generate critique even when a section is solid. "If this section is working, say so and move on." (AI-generated text, ~100 words)
- Use more than one model if the stakes are high; different LLMs flag different things. (AI-generated text, ~80 words)

**Time check:** 10.05 (10 min) → ~1,190 words text (~6 min reading) + prompt templates (~2 min) + thinking time. Within budget; the capstone note and the same-week-use pointer are short add-ins. ✓

### 4. What's Next
**Type:** Transitional prompt
**Absorbs:** 10.09

Bridges to the Refinement phase. Week 11 opens with the Near-Final Review — the employer's second document review of the project, and the next employer touchpoint after your last check-in. **Sub-elements:**

**What's next: Refinement phase opens**
- How Refinement is different from Development: the work shifts from building new sections to sharpening what's there; the employer starts weighing in on quality, not direction (AI-generated text, ~200 words). Open with what the phase is *for*, not with what learners won't be doing.
- The Near-Final Review (next week): document review meeting with the employer; different shape from the question-driven working sessions of your last check-in (AI-generated text, ~120 words). Evergreen phrasing — no "Development Check-In #2" reference.

**Previewing Reflection #4** (forward prep, the main work of 10.09)
- The Reflection #4 prompt is linked here so students can read it now, two weeks before it's due (`URL_TBD` — Reflection #4 prompt location)
- Why read it early: the prompt asks students to examine the gap between the plan they made in Week 4 and the work they've actually produced — that gap is most visible while students are still in the production work, not after (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- What to notice between now and Week 12: three concrete noticing prompts students can carry into their production work (e.g., "where did our plan hold up?", "where did we have to change course, and what triggered the change?", "what did we include that wasn't in the plan, and why?") (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Previewing the Final Deliverables rubric**
- Final Deliverables rubric link (`URL_TBD` — rubric location)
- Why read it now: "near-final" in Week 11 is judged against the final rubric; knowing the standard before the Near-Final Review helps teams prioritize remaining work (AI-generated text, ~100 words). Pointer to `/rubrics` for the framework doc.

**How to prepare for Week 11**
- Short checklist (AI-generated text, ~180 words):
  - Read the Reflection #4 prompt
  - Scan the Final Deliverables rubric
  - Enter Week 11 with the internal review gate output (the list of what still needs to change) in hand
  - **Audit your Change Log before Near-Final.** The log has been running since Week 6; by now it should be a substantive record of employer feedback received and how the team addressed it. If entries are thin or inconsistent, use this week's internal review gate as a prompt to catch up. The Near-Final Review surfaces the Change Log as the paper trail of feedback incorporation, and the Final Deliverables rubric assesses Responsiveness to Feedback against it.
  - **AI disclosure** is required on the Near-Final Package submission next week. Teams should be tracking AI use across production so the disclosure is accurate rather than reconstructed from memory.
  - No "Status Update to Employer" item.

**Time check:** 10.09 (15 min) → ~900 words text (~4–5 min reading) + two external links (Reflection #4 prompt + rubric, ~8–10 min combined). ✓
