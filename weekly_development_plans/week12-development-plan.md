# Week 12 — From Feedback to Final

## Decisions encoded in this plan (vs. design doc)

This plan departs from the Week 12 design doc on several points, based on a cross-check against `guides/actual-content-map.md`, `guides/ai-arc.md`, and the terminology conventions established in Weeks 7–10.

1. **"Status Update to Employer" is cut from 12.09.** The design doc references it as a standing prompt that "continues from Week 7" and is "appropriate between reviews." It was never introduced. Week 8 explicitly cut it per the Week 8 CHANGELOG, and Week 10's plan confirmed it does not appear in produced content. It cannot "continue" in Week 12. 12.09 retains the Weekly Progress Check-In rhythm and async coordination but drops the Status Update bullet.
2. **"Change Log practice introduced in Week 10" is corrected.** The design doc (12.02) references the Change Log as introduced in Week 10. It was introduced in Week 6 page 3 ("The Change Log: a component to start now") and has been a running artifact since. Page 2 frames it as an established record the team has been maintaining for six weeks, not as a practice from two weeks ago.
3. **"Peer Evaluation #2 in Week 15" → "End-of-Project Peer Evaluation."** Per the terminology convention in `guides/actual-content-map.md`, numbered peer-evaluation labels ("#2") are replaced with evergreen names. Page 5 and any other references use "End-of-Project Peer Evaluation."
4. **Evergreen phrasing for employer touchpoints.** No learner-facing Week 12 content uses "Development Check-In #2" or "Progress Review #2" — references are to "your last check-in with the employer" or "the Near-Final Review" (which is a proper noun for a distinctive event, not a numbered check-in). The optional Week 13 touchpoint is referenced as "the optional Final Check-In" (also a proper event name, not a numbered label).
5. **AI-as-Socratic-partner framing tightened.** The design doc's AI section says the Socratic partner strategy was "introduced in Week 1, reinforced in Weeks 3, 6, and 9." Per `guides/ai-arc.md`, Week 1 introduces AI policy and the defend-your-work standard, not the Socratic partner pattern specifically. The Socratic partner role for reflection is a pattern that runs across reflection weeks (reinforced at every reflection). Page 4 frames it as "the reflection AI strategy you've been using since Reflection #1," not as a Week 1 formal introduction.
6. **Weekly team check-in rhythm framing.** Per `project_week1_weekly_checkins_followup.md` in project memory, the weekly team check-in should be framed as a rhythm established in Week 1, not as something that "continues from Week 7." Page 1's suggested-order language and 12.09's async reference phrase it as "the rhythm your team has been using," consistent with Week 10's language.

All other design doc activities stand as written.

## Content Architecture

Week 12 is a **Pattern D (multi-anchor)** week with no employer touchpoint. Three arcs run in parallel: Reflection #4 (graded individual), Final Deliverable finalization (leading to Week 13 submission, the largest graded deliverable of the course at 30%), and the new Presentation Outline arc (forward-looking; drafted this week, submitted Week 14). The modules serve three distinct purposes: 12.02 is a finalization skill (knowing when to stop), 12.03 scaffolds Reflection #4 and is absorbed into the reflection page, and 12.04 opens a new presentation-skills arc that continues into Weeks 14–15. Following the Week 10/11 pattern, 12.07 (Draft Presentation Outline) folds into the Presentation Fundamentals page as an "apply this now" section rather than taking its own page — it is project work, not a checkpoint. The week reduces to **five content pieces**: Week Overview → Final Iteration → Presentation Fundamentals → Reflection #4 → What's Next. Project work (team sync, production block, async coordination) is no-content time.

### 2. Final Iteration — Knowing When to Stop
**Type:** Standalone module
**Absorbs:** 12.02

The hardest professional judgment call in revision: deciding that the work is done. Most students have never been asked to make this call in academic work — they revise until the deadline. This module frames "knowing when to stop" as a teachable professional skill connected to the triage framework from Week 7 (peer review four lenses) and the Change Log as the paper trail of feedback decisions. Sized to sit immediately before the 180-minute production block so the skill is active while teams are applying Near-Final Review feedback.

**Sub-elements:**

**Why this matters** (opening frame)
- The professional version of the question: not "did I do enough?" but "is the remaining work worth more than the risk of breaking what works?" Open with what the skill is *for* — making deliberate submit-ready decisions under time pressure — not with what perfectionism gets wrong (AI-generated text, ~200 words).
- Video: the distinction between perfectionism and quality, illustrated with a concrete example of a late-stage overhaul that introduced more errors than it fixed (AI-generated video, ~1–2 min). Candidate title: *"The revision that made it worse."* This is a framing video — scatter candidate per Principle 7 given the week's text density.

**Triaging Near-Final Review feedback**
- Three categories for every feedback item: (1) requires revision, (2) acknowledge in the Change Log as a deliberate decision, (3) out of scope / document and move on (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- How to tell the difference: what makes a feedback item action-requiring versus acknowledgment-sufficient; the professional asymmetry where late changes carry more risk than mid-stage ones (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- The Change Log as defense, not just record: the log has been a running artifact since Week 6, and by Week 12 it is the document that makes "we decided not to change X" a professional response rather than an oversight. The Responsiveness to Feedback dimension on the Final Deliverables rubric (10%) is evaluated against this record (AI-generated text, ~200 words). No re-introduction of the Change Log — reference it as known.

**Why late-stage overhauls usually cost more than they add**
- The risk calculus of late revision: every change at this stage can destabilize something that already worked; the newer the change, the less time to catch its downstream effects (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- The "last 10%" distinction: polish, consistency, and error-catching are low-risk final-stage work; structural rewrites, re-scoping, and new content generation are high-risk and usually wrong at this stage (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Curated link** — practitioner-focused piece on knowing when a professional document is done, or on the professional editing cycle and the move from substantive to copy-level revision. Criteria: practitioner, not academic; stable URL; ~6–8 min read. (Curated link + framing)

**Time check:** 12.02 (15 min) → ~1,050 words text (~5 min reading) + video (~2 min) + curated link (~6–8 min). Reading + media ≈ 13–15 min; thinking time fills the rest. ✓

### 3. Presentation Fundamentals
**Type:** Standalone module (with embedded "apply this now" section)
**Absorbs:** 12.04, 12.07

The largest single module of the week (25 min) plus the drafting activity it feeds (45 min). Following the Week 10 pattern for AI as Quality Reviewer, the module content and the same-week application live on one page: teach the concepts at the top, draft the outline at the bottom. This is the first formal presentation-skills content in the course — prior employer meetings have been question-driven or document-review, so learners come in without any shared vocabulary for narrative, audience, or speaker coordination as presentation skills. The outline is a working draft this week; refinement and rehearsal happen in Week 14, and the checkpoint submission is in Week 14.

**Sub-elements:**

**Why this matters** (opening frame)
- What a presentation does that a document cannot: a document walks the reader through at their own pace; a presentation compresses the work into 20–30 minutes for a room full of people who cannot re-read. The professional challenge is choosing what to say, not what to include (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Video: anchoring the core move — the employer already knows the project details, so the presentation is not a report of activities but a narrative about what the team found and what it means for the client (AI-generated video, ~1–2 min). Candidate title: *"Not a report, a story."* Opening video for a text-heavy module.

**Knowing your audience**
- The employer's starting knowledge: they have been in the Kickoff, Direction Check, two check-ins, and the Near-Final Review. They do not need context; they need conclusions and implications (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- The hidden audience: classmates, instructor, and — sometimes — additional stakeholders from the employer's side. How audience mix changes emphasis (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Narrative arc: not a report, a story**
- Why "here's what we did" is the wrong organizing principle; why "here's what the client should now believe or do" is the right one (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- A simple three-part arc learners can adapt: the problem the project addressed → what the team found → what the client should do with it (AI-generated text, ~200 words)

**Visual clarity: slides support, they do not replace**
- What a slide is for: a visual reinforcement of what the speaker is saying, not a transcript of it. The test: if a slide works as an email attachment, it probably fails as a slide (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- What belongs on a slide vs. what belongs in speaker notes; the cost of slides that make the audience read instead of listen (AI-generated text, ~200 words)

**Speaker coordination: the presentation should feel unified**
- Why team presentations often feel like three separate presentations in a trench coat; how to prevent that with deliberate handoffs, consistent voice, and shared narrative (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Handoff patterns that work: explicit cueing, narrative threading, and the "one-sentence recap of what the previous speaker just said" technique (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Using AI to structure a presentation** (fresh AI skill for this week)
- The new AI application: using AI to help structure slide narratives and draft speaker notes from the team's research and findings. The key constraint — AI-generated presentation language tends toward generic polish ("Today we'll walk you through our findings") and has to be grounded in the team's specific work and voice (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Prompt strategy for structuring a narrative: "Here are our findings and recommendations. What is the story arc that connects them? What should we lead with and what should close?" (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- Prompt strategy for speaker notes: "Draft speaker notes for this slide based on the underlying finding. Keep it conversational, not scripted." (AI-generated text, ~100 words)
- The caution: AI has not been in the meetings, has not read the deliverable, and cannot make judgment calls about what matters most to *this* employer. It is a structuring tool, not a narrator (AI-generated text, ~100 words)

**Apply this now: draft your presentation outline** (absorbs 12.07)
- What the outline should include: key messages (what the employer needs to take away), narrative arc (how the presentation tells a story, not just reports activities), visual approach (what needs slides, what is better said than shown), and speaking assignments (who presents what, and how handoffs work) (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Outline template (Template): sections for Key Messages (3–5 bullets), Narrative Arc (problem → findings → recommendations, with one sentence per phase), Visual Approach (slides vs. said-only by section), Speaker Assignments (who presents what, handoff cues).
- Note that the outline is a working draft this week. Refinement and rehearsal happen in Week 14; the checkpoint submission is Week 14, not this week (AI-generated text, ~100 words)

**Time check:** 12.04 (25 min) + 12.07 (45 min) = 70 min → module content ~2,450 words (~12 min reading) + 1 video (~2 min) + ~8 min thinking ≈ 25 min for the module; outline template + drafting time ≈ 45 min for the application. ✓

### 4. Reflection #4
**Type:** Reflection prompt
**Absorbs:** 12.03, 12.08

Fourth DEAL reflection — framework is long-established, so module 12.03 (Approaching Reflection #4) is embedded here as scaffolding immediately before the prompt. The distinctive analytical move for this reflection is the gap between plan and reality: students examine what changed between the Context Analysis & Project Plan (Week 4) and the work they have actually produced. This is a different move than earlier reflections, which asked learners to examine specific moments or events. Page 2 of Week 10 ("What You're Actually Learning") prepared students for this move two weeks ago; this page re-connects learners to that preparation rather than re-teaching it.

**Sub-elements:**

**Approaching Reflection #4: the gap between plan and reality**
- Why the plan-vs-reality gap is the richest source of professional learning at this point in the course: it is where tacit learning becomes visible — things the team came to understand only by doing the work, which no one could have written into the Week 4 plan (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- The analytical move Reflection #4 asks for: connect specific project experiences to broader professional understanding. Not "I learned X in this project" but "I learned X, and here is how it changes how I would approach Y in the future." This connects to the Articulation of Learning rubric dimension (25% of the reflection grade) (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- How to strengthen the Examine stage when the subject is your own evolving understanding rather than a single event: three prompts to carry into drafting — where did our Week 4 plan prove accurate, where did it prove wrong, and what did we include that we did not plan? (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Weak vs. strong plan-vs-reality example (Scenario card, ~80 words): one pair showing a surface description of what changed ("we had to revise our scope because the employer changed their mind") vs. genuine examination ("our Week 4 plan assumed the employer had a stable set of priorities; what we found was that their priorities shifted as they saw our work — the change was evidence that the project was doing its job, not that our plan was wrong").

**Reflection #4 assignment**
- Focus area: a specific moment from Weeks 10–12 — the transition from Development to Refinement, the quality review process, the near-final production push, or the distance between early assumptions and current reality. Use the DEAL model with higher depth expectation in Articulation of Learning (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- AI guidance: the Socratic partner strategy you have been using since Reflection #1 applies — using AI to push back on surface observations and challenge generalizations, not to draft the reflection. Example prompt: "Push back on this interpretation of what changed. What else might explain the gap? What am I missing?" (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- AI-assisted drafting is off-limits for Reflection #4. AI cannot supply the experience, the examination, or the articulated learning (AI-generated text, ~100 words)
- AI disclosure required. Submission instructions: `[PLATFORM: TBD]`

**Time check:** 12.03 (10 min) + 12.08 (90 min) = 100 min → scaffolding content ~900 words text (~4–5 min reading) + scenario card (~2 min) + thinking time (~3 min) ≈ 10 min for the scaffolding; ~90 min drafting for the reflection itself. ✓

### 5. What's Next
**Type:** Transitional prompt
**Absorbs:** nothing (forward-looking)

Bridge to Week 13. Final Deliverables are due next week (30% of grade). The optional Final Check-In is available as the last employer touchpoint before final submission. Week 13 is the lightest production week of the Refinement phase — its job is to finalize and submit, not to generate new work. Frames what teams should enter Week 13 with (a deliverable package that is substantively complete after the 180-minute production block this week) and what Week 13 adds (final polish pass, submission logistics, optional touchpoint).

**Sub-elements:**

**What's next: finalize and submit**
- How Week 13 is different from Week 12: the work is done; the remaining moves are the final polish pass, the AI quality-review pass on the full assembled package, and submission (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- The optional Final Check-In: what it is *for* (targeted last questions, not substantive review), when to use it vs. skip it, and what to bring if you do (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**How to prepare for Week 13**
- Short checklist (AI-generated text, ~200 words):
  - Enter Week 13 with the deliverable at Near-Final Review quality, updated with all feedback items triaged in 12.05 and addressed in 12.06
  - Change Log current through Week 12 — every Near-Final Review feedback item documented with the team's decision and, where action was taken, where it shows up in the deliverable
  - Presentation outline drafted (the working document from 12.07) — Week 14 will refine and rehearse from this
  - Reflection #4 submitted (graded this week)
  - **AI disclosure** tracking current across production so the Final Deliverables disclosure next week is accurate, not reconstructed
  - No "Status Update to Employer" item.

**Looking ahead: Week 14**
- A brief forward-pointer that Week 14 is the presentation-refinement week: rehearse, adjust based on peer feedback, submit the outline checkpoint. The End-of-Project Peer Evaluation also opens in Week 14 (AI-generated text, ~120 words). Use "End-of-Project Peer Evaluation" not "Peer Evaluation #2."

**Time check:** Forward-looking bridge, no activity absorbs here → ~620 words text (~3 min reading). Short page consistent with other What's Next pages. ✓
