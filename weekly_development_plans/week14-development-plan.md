# Week 14 — Preparing to Present and Looking Ahead

## Decisions encoded in this plan (vs. design doc)

This plan departs from the Week 14 design doc on several points, based on the terminology and framing conventions established across Weeks 7–13 and cross-referenced against `guides/actual-content-map.md` and `guides/ai-arc.md`.

1. **"Peer Evaluation #2" → "End-of-Project Peer Evaluation"** throughout learner-facing content. Per the terminology convention, numbered peer-evaluation labels are replaced with evergreen names. Pairs with "Mid-Project Peer Evaluation" used for the Week 8 instrument.
2. **"Peer Eval #1" → "Mid-Project Peer Evaluation"** wherever it appears in learner-facing content (14.11 uses the numbered form).
3. **Specific grade percentages replaced with assessment-component names.** The design doc cites "70% of the overall peer evaluation score" and "20% of course grade" for Professional Engagement. Per the terminology convention in `guides/actual-content-map.md`, learner-facing content uses component names ("the Professional Engagement component of your final grade") rather than specific percentages. The weights can be accurate internally but should not appear as student-facing numbers in Week 14 copy.
4. **AI-as-Socratic-partner framing.** Design doc AI section says "introduced in Week 1, applied in every reflection since." Per `guides/ai-arc.md`, Week 1 introduces AI policy and the defend-your-work standard, not the Socratic partner pattern specifically. Page 3's reflection section frames Socratic-partner use as "the strategy you've been using since Reflection #1," consistent with Week 12's Page 4.
5. **Evergreen employer-touchpoint terminology carries through.** No learner-facing Week 14 content uses "Check-In #N" or "Progress Review #N" labels. Proper event names (Kickoff, Direction Check, Near-Final Review, Final Check-In, Final Presentation) are acceptable where they refer to the distinctive events themselves.
6. **"Continue/Start/Adjust framework" reference in 14.11 needs verification.** The design doc references this as Week 8 module content; `guides/actual-content-map.md` describes the Week 8 Mid-Project Peer Evaluation as using BARS (behaviorally anchored scales, four Teamwork subskill dimensions) without naming a Continue/Start/Adjust framework. Before drafting Page 4, confirm against actual Week 8 produced content — if Continue/Start/Adjust is not part of Week 8's content, remove the reference from Page 4 and rely on the BARS framework only.
7. **Weekly team check-in rhythm framing.** 14.12 async coordination references carry the convention from Weeks 10, 12, 13 — weekly check-ins are framed as "the rhythm your team has been using," not as a practice continuing from Week 7.
8. **Q&A module's "outsourced the thinking" framing is softened.** The design doc line — "the skill that distinguishes students who genuinely engaged with the work from those who outsourced the thinking" — is design-doc-internal framing. Page 2's learner-facing version names the defend-your-work standard positively rather than accusingly: the Q&A is the moment where the team's own understanding becomes visible, and preparation is about making that visibility an asset rather than a risk.

All other design doc activities stand as written.

## Content Architecture

Week 14 is the most structurally complex week since Week 3 — three parallel arcs running simultaneously. The presentation arc (14.02, 14.03, 14.05, 14.08, 14.09) finishes the two-week presentation-skills sequence opened in Week 12: planning and Q&A modules, outline finalization, deck build, and full rehearsal. The reflection arc (14.04, 14.06, 14.10) introduces the Transfer framework as the conceptual move Reflection #5 requires, then scaffolds and opens the drafting work — the reflection is not submitted until Week 15 but begins here. The peer evaluation arc (14.07, 14.11) similarly previews instructions and begins the noticing work for the End-of-Project Peer Evaluation, also submitted Week 15. This is a **multi-anchor week** (Pattern D variant) with one team checkpoint (Presentation Outline, 14.05 — completion-based) and two begun-but-not-submitted individual instruments. Following the Week 9/12 pattern for content consolidation, related modules fold together: 14.02 + 14.03 + 14.05 into the presentation-build page; 14.04 + 14.06 + 14.10 into the Transfer + Reflection #5 page; 14.07 + 14.11 into the peer evaluation page. The week reduces to **five content pieces**: Week Overview → Planning Your Presentation → Transfer + Reflection #5 → End-of-Project Peer Evaluation → What's Next.

### 2. Planning Your Presentation
**Type:** Standalone module + assignment prompt with embedded guidance
**Absorbs:** 14.02, 14.03, 14.05

Combines the two operational presentation modules (Planning + Q&A) with the Presentation Outline checkpoint submission. Positioned this way because 14.02 directly feeds 14.05 (outline finalization) and 14.03 directly feeds the rehearsal (14.09) and Week 15 Q&A. Distinct from Week 12's Presentation Fundamentals page, which was conceptual (audience, narrative, visual clarity, speaker coordination as ideas) — this page is operational (who does what, how you handle questions, how you finalize the outline). Week 12 is referenced as known; no re-teach of audience awareness or narrative arc.

**Sub-elements:**

**Why this matters** (opening frame)
- What makes a team presentation harder than an individual one: three or four people have to sound like one voice telling one story, not three separate reports stapled together. Open with what the operational skill is *for*, not with what team presentations get wrong (AI-generated text, ~180 words)

**Planning Your Presentation** (absorbs 14.02)
- Who presents what: how to assign sections based on who knows the content best, not who is most comfortable presenting; when equal time splits are the wrong call; when to assign openers and closers deliberately (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Handling team dynamics during the presentation: what to do if a teammate gets stuck, loses their place, or gets a question they can't answer; the handoff patterns that keep the presentation moving without making the stuck moment the memorable part (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Rehearsing effectively: what a rehearsal is *for* — timing, transitions, narrative coherence, Q&A anticipation — not memorization. The 90-min rehearsal block (14.09) is a full run-through plus debrief plus one targeted re-run of whatever broke. Guidance applies at the rehearsal itself, not as homework (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Continuity with Week 12: Presentation Fundamentals taught audience awareness, narrative structure, visual clarity, and speaker coordination as concepts. Planning applies those concepts as operational decisions; the outline finalization below is where those decisions become concrete (AI-generated text, ~120 words). Brief — don't re-teach, reference.

**Handling Q&A** (absorbs 14.03)
- Video: saying "I don't know but here's how I'd find out" — how the defend-your-work standard actually plays out in the Q&A, and why it is a stronger professional move than guessing or deflecting (AI-generated video, ~1–2 min). Candidate title: *"Saying I don't know well."* Scatter opportunity for this text-dense week; the Q&A skill is easier to model than to describe.
- What the Q&A is *for*: the moment where the team's own understanding becomes visible. Preparation is about making that visibility an asset — evidence that the team built the work themselves — rather than a risk (AI-generated text, ~200 words). Reframes the design doc's "outsourced the thinking" framing into a positive skill claim.
- Thinking on your feet without fabricating: the professional move is to describe what the team does know, name the edge of what's known, and say what would need to be true to answer the question confidently (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Handling pushback on recommendations: when a question is a challenge rather than a request for clarification; how to hold the team's position without being defensive, and how to accept a legitimate correction without collapsing the recommendation (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Using AI to anticipate questions: extending the stress-testing strategy from Week 7 — "What would a skeptical employer ask about these recommendations? What's the question we'd least like to get?" Practice answers out loud in the rehearsal, not just in writing (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Curated link candidate** — practitioner-focused piece on handling Q&A under pressure or managing difficult questions in professional presentations. Criteria: practitioner, not academic; stable URL; ~5–8 min read. (Curated link + framing)

**Finalize and Submit the Presentation Outline** (absorbs 14.05; checkpoint submission)
- What this checkpoint is (and isn't): the outline is a completion-based checkpoint, not a quality-graded deliverable. The point is evidence of deliberate planning before the deck build. A thin outline predicts a thin presentation (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- What the finalized outline should include: narrative structure (the story, not the sequence), role assignments (who presents what, handoff cues), key talking points per section, planned visual support (what needs slides, what's better said than shown) (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- How this differs from the Week 12 draft: the draft was working material; the finalized version incorporates any adjustments from planning and Q&A thinking and is submission-ready (AI-generated text, ~100 words)
- AI guidance: AI-assisted structuring is permitted (organizing the narrative arc, checking flow, identifying audience needs) — this is application of the strategy taught in Week 12 Page 3, not a new skill. AI disclosure required on the submission (AI-generated text, ~100 words)
- Submission instructions: `[PLATFORM: TBD]`

### 3. Transfer + Reflection #5
**Type:** Standalone module + reflection prompt
**Absorbs:** 14.04, 14.06, 14.10

The reflection arc for the week, combining the Transfer module (14.04) as conceptual frame, the Synthesis Reflection scaffolding (14.06) as practical writing guidance, and the Reflection #5 prompt and drafting activity (14.10). Follows the Week 9 Reflection #3 pattern of scaffolding + assignment on one page, but expanded because Reflection #5 is the most demanding reflection in the portfolio and requires the conceptual move that Transfer teaches. Reflection #5 is not submitted this week — it begins here and finalizes Week 15. Page is explicitly positioned as preparation for the highest-stakes articulation of learning the course asks students to do.

**Sub-elements:**

**Why this matters** (opening frame)
- The professional relevance: every hiring conversation, graduate school application, and performance review after this course will ask students some version of "what did you learn, and what can you do now?" The Transfer move — pulling transferable skill out of specific project experience — is what lets students answer those questions with something stronger than generic skill words (AI-generated text, ~200 words)

**Part 1 — Transfer: Taking It With You** (absorbs 14.04)
- Video: the shift from project-specific to career-specific — what "I learned how to scope ambiguous client requests" sounds like vs. "I learned about credit unions"; why the first travels to new work and the second does not (AI-generated video, ~1–2 min). Candidate title: *"What you can actually take with you."* Scatter candidate for a text-heavy reflection page.
- Project-specific vs. transferable: the two categories; why project-specific knowledge is valuable but only to the specific context that produced it, and why transferable principles are the output that the project produced for the student's own career (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Building on Week 10: Week 10 Page 2 taught the noticing move (surfacing tacit learning) and the "strip the proper nouns" test. This module extends that into articulation — how to state the transferable principle in words that would hold up in a job interview, not just a course reflection (AI-generated text, ~200 words). Reference Week 10; do not re-teach.
- A sharpening exercise: three prompts for articulating transfer — "what can you now do that you couldn't do 14 weeks ago?", "what pattern did you see across the project that you'd expect to see in future professional work?", "what would you tell a student starting this course so they'd know what to pay attention to?" (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Weak vs. strong transferable articulation example (Scenario card, ~80 words): one pair showing project-specific statement vs. transferable articulation, extending the Week 10 example into the career-articulation register.

**Part 2 — Approaching Reflection #5** (absorbs 14.06)
- How Reflection #5 differs from earlier reflections: earlier reflections examined specific moments or the gap between plan and reality; Reflection #5 asks students to look across the full 15-week arc and identify the single most important thing they learned about how they work, learn, or engage professionally. Not about the project's subject matter — about the student (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- Selecting an anchor experience: why the most recent experience is usually not the right anchor; how to scan across all fifteen weeks and identify the moment or pattern that most changed how the student sees their own professional work; the danger of reconstructing the arc from the last two weeks only (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- From "what I learned" to "what changed in how I work": the analytical move that raises a surface reflection to a professionally articulated one — not "I learned to communicate better" but "I learned that my default mode under time pressure is to over-explain, and I started catching that earlier in Week 9" (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Weak vs. strong full-arc reflection example (Scenario card, ~100 words): one pair showing a surface arc summary vs. a specific professional-change articulation.

**Part 3 — Reflection #5 assignment** (absorbs 14.10 — drafting begins this week, submits Week 15)
- Focus area: the single most important thing learned about how the student works, learns, or engages professionally across the full 15-week arc. Use the DEAL model; highest depth expectation in the portfolio on Articulation of Learning (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- AI guidance: the Socratic partner strategy you have been using since Reflection #1 applies at its most demanding. Example prompt: "Push back on this candidate insight — is it actually transferable or is it project-specific dressed up? What would it look like if I applied it somewhere new?" (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- AI-assisted drafting is off-limits for Reflection #5. AI cannot supply the experience, the identification of what changed, or the articulation of transferable learning. Disclosure required on submission in Week 15 (AI-generated text, ~100 words)
- Drafting guidance for this week: use the 60-min drafting block (14.10) to scan the full arc, land on a candidate anchor, and draft the Describe + Examine stages. Return to finalize Articulate and Learn next week. Do not try to finish the reflection in one sitting (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

### 4. End-of-Project Peer Evaluation
**Type:** Standalone module + assignment prompt
**Absorbs:** 14.07, 14.11

The peer evaluation arc for the week. The End-of-Project Peer Evaluation is not submitted this week — it begins here and finalizes Week 15. Follows the same consolidation pattern as Page 3 (scaffolding + assignment + begun drafting). The Mid-Project Peer Evaluation in Week 8 already taught the BARS instrument and the four Teamwork subskill dimensions — this page references that foundation and focuses on what's different about evaluating the full project arc. Page explicitly treats evidence-gathering as the core skill: the evaluation is as strong as the behavioral evidence that supports each rating.

**Sub-elements:**

**Why this matters** (opening frame)
- What the End-of-Project Peer Evaluation is for professionally: the practice of writing feedback that's actually useful to a teammate entering their next professional experience. Generic feedback ("good teammate") helps no one; specific behavioral feedback becomes something a colleague can act on. Open with the professional skill the evaluation builds (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- How this differs from the Mid-Project Peer Evaluation: same instrument (BARS, four Teamwork dimensions), expanded scope (full 15-week arc instead of Weeks 1–8), higher stakes (the End-of-Project ratings carry more weight in the Professional Engagement component of the final grade than the Mid-Project ratings) (AI-generated text, ~200 words). Do not cite specific percentages; use component names.

**Evaluating contributions that evolved over time**
- The complicating factor that didn't exist in Week 8: teammates' contribution patterns change across a 15-week project. A teammate who started slowly but stepped up significantly in the Refinement phase is not the same as a teammate who coasted throughout; a teammate who led early and faded is not the same as a teammate who showed up consistently. The evaluation needs to capture the arc, not just the endpoint (AI-generated text, ~250 words)
- How to think about evaluated contribution across time: specific behavioral examples from different phases of the project (early, middle, late), weighted by what the phase demanded of the team (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- What counts as behavioral evidence: observable actions and their effects on the team's work, not impressions or personal affinity. The Mid-Project evaluation's calibration examples apply — if the examples on file from Week 8 still hold as a reference, lean on them; if not, the BARS scale descriptors themselves are the reference (AI-generated text, ~200 words)

**Using AI to organize observations** (extending Week 8 guidance)
- AI is permitted to help structure observations — taking a set of behavioral notes across the 15 weeks and organizing them by BARS dimension, for example — but not to generate the observations themselves or assign ratings. The evaluation must reflect the student's own assessment (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Example prompt for organizational support: "Here are my notes on [teammate] from across the project. Help me sort these into the four Teamwork dimensions. Flag dimensions where I have thin evidence so I can think more before rating." (AI-generated text, ~100 words)

**Begin the evaluation this week** (absorbs 14.11 — drafting begins this week, submits Week 15)
- Start with the instructions review (10 min): the Mid-Project evaluation module content from Week 8 is the same instrument. Revisit the BARS dimensions and the calibration examples before beginning (AI-generated text, ~100 words). *If the "Continue/Start/Adjust framework" is part of Week 8 content, reference it here; otherwise omit this reference entirely per the Week 8 content check in the flags section.*
- Then gather evidence (~25 min): scan across the full project arc for specific behavioral examples per teammate; note where evidence is thin (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- End the session (~5 min) with a list of what additional noticing to do during the final week — especially during the rehearsal and Final Presentation, which are the last sources of behavioral evidence (AI-generated text, ~100 words)
- Submission: Week 15 — `[PLATFORM: TBD]`

**Time check:** 14.07 (15 min) + 14.11 (40 min) = 55 min → ~1,500 words text (~7–8 min reading) + thinking time (~8 min) ≈ 15 min for scaffolding; ~40 min for instructions review + evidence gathering. ✓

### 5. What's Next
**Type:** Transitional prompt
**Absorbs:** nothing (forward-looking)

Bridge to Week 15 — the final week of the course. Frames Week 15 as the convergence point: the Final Presentation (the live employer meeting with defend-your-work stakes), Reflection #5 submission (the final DEAL reflection), and End-of-Project Peer Evaluation submission (the summative evaluation closing the feedback loop). Also previews the course wrap activities — whatever the platform uses for course closure — so students have a complete picture of what the last week looks like. This is the last What's Next page of the course.

**Sub-elements:**

**What's next: Week 15**
- Week 15 is the course's convergence week: Final Presentation to the employer, Reflection #5 submission, End-of-Project Peer Evaluation submission, and course wrap (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- The Final Presentation is the course's single live Q&A moment. Defend-your-work is tested in real time, not in writing. Preparation this week (rehearsal, Q&A thinking) is the preparation for that moment (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**How to enter Week 15**
- Short checklist (AI-generated text, ~200 words):
  - Presentation Outline submitted (this week)
  - Deck built, rehearsal completed, peer feedback from the rehearsal incorporated
  - Reflection #5 in draft form — anchor experience identified, Describe + Examine drafted; Articulate + Learn waiting for final-week thinking time
  - End-of-Project Peer Evaluation begun — BARS dimensions reviewed, behavioral evidence gathered, thin spots flagged for final-week noticing
  - Logistics confirmed with employer (format, timing, attendees)
  - Any outstanding Change Log items documented

**Closing the preparation phase**
- Brief framing: Week 14 is where the work becomes ready for the final forms it takes. Week 15 is where those forms are delivered. Nothing left to build — everything left to present, articulate, and close (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Time check:** Forward-looking bridge, no activity absorbs here → ~700 words text (~3–4 min reading). Consistent with other What's Next pages. ✓
