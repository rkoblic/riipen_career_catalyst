# Week 13 — Finalizing Deliverables
## Content Development Plan

---

## Decisions encoded in this plan (vs. design doc)

This plan departs from the Week 13 design doc on several points, based on the terminology and framing conventions established across Weeks 7–12.

1. **"Peer Evaluation #2" → "End-of-Project Peer Evaluation"** throughout learner-facing content. Per the terminology convention in `guides/actual-content-map.md`, numbered peer-evaluation labels are replaced with evergreen names. This affects Page 4 (What's Next) and any reference in Page 1's suggested-order language or 13.10 async framing.
2. **"Progress Reviews #1–3 and the Near-Final Review" → evergreen phrasing.** The design doc's design-decision section uses numbered check-in labels that, if carried into learner-facing content, would break the convention. Use "the check-ins and reviews you've had across the project" or list proper event names (Kickoff, Direction Check, Near-Final Review, Final Check-In, Final Presentation) without numbered labels for the working-meeting check-ins.
3. **Weekly team check-in rhythm framing.** Per `project_week1_weekly_checkins_followup.md` in project memory, the weekly team check-in is a rhythm established in Week 1, not something that "continues from Week 7." 13.10 reference on Page 1 and What's Next should phrase it as "the rhythm your team has been using," consistent with Weeks 10 and 12.
4. **Internal review gate: fourth and final instance, not re-introduced.** Per `guides/actual-content-map.md` and the Week 9 plan, the gate was introduced on Week 9 page 3 (The Draft Deliverable). Page 2 references the gate as an established practice — no re-teach, no re-definition — and uses its final appearance to close the loop on the protocol rather than extend it.
5. **Change Log: treated as a thirteen-week running artifact, not a recent practice.** Introduced Week 6 page 3; referenced every week since. Page 2's checklist and submission guidance frame the Change Log as the paper trail the team has been keeping all along, not as something to assemble now. Teams that reach Week 13 without a substantive Change Log have a larger problem that Week 13 content cannot solve.
6. **AI policy framing on Page 2.** The design doc's AI section closes with the observation that AI-polished content the team cannot explain will surface in the Week 15 Final Presentation. This is the defend-your-work standard in its culminating form — Page 2 names it explicitly as the reason AI disclosure matters on this submission, not as a generic reminder.

All other design doc activities stand as written.

---

## Content Architecture

Week 13 is a **Pattern B (single anchor deliverable, production-centered)** week with a light optional-touchpoint wrinkle. The Final Deliverables submission is the week's anchor — 30% of the course grade and the highest-weighted submission in the course. Module content is deliberately minimal (20 min total: 13.02 + 13.03), reflecting the design principle that students at this stage need time, not instruction. The two modules fold together into the submission page: 13.03 (The Home Stretch) as opening mindset frame, 13.02 (Final Deliverable Checklist) as the pre-submission tool, 13.08 as the submission action itself. The optional Final Check-In (13.05) gets its own page because the hold/cancel decision and the async presentation-logistics communication are distinct tasks that need clear guidance. The week reduces to **four content pieces**: Week Overview → Final Deliverables Submission → Final Check-In (Optional) → What's Next. Project work (team sync, production block, internal review gate, async coordination) is no-content time.

### 2. Final Deliverables Submission
**Type:** Assignment prompt with embedded guidance
**Absorbs:** 13.02, 13.03, 13.08

The week's central page. Combines the Home Stretch mindset frame (13.03), the Final Deliverable Checklist (13.02), and the submission action itself (13.08) into one required checkpoint page — the model follows Week 11's Near-Final Package page but expands the AI and rubric guidance because this is a graded submission, not a completion-based checkpoint. The mindset content opens the page because the impulse to overhaul at the last minute is the named failure mode, and naming it at the top reframes the rest of the page as a structured route to submission rather than a late opportunity to add more.

**Sub-elements:**

**The Home Stretch** (opening mindset frame; absorbs 13.03)
- Video: finishing strong, trusting the work, and why late-stage overhauls usually cost more than they add (AI-generated video, ~1–2 min). Candidate title: *"Trust your process."* Opens the page with permission to stop adding and start submitting — a framing video is the right medium for mindset content that would read as preachy in text.
- Why this mindset module exists at all: the pull to make substantive changes the night before submission is predictable and professional workplaces have the same pattern; the module is less about learning something new than about giving teams language to resist the pull (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- The continuity with 12.02: knowing when to stop is the same skill you practiced last week, now at the final stage; the Change Log is still the defense when you decide not to change something (AI-generated text, ~100 words)

**What this submission is**
- What the Final Deliverables package includes: the deliverable components specified in the project brief, the Change Log, and the AI disclosure statement (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- What the rubric assesses: five dimensions with weights (Deliverable Quality 25%, Analytical Rigor 25%, Actionability 25%, Communication 15%, Responsiveness to Feedback 10%); the package is evaluated, not just the deliverable content — the Change Log and disclosure are part of what's graded (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- What 30% of the course grade means here: this is the single highest-weighted submission; it carries more than any individual reflection, any peer evaluation, and any other checkpoint in the course (AI-generated text, ~100 words)

**Final Deliverable Checklist** (absorbs 13.02)
- Pre-submission checklist students run through before hitting submit (Checklist, ~8 items):
  - All deliverable components specified in the project brief are included
  - Formatting is consistent across sections (fonts, headings, spacing, visual style)
  - Terminology and voice read as one document, not separately authored pieces
  - Errors — typos, broken references, orphaned placeholders — caught on a final read
  - Every rubric dimension has visible evidence in the package (not left to the evaluator to infer)
  - Change Log is complete: every substantive feedback item from employer touchpoints and draft reviews is documented with the team's response or rationale
  - AI disclosure statement is accurate (not reconstructed from memory)
  - The submitted version is the version the team agreed is final (no accidental old drafts)
- How to use the checklist: run through it in the internal review gate (13.07), not while assembling (AI-generated text, ~120 words)
- Connection to the self-review protocol from 11.03: scaled down — this is a final-pass checklist, not a deep review. The deep review was two weeks ago (AI-generated text, ~100 words)

**About the Change Log**
- Reference, not re-introduction: the Change Log has been a running artifact since Week 6; by Week 13 it should be a substantive record of every piece of employer and peer feedback the team received and how the team responded (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- Why it matters for the rubric: the Responsiveness to Feedback dimension (10%) is assessed through the Change Log. Without it, the dimension scores as "Not Submitted" — that's 10% of the 30% Final Deliverables grade gone (AI-generated text, ~120 words)
- What a complete Change Log looks like at this stage: feedback item → team's response (action taken, deliberate decision not to act, or deferred to future scope) → where the change appears in the package, if applicable (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**AI guidance for this submission**
- What AI is for at this stage: checking, not creating. Formatting consistency, coherence across sections, prose tightening, error-catching. The Change Log completeness check is a particularly good use (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- The defend-your-work standard at its culminating form: AI-polished content the team cannot explain will surface in the Week 15 Final Presentation, where the employer asks live questions. Teams submitting AI-generated analysis or recommendations they haven't verified are creating a problem for themselves two weeks from now, not solving one now (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- AI disclosure: required on submission. Use the disclosure the team has been tracking across production rather than reconstructing from memory (AI-generated text, ~80 words)

**Submission logistics** (absorbs 13.08)
- Final formatting checks, upload, team confirmation that the submitted version is correct (AI-generated text, ~100 words)
- Submission instructions: `[PLATFORM: TBD]`

**Curated link candidate** — practitioner-focused piece on the final edit / the difference between polish and overhaul / hitting submit on professional work. Criteria: practitioner, not academic; stable URL; ~5–8 min read. Optional; if no adequate source exists, cut. (Curated link + framing)

### 3. Final Check-In (Optional)
**Type:** Assignment prompt with embedded guidance
**Absorbs:** 13.05

The Final Check-In is structurally different from every other employer touchpoint in the course: it is optional by design. The page's first job is framing the hold/cancel decision as a professional judgment call — not as a default meeting with an escape hatch. The page also owns the async presentation-logistics communication, which happens regardless of whether the meeting is held. Keeping both on the same page surfaces that the employer communication is the non-negotiable part; the meeting is the negotiable part.

**Sub-elements:**

**What this touchpoint is (and isn't)**
- The Final Check-In is a 60-minute hold available for teams with substantive remaining questions about deliverable quality or completeness; it is not a default meeting teams are expected to hold (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Opening frame: cancelling a meeting you don't need is not laziness — it is respect for the employer's time and a professional judgment skill the course has been building toward. Open with what the decision is *for* (AI-generated text, ~150 words). Frame video candidate if the text feels thin; probably not needed given the week's lighter overall content mix.

**How to make the hold/cancel decision**
- The "substantive" test: a substantive question is one where the answer would change the deliverable; a reassurance question is one where the answer would make the team feel better but the deliverable would ship the same either way. Hold for the first; cancel for the second (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- What your Change Log and internal review gate tell you about whether you have substantive questions: if the gate flagged gaps that need employer input, you have questions; if the gate confirmed the work is ready, you don't (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- When to cancel even if you have questions: if the question can be resolved by the team without the employer, resolve it internally; the employer's time is a finite resource (AI-generated text, ~100 words)

**If the Final Check-In is held**
- Suggested time breakdown for 30 minutes of a 60-minute hold: brief status update (~5 min) → remaining questions about deliverable quality or completeness (~15–20 min) → confirm presentation logistics for Week 15 (~5 min) (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- How to structure the conversation: lead with where you are (submission-ready except for a handful of targeted items), ask the questions you prepared, close with logistics (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- Using AI to quickly triage any last-minute feedback from this meeting: extending the Week 12 framework — what requires action before submission, what becomes a Change Log acknowledgment, what is genuinely out of scope (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Communicating with the employer about Week 15 presentation logistics**
- Non-negotiable regardless of whether the Final Check-In is held; the Week 14 presentation work cannot proceed without format, timing, and attendees confirmed (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- What to confirm: presentation format (live, recorded, hybrid), presentation timing (date, time, duration), attendees (who from the employer will be present, any additional stakeholders) (AI-generated text, ~120 words)
- Tone: administrative and forward-looking, not feedback-seeking; if the meeting was cancelled, the communication can briefly confirm the team is on track so the cancellation reads as initiative rather than absence (AI-generated text, ~150 words)
- Short template: subject line + 3–4 sentence body covering format / timing / attendees / next action (Template)

**After the check-in (if held)**
- Debrief immediately, update the Change Log, apply the triage framework from 12.02 to any new feedback before the production block (13.06) closes out (AI-generated text, ~120 words)

**Time check:** 13.05 (60 min hold) → ~1,440 words text (~7 min reading) + template (~3 min) + meeting time if held (~30 min actual of the 60-min hold). If cancelled, the ~30 min recovered folds into the production block. ✓

### 4. What's Next
**Type:** Transitional prompt
**Absorbs:** 13.09

Bridge to Weeks 14–15. Absorbs the forward prep activity (13.09) — previewing both the Reflection #5 prompt and the End-of-Project Peer Evaluation instructions. This page is heavier than a typical What's Next because it's the last transition page before the course's closing arc: Week 14 is presentation refinement and forward prep for the final reflection and peer evaluation; Week 15 is the Final Presentation and the course wrap. Framing here sets up the noticing students need to do before the final week so they don't arrive at Week 15 trying to reconstruct fifteen weeks of experience under deadline pressure.

**Sub-elements:**

**What's next: Weeks 14 and 15**
- Week 14 is the presentation-refinement week: teams rehearse, adjust based on peer feedback, and submit the Presentation Outline checkpoint. The End-of-Project Peer Evaluation also opens in Week 14. Reflection #5 drafting begins (AI-generated text, ~200 words). Use evergreen terminology — "End-of-Project Peer Evaluation," not "Peer Evaluation #2."
- Week 15 is the Final Presentation and the course wrap: the presentation to the employer (the single live Q&A with defend-your-work stakes), Reflection #5 submission (the final DEAL reflection), and End-of-Project Peer Evaluation submission (AI-generated text, ~200 words)

**Previewing Reflection #5** (forward prep, part of 13.09)
- The Reflection #5 prompt is linked here so students can read it now, two weeks before it's due (`URL_TBD` — Reflection #5 prompt location)
- Why read it early: Reflection #5 asks students to step back over the full course arc and examine the most significant shift in their understanding or capability. That is easier to write if students have been noticing across the last two weeks of the project, not trying to reconstruct fifteen weeks from memory (AI-generated text, ~180 words)
- What to notice between now and Week 15: three concrete prompts students can carry — what can you now do that you couldn't do in Week 1? where did you change your mind about how professional work gets done? what moment across the project would you tell someone else about if they asked what this course was like? (AI-generated text, ~180 words)

**Previewing the End-of-Project Peer Evaluation** (forward prep, part of 13.09)
- The End-of-Project Peer Evaluation instructions are linked here (`URL_TBD` — instructions location)
- Framework reference: same BARS (behaviorally anchored scales) framework used for the Mid-Project Peer Evaluation in Week 8; same four Teamwork subskill dimensions. The evaluation is summative rather than formative — the ratings contribute to the final grade, and the qualitative feedback closes the peer feedback loop (AI-generated text, ~200 words)
- Why preview now: peer evaluation quality depends on behavioral evidence about teammates, and that evidence is easier to gather when students know what they'll be asked about. Use the remaining two weeks to notice specific moments, not to reconstruct impressions (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**How to prepare for Week 14**
- Short checklist (AI-generated text, ~180 words):
  - Presentation outline from 12.07 is in hand — Week 14 refines and rehearses from it
  - Final Deliverables submitted (this week)
  - Change Log current — it is referenced in the Final Presentation's defend-your-work moment
  - Reflection #5 prompt scanned; begin noticing
  - End-of-Project Peer Evaluation instructions scanned; begin noticing teammate contributions and patterns
  - Presentation logistics confirmed with the employer (format, timing, attendees)

**Closing the Refinement phase**
- Brief framing: Week 13 closes the Refinement phase; Week 14 opens the Presentation phase. The shift is from finishing work to translating it — from what the team made to how the team tells the story of what the team made (AI-generated text, ~150 words)

**Time check:** 13.09 (15 min) → ~1,260 words text (~6 min reading) + two external links (Reflection #5 prompt + End-of-Project Peer Evaluation instructions, ~8–10 min combined scanning) ≈ 15 min. ✓
